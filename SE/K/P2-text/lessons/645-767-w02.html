Stakeholder evidence and current-state analysis

Companion chapter: EN.645.767 · Week 02 · Phase 2 edition 1.0 (2026-10-30)
Original study guide: System Conceptual Design — consult Week 2 for the complete assignment, original source readings and mastery rubric.
Concepts: stakeholder analysis, interview bias, current-state mapping, evidence triangulation
Case: Fictional Autonomous Campus Shuttle, dispatch and transport service; all figures are illustrative, not deployment guidance.

The engineering question

How do you translate competing stakeholder stories into defensible evidence?

Conceptual tutorial

Stakeholders hold different slices of the system

Riders observe waiting and comfort; drivers observe handoffs and route obstructions; facilities staff observe curb access; safety officers own acceptance boundaries; finance sees lifecycle affordability. None is the entire truth. Build a stakeholder map identifying influence, affected outcome, decision rights, and missing voices. Use observation, records, and structured interviews rather than letting one articulate sponsor define the whole problem.

Distinguish what people report, what the process actually does, and what available data can support. A process map with arrival, boarding, transit, missed pickup and exception paths often exposes hidden delays. Triangulate statements: compare waiting-time interview responses with timestamped dispatch data and on-site queue observation. Identify measurement bias: people who abandon a service may be absent from satisfaction surveys.

Convert findings into explicit uncertainties

For each concern write a claim, evidence source, alternative explanation, uncertainty and next investigation. Missing observations are not zero demand. Represent service variation by time, user group, weather and route rather than one average. Seek disconfirming evidence: interview riders who do not use the service and inspect days with abnormal demand. Ethical research requires privacy-conscious collection and appropriate consent for sensitive observations.

Worked example: follow the reasoning

Three observations, one concern. A student says "I always wait 20 minutes." The app log reports 9 minutes median for completed pickups, and an observer records five riders who gave up before boarding. These measures have different denominators. Define waiting time starting at request submission, include abandoned requests as censored or failed-service records, and report the fraction of requests meeting a defined threshold alongside the completed-trip median.

Retrieval questions — attempt these first

  1. Why might completed-trip logs understate rider dissatisfaction?
  2. Name two independent ways to investigate a disputed waiting-time claim.
  3. What belongs in a current-state process map?

<details><summary><strong>Reveal explained answers</strong></summary>

  1. Why might completed-trip logs understate rider dissatisfaction? People who cancel or abandon are missing from completed-trip statistics, creating selection bias.
  2. Name two independent ways to investigate a disputed waiting-time claim. Compare instrumented timestamps with direct observations or sampled rider reports; examine definitions and missing records.
  3. What belongs in a current-state process map? Normal stages, decision points, roles, handoffs, queues, exceptions, and observable measures.

</details>

Critical misconception

Watch for: An interview count is not a representative demand estimate without a sampling frame and definitions.
