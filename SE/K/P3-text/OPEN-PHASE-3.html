Integrated fictional campus shuttle case: from model to decision

The integrated case carries a consistent analysis question through six courses: which dispatch strategy and fleet configuration can meet accessible-service expectations at acceptable lifecycle cost under credible uncertainty?

What each course adds

  1. Applied MBSE analytics: controlled semantics, functions, interfaces, traceability and a parametric capacity estimate.
  2. Metrics and statistics: distributions, sample validity, dependence, experiments, requirement margins and evidence limits.
  3. M&S foundations: a reproducible discrete-event simulator and independent known-answer checks.
  4. Advanced M&S: dynamic power/thermal coupling, time synchronization and numerical convergence.
  5. Systems dynamics: demand and fleet investment feedback over months rather than minutes.
  6. Decision science: feasible alternatives, Pareto frontier, stakeholder values, robustness and whether another pilot has decision value.

Deliberate stress case

A baseline simulator predicts 11.4 min p95 pickup wait against a 12 min illustrative limit. Later you discover the training dataset omitted peak rain; charging parameters came from an earlier battery configuration; and the measure excluded accessibility trips. Do not mark the requirement verified. Update data and model baselines, rerun scenario and sensitivity analyses, and require appropriately independent test evidence before a qualification claim.

Suggested capstone dossier

Use the supplied CSV templates for a controlled scenario manifest, parameter register, measurement contracts, model credibility claims, verification fixtures, experiment design, selected alternatives, uncertainties and decision conditions. At every stage preserve the difference between modeled predictions and observed evidence.

Phase 3 knowledge threads (cross-course)

The six courses build one analysis decision chain, not six disconnected software demonstrations.

  1. Scenarios → stochastic inputs → risk: MBSE analytics → distribution modeling → simulation input modeling → uncertainty and dependence.
  2. Functional architecture → event logic → evidence: MBSE behaviors → DES → verification and credibility.
  3. Parameter equations → physics → numerical credibility: parametric MBSE → ODE derivation → numerical solver tutorial.
  4. Experiments → response surfaces → optimal design: DOE → surrogates → model-selection and error.
  5. Feedback → operations → investment policy: stocks and flows → fleet sustainment → value of information.
  6. Trade space → preference → uncertainty: model-linked trade study → Pareto analysis → decision gates.
  7. Baseline → model credibility → governing action: MBSE baseline → simulation credibility → conditional design recommendation.

645-632 · Week 1

Receive the baseline, define intended use, and tailor the MBSE method and modeling plan

Course: Applied Analytics for Model-Based Systems Engineering · Week: 01 of 12 · Phase 3 companion: edition 1.0
Study guide: Open the original course guide and jump to Week 1 for prescribed readings, full assignment and mastery criteria.
Cross-course case: Fictional Autonomous Campus Shuttle. Figures are synthetic and illustrate reasoning; they do not validate real transport operations.

The idea in context

A model has an intended decision, not just an intended audience. Begin by specifying the decision, authorized baseline, model boundary, permitted notation, ownership and change control. Separate requirements that the model must represent from questions the model must answer. A modeling plan should define viewpoints, element identifiers, naming rules, validation queries, evidence links and exit criteria. Completeness is meaningful only relative to those declared questions; a dense diagram can still fail to expose a critical missing interface.

Worked reasoning: apply it to the shuttle case

The campus shuttle model will support a fleet-sizing decision. Its scope includes demand, dispatch logic, vehicle capacity and charging constraints, but not detailed tire dynamics. A decision query asks whether all proposed fleets meet a 12-minute service threshold in each approved scenario. The excluded dynamics need an assumption and escalation condition.

Assumption to challenge: Confusing a tool-created diagram with an authoritative, versioned system model. Record what observation or test would expose this failure before using the result in a decision.

645-632 · Week 2

Analyze stakeholders, mission, scenarios, outcomes, and measures in the authoritative model

Course: Applied Analytics for Model-Based Systems Engineering · Week: 02 of 12 · Phase 3 companion: edition 1.0
Study guide: Open the original course guide and jump to Week 2 for prescribed readings, full assignment and mastery criteria.
Cross-course case: Fictional Autonomous Campus Shuttle. Figures are synthetic and illustrate reasoning; they do not validate real transport operations.

The idea in context

A stakeholder statement is not automatically a system requirement. Translate mission outcomes into operational scenarios and measurable effectiveness before allocating behavior. Capture nominal, degraded and exceptional operations with actors, triggers, outcomes and boundary conditions. A useful model distinguishes user experience measures (such as completed accessible trips) from engineering response measures (such as dispatch latency). Their relationships are causal hypotheses requiring validation, not guaranteed mappings.

Worked reasoning: apply it to the shuttle case

A requested trip can be accepted in 0.2 seconds yet fail to be fulfilled. Define effectiveness as completed eligible trips / eligible requests and a performance measure as 95th-percentile dispatch latency. Trace both to scenarios and recognize that improving one need not improve the other.

Assumption to challenge: Using a software response-time measurement as a substitute for a mission-level outcome. Record what observation or test would expose this failure before using the result in a decision.

645-632 · Week 3

Develop and analyze the functional architecture from black-box behavior to allocated white-box functions

Course: Applied Analytics for Model-Based Systems Engineering · Week: 03 of 12 · Phase 3 companion: edition 1.0
Study guide: Open the original course guide and jump to Week 3 for prescribed readings, full assignment and mastery criteria.
Cross-course case: Fictional Autonomous Campus Shuttle. Figures are synthetic and illustrate reasoning; they do not validate real transport operations.

The idea in context

Functional decomposition is about responsibility and flow, not organizational departments. Start from black-box inputs, outputs, controls and required transformations. Decompose only far enough to make allocations, interface exchanges and testable conditions explicit. Every child function must contribute to its parent; orphan functions or unbalanced inputs reveal model defects. Activity, sequence and functional-flow views are complementary and should agree about triggers, outputs and exceptions.

Worked reasoning: apply it to the shuttle case

A black-box function “transport passenger” can be refined into authenticate request → assign resource → board → move → alight → close trip. If a boarding event produces no custody record for mobility assistance, the flow contains an omitted interface; adding a diagram label alone does not close the gap.

Assumption to challenge: Treating every box on a functional diagram as an existing hardware component. Record what observation or test would expose this failure before using the result in a decision.

  1. What is black-box behavior?
  2. What is white-box behavior?
  3. Why model enabling functions?
Reveal guided answers

  1. What is black-box behavior? Externally observable transformation and interaction without exposing internal performers.

  2. What is white-box behavior? Behavior expressed through internal responsibilities, interactions, and allocations.

  3. Why model enabling functions? They make mission behavior possible, safe, secure, supportable, recoverable, and maintainable.

645-632 · Week 4

Model state-based behavior, modes, exceptions, hazards, and temporal consistency

Course: Applied Analytics for Model-Based Systems Engineering · Week: 04 of 12 · Phase 3 companion: edition 1.0
Study guide: Open the original course guide and jump to Week 4 for prescribed readings, full assignment and mastery criteria.
Cross-course case: Fictional Autonomous Campus Shuttle. Figures are synthetic and illustrate reasoning; they do not validate real transport operations.

The idea in context

A state machine represents legal configurations and transitions under events; it is not merely a flowchart. Define invariant conditions, guards, transition actions, entry/exit behavior and event priorities. A temporal property specifies how behavior must unfold, such as “a vehicle must never report AVAILABLE while an emergency stop is active.” Analyze unreachable states, deadlocks, concurrent events and reset behavior. Trace every exception to a safety or service consequence.

Worked reasoning: apply it to the shuttle case

States IDLE, ASSIGNED, BOARDING, EN_ROUTE and E_STOP are insufficient unless the transition from E_STOP to IDLE requires an authorized reset and health check. Formalize the invariant as E_STOP ⇒ not_available; a model query should flag any violation.

Assumption to challenge: Drawing transitions without guards or defining how conflicting events are ordered. Record what observation or test would expose this failure before using the result in a decision.

Test understanding before reading the answers

The next three questions are adapted from the original guide's Week 4 knowledge check, with its answer guidance retained so you can learn and self-check in one place.

  1. What is a state invariant?
  2. What is a guard?
  3. What is an orthogonal region?
Reveal guided answers

  1. What is a state invariant? A condition that must remain true while an element is in a state.

  2. What is a guard? A Boolean condition that must be true for a triggered transition to occur.

  3. What is an orthogonal region? A concurrent region of a state machine whose state evolves in parallel with other regions.

645-632 · Week 5

Specify and evaluate the logical architecture and its responsibilities

Course: Applied Analytics for Model-Based Systems Engineering · Week: 05 of 12 · Phase 3 companion: edition 1.0
Study guide: Open the original course guide and jump to Week 5 for prescribed readings, full assignment and mastery criteria.
Cross-course case: Fictional Autonomous Campus Shuttle. Figures are synthetic and illustrate reasoning; they do not validate real transport operations.

The idea in context

Logical architecture allocates responsibilities without prematurely committing to physical technology. Define services, ports, ownership, dependencies and failure domains, then evaluate cohesion and coupling. Every logical component needs reasons to exist, exposed interactions and the behavior it provides. Compare alternate boundaries by change ripple, latency and fault isolation—not just component count. Keep the function-to-logical-element allocation separate from the later logical-to-physical realization.

Worked reasoning: apply it to the shuttle case

Separating trip scheduling from safety interlock logic gives each independent authority and assurance obligations. Combining them might reduce interfaces but widen the blast radius of a scheduler defect. The right boundary depends on timing, safety integrity and deployment constraints.

Assumption to challenge: Assuming the smallest number of logical blocks gives the most maintainable architecture. Record what observation or test would expose this failure before using the result in a decision.

Specify interfaces, exchanges, allocations, ownership, and model-based change exposure

Course: Applied Analytics for Model-Based Systems Engineering · Week: 06 of 12 · Phase 3 companion: edition 1.0

The idea in context

Interfaces should be modeled as contracts: exchanged information or energy, units, valid ranges, timing, initiators, acknowledgment and failure action. Architecture queries can reveal missing ownership, incompatible units, untraced exchanges and unexpected dependencies. Change exposure is a graph property: a revised message schema may affect publishers, subscribers, test data, monitoring and operator procedures. A model query is only useful if relations and identifiers are kept current.

Worked reasoning: apply it to the shuttle case

A dispatch message contains eta_s with a required age below 5 seconds. A consuming display assumes minutes. An interface contract with units and freshness detects the mismatch before integration; a change-impact query identifies all subscribers and tests.

Assumption to challenge: Calling a drawn connector an interface specification without semantics or tolerances. Record what observation or test would expose this failure before using the result in a decision.

645-632 · Week 7

Elicit, derive, validate, and query system requirements from the integrated analysis

Course: Applied Analytics for Model-Based Systems Engineering · Week: 07 of 12 · Phase 3 companion: edition 1.0
Study guide: Open the original course guide and jump to Week 7 for prescribed readings, full assignment and mastery criteria.

The idea in context

Requirements are decisions about required behavior and constraint, not copy-pasted stakeholder prose. Derive them from scenario analyses, hazards, interfaces and allocations; distinguish origin, rationale, acceptance method and priority. Validate necessity and stakeholder relevance separately from syntactic quality. Query for unallocated requirements, conflicting thresholds, unmatched test methods and requirements with no source. Requirements remain provisional when the evidence or operating envelope is uncertain.

Worked reasoning: apply it to the shuttle case

“Shuttles shall be fast” is not testable. “For approved weekday scenarios, 95% of accepted requests shall have pickup wait ≤12 min” is measurable if “accepted,” measurement clock, sample plan and exclusions are defined.

Assumption to challenge: Mistaking bidirectional traceability for proof that the traced requirement is correct. Record what observation or test would expose this failure before using the result in a decision.

645-632 · Week 8

Build executable parametric models with units, provenance, constraints, and linked analytics

Course: Applied Analytics for Model-Based Systems Engineering · Week: 08 of 12 · Phase 3 companion: edition 1.0
Study guide: Open the original course guide and jump to Week 8 for prescribed readings, full assignment and mastery criteria.
Cross-course case: Fictional Autonomous Campus Shuttle. Figures are synthetic and illustrate reasoning; they do not validate real transport operations.

The idea in context

Parametric models connect design variables, physical units, constraint equations and outputs. They are not spreadsheets copied into model attributes. Declare dimensions, parameter ranges, estimation provenance, uncertainty type and solver assumptions. Verify dimensional consistency and boundary behavior before trusting a sensitivity analysis. An executable constraint is credible only within the model form and calibration range; numerical precision is not empirical accuracy.

Worked reasoning: apply it to the shuttle case

At 30 km/h, a 5-km route takes 10 min before stops. If boarding and intersections add 6 min, cycle time becomes 16 min. Explicit distance_km / speed_kmh × 60 + delay_min constraints prevent a km/h vs m/s unit error.

Assumption to challenge: Allowing a parameter equation to silently mix units or treating a calibrated point as a global law. Record what observation or test would expose this failure before using the result in a decision.

Uncertainty is not just a standard deviation

Cross-course pathways: Metrics 2, 3 and 9; Foundations M&S 3 and 8; Decision Science 11.

Four different reasons a number is uncertain

A waiting-time estimate can vary because different riders experience genuinely different trips (aleatory variation), because arrival-rate parameters were estimated from limited data (epistemic uncertainty), because the timestamp device has bias (measurement uncertainty), or because the simulator omits a relevant mechanism (model-form uncertainty). One error bar does not make these sources interchangeable. Some can be reduced by measurement or improved model design; others characterize the system under its operating scenarios.

The law of total variance helps separate scenarios: Var(Y) = E[Var(Y | S)] + Var(E[Y | S]). The first term is variation within scenarios and the second is the effect of having different scenario means. If dry-day and rainy-day mean waits differ significantly, a single pooled distribution can conceal the scenario risk even when its overall mean looks acceptable.

A derivation for correlated time components

Suppose trip time T = A + B, where A is travel time and B is boarding time. From expansion of E[(T-E[T])²],

Var(T) = Var(A) + Var(B) + 2 Cov(A,B).

For standard deviations 2 and 1 minute, and correlation ρ=0.6, variance is 4 + 1 + 2(0.6)(2)(1) = 7.4 min²; standard deviation is √7.4 = 2.72 min. With false independence, the result would be √5 = 2.24 min, understating the spread by about 18%. Under a joint normal assumption, the corresponding approximate 95th percentile is mean + 1.645 SD; with mean 15, it is 19.47 min versus 18.68 min. Real trip times may not be normal, so test support and tail behavior.

What a Monte Carlo run can and cannot do

tools/phase3_lab.py implements correlated_sum_mc. Fix a seed to reproduce a run; change seeds to measure Monte Carlo error; vary the correlation assumption to estimate model sensitivity. A million runs of a poorly chosen distribution do not validate it. The Monte Carlo standard error of an estimated probability p is approximately sqrt(p(1-p)/N) for independent draws; correlated replications require more care. For a rare p=0.001 and N=10,000, the expected count is only ten events—often inadequate for a confident tail estimate.

Practice and explanation

Problem: A and B have SDs 3 and 4 with correlation -0.5. What is the sum SD? Solution: Var(A+B)=9+16+2(-0.5)(3)(4)=13; SD √13≈3.606. Negative correlation reduces spread, though it does not guarantee small tails in nonnormal models.

Why randomized comparisons matter

Suppose dispatch policy A is evaluated only during low demand and B only in rain. Their observed difference combines policy and environment. Randomization aims to break systematic association between treatment and nuisance causes; blocking uses known nuisance regimes (such as dry/rain) so comparisons happen within comparable contexts. Replication provides a measure of residual variation. Neither makes a poorly defined measurement reliable.

A complete 2×2 calculation

Choose factor A = adaptive dispatch (low/high) and factor B = rainy conditions (dry/wet). Let observed mean wait at the four cells be: y--=14, y+-=12, y-+=18, y++=11 minutes. Define A effect as the average change on going from A- to A+: [(12-14)+(11-18)]/2 = -4.5 min. B effect: [(18-14)+(11-12)]/2 = +1.5 min. The A×B interaction is the difference in A effects across B, divided by two: [(11-18)-(12-14)]/2 = -2.5 min under effect coding. The policy benefit changes markedly in rain; the main-effect average does not fully convey the operational story.

A factorial design is not inherently causal when assignment is nonrandom or hidden interventions differ among cells. For two independent normally distributed groups with approximate common standard deviation σ and equal sample size per group n, a basic planning approximation for a two-sided test is n ≈ 2 (z_(1-α/2)+z_(1-β))² σ² / δ². This idealized formula is not a guarantee for interactions, heteroscedasticity or serial dependence. Blocking may reduce variance; clustering may increase it.

Separate event time from state

In discrete-event simulation (DES), time advances from one scheduled event to the next. The state may include busy, the waiting queue, resources, number served and the event calendar. At time t, process all relevant event types with deterministic tie rules; then schedule future events. Two critical assertions are that no resource serves two jobs simultaneously and every completed job must have entered the system.

A minimal example has arrivals at minutes [0,1,2], one server and constant three-minute service. The first starts at 0 and leaves at 3. The second starts at 3, waits two minutes and leaves at 6. The third starts at 6, waits four minutes and leaves at 9. Mean waiting is (0+2+4)/3=2 minutes. Run both deterministic_queue and des_queue to confirm this known-answer fixture. A code trace is more informative than an animation for detecting double booking.

Analytic queueing model and its limitations

For a stationary M/M/1 queue with Poisson arrival rate λ, independent exponential service rate μ and λ<μ, utilization is ρ=λ/μ. Expected total time in system is W=1/(μ-λ), queue wait is Wq=λ/[μ(μ-λ)], and expected number in system is L=λW by Little's law. If λ=8/hour and μ=12/hour, ρ=2/3, W=0.25 h=15 min, and Wq=8/(12×4) h=10 min. These are steady-state expectations, not 95th percentiles or guarantees. At λ≥μ the ideal stationary mean diverges.

Define horizon, initial queue, arrival regime, service discipline, abandonments and replications. If studying the 8–9 a.m. rush period, starting with an empty queue may be appropriate if that is the real state at 8 a.m.; applying warm-up to remove the rush period would be wrong. If studying long-run behavior, quantify warm-up bias and serial correlation. Report variation across independent replications, not across adjacent timestamps.

Differential equations and solver error from first principles

Derive a state equation from a balance

Choose temperature T (°C) as a state, ambient temperature Ta and positive cooling coefficient k (1/min). In a well-mixed single-node cooling approximation, heat loss is proportional to temperature difference: dT/dt=-k(T-Ta). Separate variables: d(T-Ta)/(T-Ta)=-k dt; integrate to obtain T(t)=Ta+(T0-Ta)e^(-kt).

For T0=50°C, Ta=20°C, k=0.1/min and t=10 min, T(10)=20+30 e^-1≈31.036°C. This closed-form solution is an oracle for testing a numerical integrator. Do not interpret the result as a validated battery thermal model: real packs have distributed states and changing heat production.

Explicit Euler, step size and stability

For a general ODE dx/dt=f(x,t), Euler uses x_(n+1)=x_n+h f(x_n,t_n). Here, T_(n+1)=T_n-hk(T_n-Ta). Define error against the analytical solution, halve h and check the expected trend. For this linear homogeneous deviation, the Euler amplification factor is (1-hk); absolute stability requires |1-hk|<1, i.e. 0<hk<2. Even stable steps can be inaccurate or give nonphysical oscillations when h is too large.

Use euler_cooling(step_min=1) and compare with step_min=0.1. Inspect conservation and signs as additional checks. More complex stiff systems may require implicit methods and solver diagnostics.

Hybrid switching is a separate issue

A controller that turns cooling on at 40°C and off at 37°C creates a hybrid model with continuous temperature and discrete switch state. A large time step can overshoot thresholds and miss rapid events. A real-time fixed-step implementation trades computation deadlines against event accuracy. Study hysteresis, event detection and end-to-end response rather than only smooth thermal trajectories.

Feedback turns a causal story into a dynamic model

Cross-course pathways: Systems Thinking 1–12; Simulation Foundations 1 and 12; Decision Science 11.

Signs describe direction, not goodness

A positive causal link means “an increase in X, everything else fixed, tends to increase Y relative to what it otherwise would have been.” It does not mean X is beneficial. A balancing feedback loop counteracts change; reinforcing loops amplify. A narrative about feedback remains a hypothesis until it produces an observable, falsifiable behavior pattern.

Imagine greater ridership produces revenue, which supports service frequency, which attracts ridership: reinforcing. However, ridership also increases queueing and waiting, reducing attractiveness: balancing. Which dominates can change over time. The causal diagram alone does not determine whether the system will oscillate; stocks, equations, thresholds, delays and parameters are still needed.

Stock versus flow

Let Q be people waiting and λ and μ be people per hour entering and boarding. The conservation identity is dQ/dt=λ-μ_eff, with μ_eff≤Q/dt + λ in a discrete numerical step to prevent departures without passengers. Units must match: people/hour times hours gives people. Starting with Q=5 and λ=12/h and μ=10/h over four hours gives Q=13, assuming no saturation or abandonment. Verify with stock_flow(5,12,10,hours=4).

An actual service includes abandonment, time-dependent demand, capacity adaptation and multiple queues. Document these omissions and their potential effects.

Delay produces unintuitive decisions

Suppose management orders vehicles based on the backlog four weeks ago. The queue may already have declined by the time vehicles arrive. A model can represent perceived queue as a smoothed or delayed state and a capacity adjustment with its own delivery delay. Test the intervention with the delay halved and doubled; if its benefit reverses, the model suggests a monitoring experiment rather than a universal prescription.

Extreme-condition tests: zero arrivals should not produce phantom passengers; no vehicles should not allow departures; unlimited capacity should eliminate service backlog under the model's assumptions; stocks must not become negative.

Challenge: distinguish an observed peak in ridership from evidence of an internal reinforcing loop. Demand seasonality and reporting changes are rival explanations that require data.

Three gates before a recommendation

Gate 1 — feasibility: hard constraints such as acceptable safety conditions, accessible service and capacity. An infeasible design is not rescued by a high preference score. What a weight means

A weight in additive multiattribute value should reflect the importance of moving an attribute from its worst to its best defined endpoint. Suppose normalized cost value for A is 1 and B is 0, whereas wait-time value for A is 0 and B is 1. With cost swing weight w, additive scores are V_A=w and V_B=1-w. They tie at w=0.5. The calculation is transparent but depends on agreed endpoints, independence assumptions and the shape of the value functions.

A raw weight of “60% safety, 40% cost” has no meaning without defining the safety and cost swings. Never normalize non-compensable safety criteria into a score that lets low cost offset unacceptable risk.

Sensitivity and regret

Evaluate decisions across plausible weights and uncertain states. A robust candidate can sacrifice a little expected performance to avoid catastrophic regret. Report switching thresholds, not simply the top-ranked option. A Pareto chart is an argument about trade-offs; it is not a final approval and does not establish predicted performance as valid.

When learning first is worth more than choosing now

Cross-course pathways: Decision Science 3 and 11; Metrics 11; Simulation Foundations 7.

A two-state decision tree

Suppose a flexible fleet policy A pays +$50k in high-demand conditions and -$20k in low demand. A conservative policy B pays +$25k in both states. With equal probability of high and low demand, EV(A)=0.5(50)+0.5(-20)=15 thousand and EV(B)=25 thousand. Without new information, choose B under risk-neutral expected value.

Perfect information would reveal the state before choice: choose A in high demand and B in low, yielding 0.5(50)+0.5(25)=37.5 thousand. Expected value of perfect information EVPI=37.5-25=12.5 thousand. This is an upper bound on the expected value of any information about the state for the specified model, before information cost; real tests are imperfect and may arrive too late.

Call expected_value_and_evpi([[50,-20],[25,25]],[0.5,0.5]) to reproduce the calculation.

Imperfect signals require Bayes' rule

If a demand forecast has sensitivity 80% for high demand and specificity 70% for low demand, with prior 50/50, then P(high | positive) = .8×.5/(.8×.5 + .3×.5) ≈ .727. Do not use the model's accuracy as the posterior probability of high demand. Evaluate the best action conditional on each signal, then average signal-specific values to compute expected value of sample information (EVSI). An experimental program has value only if it could change a decision enough to offset its own cost and delay.

A robust alternative

Expected value is not the only rational criterion. A risk-averse authority may minimize the worst regret; an irreversible investment may value downside protection. Compute the regret of choosing A or B relative to the state-specific optimal action, and report different decision criteria separately. Explain trade-offs to stakeholders rather than silently choosing a criterion.

Executable models and trustworthy traceability queries

Cross-course pathways: Applied MBSE Analytics 1–12; Model-Based Systems Engineering Phase 1; Decision Science 8.

A model element is more than a named box

A queryable digital model requires stable IDs, types, meaningful relations, controlled configurations and validation rules. A requirements graph might contain Need N1 → Requirement R1 → Function F2 → Component C3 → Test T4. Traversing that graph checks coverage, not correctness. If R1 contains an impossible performance target, complete traceability merely reveals a well-documented error.

Record a validation question for each relation: Is the need justified? Does the requirement logically derive from it? Is the function sufficient? Does the component provide the function under the scenario? Does the test actually exercise the specified condition? Automated traversal can catch missing links but does not answer those semantic questions without richer rules and evidence.

Parametric constraint discipline

A model quantity cycle_time_min = 60 route_km / mean_speed_kmh + boarding_min + dwell_min has a defined dimension. For 5 km at 30 km/h plus 4 min boarding and 2 min dwell, cycle time is 16 min. If the route length is accidentally entered in meters while labeled kilometers, the constraint can produce a plausible-looking but absurd answer. Include ranges, unit conversion tests and parameter provenance.

An analytic model might then constrain throughput ≤ vehicles × riders_per_vehicle × 60 / cycle_time_min, but that is an upper bound: schedules, state, headways and load distribution can lower realized throughput. It is not automatically a performance guarantee.

Change-impact graph exercise

Suppose a message field changes from eta_s to eta_ms. A graph query should identify publishers, consumers, transformations, test fixtures, dashboards and verification procedures. The units, not the name, carry the core semantic change. Require a test that demonstrates conversion before closing the change.

Coupling simulators without coupling their errors

Cross-course pathways: Advanced M&S 4–8 and 11; MBSE Analytics 6 and 10.

Interoperability has three layers

Syntactic interoperability means tools exchange structured bytes. Semantic interoperability means each side agrees what those bytes mean, including unit, coordinate frame, clock, causality and operational range. Dynamic interoperability means the coupling accurately handles time advancement, events, delays and conservation. Success at one layer does not prove the others.

The Functional Mock-up Interface (FMI) is a standard format/API for exchanging dynamic simulation models as Functional Mock-up Units, with different types for model exchange, co-simulation and scheduled execution. Its specification defines interfaces, not universal numerical correctness. HLA provides federation mechanisms for simulation interoperability; one must still make application-specific time and ownership decisions.

Step exchange and delay

Suppose the propulsion simulator integrates at h=0.01 s and the fleet/dispatch simulator communicates once each second. On a fast controller event, the dispatch side sees up to roughly 1 s of stale physical-state information depending on the handshake. A solver refined to 0.001 s inside propulsion does not eliminate interface staleness. Refine communication step, evaluate event-driven exchange, or establish a safe bounded interpolation with explicit error justification.

If electric input power is 10 kW with 85% mechanical efficiency, then heat and other losses total 1.5 kW. A coupled thermal module receiving 0.6 kW either omits a loss path or violates the energy accounting. Reconcile flows at interfaces with a declared system boundary.

Hybrid state and event handling

Continuous temperature may cause a discrete cooling mode to switch on at 40°C and switch off at 37°C. Hysteresis helps avoid chatter; the simulator must detect threshold crossings, handle exactly simultaneous events and restore consistent continuous states. Fixed-step real-time simulation needs deadline margin and safe test isolation; a model that runs offline does not automatically fit hardware-in-the-loop execution.

Checklist: model manifest; typed variables; clocks; owner; initialization; scenario; time sync; event priority; conservation; step-refinement evidence; failure behavior; baseline revisions.

When a response surface deserves your trust

Cross-course pathways: Metrics 7; Decision Science 8–10; Advanced M&S 7 and 12.

Predictive fit is not a decision guarantee

A surrogate approximates an expensive physics model or discrete-event simulator. Linear regression, response-surface quadratics and Gaussian processes differ in flexibility, data needs and uncertainty representation. Select the approximation by the smoothness and discontinuities of the real response; avoid forcing a polynomial across a mode change, capacity threshold or abrupt controller switch.

Partition experiments into design/training, validation and final confirmation. Random splitting can leak information when repeated runs share the same weather scenario or parameter configurations; group by scenario or time when future conditions differ. Report residual patterns, held-out error, domain and failure cases alongside an aggregate RMSE.

Demonstration

Suppose simulated mean wait at fleet counts 2, 3 and 4 is 18, 11 and 9 min. A quadratic interpolant can pass through all three points. That does not justify predicting physical behavior at fleet 1 or 8, and it cannot guarantee the 95th percentile. A fitted polynomial may become nonmonotonic or predict negative wait outside the observed domain. The surrogate needs constraints or explicit use bounds.

Optimization often seeks the most favorable region for the surrogate, which is also where its approximation error can be exploited. After choosing a candidate optimum, rerun the original high-fidelity model with fresh seeds and adverse scenarios. If the candidate loses its margin, update the surrogate and decision rather than rationalizing away the discrepancy.

What to write in the model card

List input domains and units, training scenarios, model family, hyperparameters, validation strategy, key error metrics including tails, uncertainty treatment, high-fidelity confirmation and prohibited uses. Refit after domain changes. A narrow but truthful use claim is more valuable than a broad unsupported one.

An end-to-end analysis: evidence to conditional choice

Cross-course pathway: All six Phase 3 courses. Use as a capstone orientation.

Build the decision chain rather than six unrelated reports

Consider a fictional shuttle service facing a choice between two dispatch policies and fleet sizes 2–4. The MBSE course specifies needs, operational scenarios, functions, interfaces and parameter constraints. Metrics analysis defines the measured wait-time distribution, demand uncertainty, reliability and suitable test design. Foundations of Simulation builds an event calendar with correct resource contention. Advanced Simulation adds battery energy, temperature and time synchronization where these mechanisms materially affect the decision. Systems Dynamics asks how demand, service reputation, maintenance backlog and investment respond over months. Decision Science compares feasible alternatives and evaluates the value of an additional pilot.

A coherent artifact chain might be:

NEED-SVC-01 → REQ-WAIT-01 → SCN-PEAK-RAIN → MODEL-DES-v2 + MODEL-THERM-v1 → DATA-WAIT-2026 → MEAS-P95 → DEC-FLEET-03.

Every arrow needs ownership and a tested interpretation. In particular, simulated 95th-percentile wait is not measured qualification evidence.

A deliberately adversarial review

A simulation yields estimated p95 wait of 11.4 min, apparently within a 12-min requirement. But the demand model omits a peak scenario, correlated rain increases boarding delay, and a charging change is not reflected in the model configuration. The data did not include accessibility trips. The correct engineering response is not “pass.” Narrow the claim, repair missing assumptions, design a discriminating test and record a conditional decision.

Capstone minimum data package

Preserve scenario manifest, input datasets (synthetic clearly labeled), model and solver versions, seeds, design matrix, results, sensitivity and uncertainty, experiment plans, visualizations, source code, tests, decision thresholds, reviewer notes, and a list of prohibited interpretations. A second student should be able to rerun one pivotal result without guessing hidden settings.
