## The idea in context

Design of experiments isolates effects by deliberately varying factors and managing noise. Randomization reduces confounding; blocking handles nuisance variation; replication estimates error. Statistical power depends on effect size, variability and sample size, not merely the number of conditions. An experiment with many factors and too few replicates can produce precise-looking but untestable interactions.

## Worked reasoning: apply it to the shuttle case

Compare scheduling policies A/B during dry and rainy days. Block by weather and randomize order within blocks. If policy A is always tested on sunny mornings, observed performance difference could be weather rather than policy.

**Assumption to challenge:** Changing one factor at a time while missing interactions or time-related confounding. Record what observation or test would expose this failure before using the result in a decision.

## Test understanding before reading the answers

The next three questions are adapted from the **original guide's Week 5 knowledge check**, with its answer guidance retained so you can learn and self-check in one place.

1. What is an experimental unit?
2. Why randomize?
3. What does blocking do?

<details><summary><strong>Reveal guided answers</strong></summary>

1. **What is an experimental unit?** The smallest unit independently assigned to a treatment or condition under the design.

2. **Why randomize?** To reduce assignment bias and distribute unknown nuisance effects, supporting valid causal comparisons.

3. **What does blocking do?** Groups similar experimental units or runs so known nuisance variability is separated from treatment comparison.

</details>
