## The idea in context

Inventory policy uses a stock of usable parts, replenishment orders, delivery delay, consumption and shortage pressure. Ordering based on perceived inventory creates dynamics different from reacting to actual stock. Pipeline inventory matters: increasing orders does not instantly produce parts. Robust policies balance safety stock, service levels, variability and obsolescence.

## Worked reasoning: apply it to the shuttle case

Demand uses 8 parts/week; shipment lead time is 3 weeks. A naive reorder point of 8 parts ignores expected 24 parts of lead-time consumption, before adding uncertainty or safety stock.

**Assumption to challenge:** Reducing on-hand inventory without modeling the supply pipeline and lead-time variability. Record what observation or test would expose this failure before using the result in a decision.

## Test understanding before reading the answers

The next three questions are adapted from the **original guide's Week 9 knowledge check**, with its answer guidance retained so you can learn and self-check in one place.

1. Why is the supply line a stock?
2. What creates bullwhip?
3. Why can higher target inventory fail?

<details><summary><strong>Reveal guided answers</strong></summary>

1. **Why is the supply line a stock?** Ordered material accumulates while in production/transit before receipt.

2. **What creates bullwhip?** Delays, correction rules, forecasting, batching, gaming, and local information can amplify demand changes.

3. **Why can higher target inventory fail?** It may increase orders, cost, expiration, and oscillation without reducing lead time.

</details>
