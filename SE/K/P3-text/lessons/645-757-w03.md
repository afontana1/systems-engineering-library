## The idea in context

Input modeling asks which process produced the data. Arrival events can have diurnal patterns and weather dependence; service times may mix passenger classes. Build an empirical baseline, test candidate families and examine dependence before parametric fitting. Parameter uncertainty and scenario uncertainty should be propagated separately when possible; a precisely fitted wrong distribution is still wrong.

## Worked reasoning: apply it to the shuttle case

During class change, arrivals average 15/min; at midday, 3/min. Fitting a single homogeneous Poisson rate of 9/min erases bursts and will systematically underpredict peak queues.

**Assumption to challenge:** Estimating an average arrival rate and treating it as constant across the entire simulation horizon. Record what observation or test would expose this failure before using the result in a decision.

## Test understanding before reading the answers

The next three questions are adapted from the **original guide's Week 3 knowledge check**, with its answer guidance retained so you can learn and self-check in one place.

1. What is aleatory variability?
2. What is epistemic uncertainty?
3. Why use an empirical distribution?

<details><summary><strong>Reveal guided answers</strong></summary>

1. **What is aleatory variability?** Inherent or treated-as-random variation in the modeled process.

2. **What is epistemic uncertainty?** Lack of knowledge about model form, parameters, data, or conditions that could be reduced or bounded.

3. **Why use an empirical distribution?** When data support is adequate and imposing a parametric family adds unjustified structure.

</details>
