## The idea in context

Mechanical models require choices of generalized coordinates, free-body diagrams, inertia, friction, constraints and actuation. Linearization describes local behavior; it can miss saturation, backlash and impacts. Mechanical energy conservation is a powerful verification oracle for lossless idealizations. Safety or performance decisions require justified parameter bounds, not simply nominal masses and stiffness values.

## Worked reasoning: apply it to the shuttle case

For a 1000-kg mass with net force 2000 N, initial acceleration is 2 m/s² before drag. At 5 m/s with quadratic drag coefficient 20 N/(m/s)², drag is 500 N and acceleration reduces to 1.5 m/s².

**Assumption to challenge:** Assuming constant acceleration while omitting speed-dependent losses and hard travel limits. Record what observation or test would expose this failure before using the result in a decision.

## Test understanding before reading the answers

The next three questions are adapted from the **original guide's Week 3 knowledge check**, with its answer guidance retained so you can learn and self-check in one place.

1. Why check both force and energy?
2. What is reflected inertia?
3. How should grade be modeled?

<details><summary><strong>Reveal guided answers</strong></summary>

1. **Why check both force and energy?** They detect different defects: instantaneous balance versus integrated work.

2. **What is reflected inertia?** Rotational inertia transformed through a gear ratio to an equivalent inertia at another shaft.

3. **How should grade be modeled?** As the gravitational force component along the route with a controlled sign convention.

</details>
