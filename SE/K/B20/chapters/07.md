## The problem you are learning to solve

Imagine the regional mobility council meets after an emergency drill. Agencies individually meet many of their own targets, yet some residents still cannot reach essential care. A sponsor wants a decisive engineering answer and a single performance number. You are responsible for making the inquiry sufficiently precise to support action without pretending away divided responsibility, incomplete evidence or affected people.

This week's question is **type e — people complexity: sast, interactive planning, and soft systems methodology**. The key conceptual move is *pluralist inquiry does not force false consensus*. The decision is not merely whether an artifact can be drawn: it is whether the claims made by that artifact survive a realistic change in authority, timing, evidence or public consequences. We therefore work from a real decision question toward an explicit representation, then test where the representation breaks.

## The central concept, built carefully

Strategic Assumption Surfacing and Testing, interactive planning and Soft Systems Methodology help people surface different purposes and worldviews. A root definition is a perspective-specific description, not a universal truth.

Write one CATWOE-style root definition from an emergency manager view and another from a wheelchair user view. Compare the conflicting transformation and ownership claims.

The engineering reasoning should move in a disciplined order. First name the observed or desired outcome, the boundary, the actors who have the right to act, and the time horizon. Next identify the mechanism hypothesized to link an intervention to that outcome. Then choose a representation—network, causal loop, interface contract, process model, probability table, capability map or deliberative inquiry—whose assumptions match the phenomenon. Finally specify evidence able to disconfirm the model. That last step matters because a persuasive representation can still be false, incomplete, or legitimate for only a narrow class of decisions.

### Foundational distinctions from the course guide

* Pluralism means actors can engage and negotiate despite different values; it does not imply easy agreement.
* SAST examines assumptions supporting competing policies and their importance and certainty.
* Interactive planning asks what would be designed now if unconstrained by the existing system, then works toward feasible movement.
* SSM models purposeful activity systems based on declared worldviews, not the real world itself.
* Accommodation is a change actors can live with, even without shared beliefs.
* Facilitators influence agenda, language, participation, and interpretation; neutrality should not be assumed.

## The mechanism beneath the method

Soft systems methods make different worldviews visible when there is no universally agreed objective function.

A rich picture or root definition is an invitation to debate perspectives, not proof of the correct system boundary.

## Deeper theory: soft systems methods address disagreements about what the system is for

Hard engineering methods are powerful when participants agree on the purpose, boundary, and relevant success measures of the system. Complex social situations often lack that agreement. One community sees a regional mobility program as transportation efficiency; another sees it as equitable access; a third sees it as a challenge to local autonomy. The disagreement is not always a lack of information that can be settled by one optimization model. It may concern legitimate but incompatible values and worldviews.

### A problem situation precedes a defined problem

Soft Systems Methodology (SSM) treats a messy situation as a starting point for inquiry rather than assuming that an objective problem statement already exists. Stakeholders can describe different activities, boundaries, power relationships, and undesirable outcomes. A **root definition** articulates a purposeful human activity system from a chosen worldview; it is not a claim that there is only one correct description of reality. A conceptual model built from that definition shows the logically necessary activities for that purpose, not necessarily a picture of how the organization currently works.

This distinction matters. A model of “a service that minimizes average transport time” may exclude riders who cannot afford the service, while a model of “a service that guarantees acceptable access for medically vulnerable people” includes different activities and constraints. Both can be internally coherent. Comparing them reveals what each definition makes visible and what it hides. The inquiry is not to pick the prettiest diagram but to identify changes that are **systemically desirable and culturally feasible**, and to make disagreement inspectable.

### Worldviews determine what counts as evidence

A service operator may regard on-time departure as the decisive metric. A patient advocate may regard reliable completion for medically vulnerable riders as more important. Their disagreement persists even if both accept the measured data. It is partly about the interpretation of success and the allocation of burdens. An optimization that selects weights without acknowledging this disagreement makes the conflict appear resolved mathematically when it has actually been embedded in an unexamined value model.

Strategic Assumption Surfacing and Testing (SAST) makes consequential assumptions and stakeholder positions explicit. It asks which assumptions support a proposed strategy, which are vulnerable, and how opposing perspectives could challenge them. Its purpose is not to produce artificial consensus; it is to expose dependencies of the recommendation on contested beliefs. Interactive planning similarly emphasizes participation in designing a desirable future, recognizing that implementation capacity and legitimacy cannot be manufactured solely through expert analysis.

### Why participation changes the model

When stakeholders participate in framing, they may reveal constraints invisible to the analyst. A dispatch algorithm can recommend a technically optimal route through an area that residents consider unsafe or inaccessible. A local official may disclose legal authority limits; a driver may describe an informal workaround that makes the current process function. These are not “soft issues” in the sense of being optional. They alter the feasible set and sometimes the operational meaning of the proposed system.

Participation also has limitations. Not every stakeholder has equal time, safety, language access, or institutional power to contribute. An apparently participatory workshop can reproduce existing hierarchies if the agenda, terminology, and decision rights are controlled by the same authority that defined the original problem. The method must therefore examine who is absent, whose evidence is dismissed, and who can challenge the final framing.

### Multiple conceptual models are a strength

A systems-engineering habit is to seek one authoritative model. In a contested problem situation, maintaining multiple purposeful models can be more informative than prematurely collapsing them. One model may focus on clinical access, another on public spending, another on emergency preparedness, and another on local autonomy. Their discrepancies indicate tradeoffs, omissions, and possible redesign opportunities. The models are tools for structured conversation and learning rather than predictive claims about a unique objective system.

The connection to technical systems engineering is not antagonistic. Once a jointly defensible intervention is identified, formal requirements, safety constraints, architecture models, and verification can be essential. The soft-systems work helps determine **which** technical problem deserves those tools and whose purposes must constrain the result. Mathematical rigor cannot compensate for silently optimizing the wrong conception of the situation.

### Where this method stops working

Voting on a single diagram can conceal minority harms or the fact that a party rejects the sponsor’s chosen boundary.

A responsible engineer should separate *model correctness* (the math or logic follows from its premises), *model credibility* (the premises are supported for this use), and *decision legitimacy* (the process respects authority, rights and affected parties). Passing the first test does not guarantee the other two. In particular, neither a hypothetical exercise nor an attractive decision matrix certifies an operational regional service.

## Reading the broader system

The three Phase 5 books offer complementary lenses. The systems-of-systems lens asks which independently managed constituents must cooperate and what technical evidence can travel between them. The enterprise lens asks how resources, incentives, governance and institutional history shape that cooperation. The critical-complexity lens asks whose interpretation dominates, which boundaries exclude harms and when no single technical objective deserves priority. Use all three when a proposed solution changes both technology and public obligations. Their explanations can disagree without one being automatically mistaken: they may be answering different questions.

## Guided rehearsal from the weekly guide

1. Create a rich picture including structures, processes, conflicts, emotions, exclusions, and environmental pressures.
2. Develop four root definitions and CATWOE analyses from distinct worldviews.
3. Build purposeful activity models and compare them with the current situation.
4. Run a SAST matrix for three intervention strategies.
5. Develop an idealized design and identify near-term changes that increase choice without pretending full consensus.

Pause after each step. Compare the result with the decision purpose and record disagreement, ambiguity, and negative evidence instead of forcing convergence.

## Self-check: work before revealing the lab response

1. **Define the model.** What is the unit of analysis and why does it match this week's decision? Name a plausible alternative boundary.
2. **Trace the mechanism.** Which chain of events, institutions or variables connects intervention to outcome? Name one missing link.
3. **Check the evidence.** Which premise is most fragile? What independent observation would falsify it?
4. **Change the case.** Suppose create two defensible conceptual activity models for the same emergency-access situation and an agenda for resolving disputes. Which claims and actors must be reconsidered?
5. **Defend a bounded decision.** What can be responsibly recommended today, to whom, with what review trigger and non-claims?

### Explanations and answer criteria

A proficient answer identifies a bounded outcome, a model whose assumptions are explicit, a provenance chain, a credible alternative explanation, and a named legitimate decision maker. For Question 4, a single undifferentiated average or a redraw of the same diagram is insufficient: explain which causal or normative premise changed and how that affects the recommendation. For Question 5, separate evidence-backed statements from conditional hypotheses and unresolved stakeholder disputes. If your answer cannot state a stop rule or the consequence of error, revise it before proceeding.

**Specific red-team test:** Voting on a single diagram can conceal minority harms or the fact that a party rejects the sponsor’s chosen boundary.
