## The problem you are learning to solve

Imagine the regional mobility council meets after an emergency drill. Agencies individually meet many of their own targets, yet some residents still cannot reach essential care. A sponsor wants a decisive engineering answer and a single performance number. You are responsible for making the inquiry sufficiently precise to support action without pretending away divided responsibility, incomplete evidence or affected people.

This week's question is **critical systems practice: explore and produce**. The key conceptual move is *explore and produce turns plural narratives into testable hypotheses*. The decision is not merely whether an artifact can be drawn: it is whether the claims made by that artifact survive a realistic change in authority, timing, evidence or public consequences. We therefore work from a real decision question toward an explicit representation, then test where the representation breaks.

## The central concept, built carefully

Explore the situation with observation, interviews, temporal patterns and conflicting causal stories. Produce candidate intervention models without pretending there is already agreement on objectives. Separate empirical uncertainty from differences in values.

For a proposal to change triage priority, draw two competing causal accounts: congestion-driven delay and discriminatory eligibility filtering. List distinct observations that could discriminate between them.

The engineering reasoning should move in a disciplined order. First name the observed or desired outcome, the boundary, the actors who have the right to act, and the time horizon. Next identify the mechanism hypothesized to link an intervention to that outcome. Then choose a representation—network, causal loop, interface contract, process model, probability table, capability map or deliberative inquiry—whose assumptions match the phenomenon. Finally specify evidence able to disconfirm the model. That last step matters because a persuasive representation can still be false, incomplete, or legitimate for only a narrow class of decisions.

### Foundational distinctions from the course guide

* Explore uses multiple systemic perspectives to prevent one framing from dominating.
* Primary issues receive direct intervention; secondary issues shape feasibility, ethics, and learning.
* Produce chooses methodologies, models, and methods based on issue structure and stakeholder conditions.
* Methods can be sequenced, nested, used in parallel, or triangulated—but handoffs must be designed.
* An intervention strategy includes participation, governance, data, evidence, facilitation, resources, safety, and adaptation.
* EPIC is iterative; new learning can require returning to Explore or Produce.

## Graduate depth: critical systems practice makes problem boundaries contestable

### Exploring a problem does not begin with a single objective

In complex public-service situations, different participants may disagree not only about which solution is best but also about what counts as the problem. A regional authority may define success as reduced average response time; disabled travelers may define it as reliable accessible service; operators may emphasize safe workload; local governments may emphasize autonomy and affordability. A conventional optimization model cannot resolve these differences by inventing one objective function. Critical Systems Practice begins by making purposes, boundaries, excluded perspectives, power, and evidence claims discussable.

An explicit boundary judgment asks who is treated as the client, who is the decision maker, whose knowledge is accepted, and who bears the consequences. These are not peripheral ethical questions added after modeling. They determine which states, outputs, constraints, and stakeholder values the model represents. If unserved people are removed from the measured population, an apparently improving service score may coexist with deteriorating mission access. A boundary critique can therefore change the measured technical conclusion.

### Producing multiple complementary representations

No single modeling tradition captures all relevant mechanisms. A stock-flow model can examine how service backlog responds to capacity investment and delay; a network graph can show interagency dependencies; a causal map can clarify stakeholder hypotheses; a multiattribute model can expose value tradeoffs. Each representation answers different questions and may contradict the others if definitions are inconsistent. Critical pluralism does not mean accepting all claims as equally correct. It means choosing methods in view of their purpose, assumptions, and blind spots, then confronting conflicting evidence.

A useful Northstar inquiry might compare three system boundaries. The vehicle-fleet boundary supports utilization analysis; the regional service boundary includes transfers and receiving capacity; the social-access boundary includes whether people can request service, understand communications, and trust the system. A decision that improves all three is strong. A decision that improves only vehicle utilization requires an explicit rationale for the costs shifted to other boundaries.

### Power, participation, and technical legitimacy

Participation must be designed rather than assumed. Those most affected may lack time, technical vocabulary, data access, or authority. Repeating a workshop with the same powerful stakeholders does not make a system boundary legitimate. Use accessible elicitation methods, record dissent, distinguish observed facts from normative claims, and identify who can veto or authorize a proposed intervention. Independent evidence can challenge an influential narrative, while lived operational knowledge can reveal model omissions that formal data systems never measured.

The tension with engineering optimization is productive. Once the decision context and non-negotiable duties are clear, mathematical analysis can compare feasible interventions and quantify tradeoffs. Before that point, precise optimization may simply encode unexamined power. A critical review asks whether the chosen population, timeframe, cost boundary, and service definition exclude material consequences. It also asks whether the team has identified a reasonable route for revising the boundary as new participants or evidence emerge.

Producing an intervention proposal therefore requires two kinds of traceability: technical links from assumptions through models to predicted effects, and institutional links from affected interests through deliberation to legitimate authorization. The outcome is not a universal consensus or a claim of neutrality. It is a transparent, revisable rationale that states what is known, who disagrees, which harms remain possible, and how the intervention will be evaluated.

Treat the next numerical example or decision matrix as a controlled thought experiment. Draw a table with columns **input**, **meaning and unit**, **source or fictional assumption**, **transformation**, **result**, and **plausible failure**. Populate that table from this scenario:

Work the result twice. On the second pass, replace one favorable condition with the adverse circumstance in the next section. Compute the difference when a valid model exists; when the problem is political or ethical rather than computational, produce an explicit comparison of consequences, affected groups, evidence and objections instead of inventing an objective function. Remember that a mathematical result never establishes the validity of assumptions by itself.

### Where this method stops working

A stakeholder workshop is not representative sampling, and conceptual models do not become causal evidence merely by being mapped.

A responsible engineer should separate *model correctness* (the math or logic follows from its premises), *model credibility* (the premises are supported for this use), and *decision legitimacy* (the process respects authority, rights and affected parties). Passing the first test does not guarantee the other two. In particular, neither a hypothetical exercise nor an attractive decision matrix certifies an operational regional service.

## Reading the broader system

The three Phase 5 books offer complementary lenses. The systems-of-systems lens asks which independently managed constituents must cooperate and what technical evidence can travel between them. The enterprise lens asks how resources, incentives, governance and institutional history shape that cooperation. The critical-complexity lens asks whose interpretation dominates, which boundaries exclude harms and when no single technical objective deserves priority. Use all three when a proposed solution changes both technology and public obligations. Their explanations can disagree without one being automatically mistaken: they may be answering different questions.

## Self-check: work before revealing the lab response

1. **Define the model.** What is the unit of analysis and why does it match this week's decision? Name a plausible alternative boundary.
2. **Trace the mechanism.** Which chain of events, institutions or variables connects intervention to outcome? Name one missing link.
3. **Check the evidence.** Which premise is most fragile? What independent observation would falsify it?
4. **Change the case.** Suppose a field interview contradicts the program dashboard. Design a cross-check that preserves dissent. Which claims and actors must be reconsidered?
5. **Defend a bounded decision.** What can be responsibly recommended today, to whom, with what review trigger and non-claims?

### Explanations and answer criteria

A proficient answer identifies a bounded outcome, a model whose assumptions are explicit, a provenance chain, a credible alternative explanation, and a named legitimate decision maker. For Question 4, a single undifferentiated average or a redraw of the same diagram is insufficient: explain which causal or normative premise changed and how that affects the recommendation. For Question 5, separate evidence-backed statements from conditional hypotheses and unresolved stakeholder disputes. If your answer cannot state a stop rule or the consequence of error, revise it before proceeding.

**Specific red-team test:** A stakeholder workshop is not representative sampling, and conceptual models do not become causal evidence merely by being mapped.
