## The engineering situation

Practices that work for one team can fail when authority, funding, contracts, suppliers, platforms, and mission dependencies span an enterprise. The essential question is: **How should NMS scale coherence and learning without recreating a centralized approval bureaucracy?**

The central idea of this chapter is **scaling requires governance of dependencies, not more ceremonies**. Before a method is selected, the engineer must distinguish the decision being made, what evidence could falsify the leading approach, and which obligations cannot be relaxed. In the Northstar case, engineers, operators, safety leads and suppliers are accountable for different portions of one mission result. A locally successful artifact is not sufficient if the complete outcome is unsupported.

## Explanation: why the method works

Multiple teams produce a system only if their contracts, shared test resources, change authority, supplier lead times and evidence formats fit together. Track dependency age, integration queue, and supplier commitments; optimize end-to-end flow rather than local team utilization.

A useful way to understand the mechanism is to follow the causal chain explicitly. Start with a stimulus or observed condition; ask which model connects it to the engineering outcome; identify the assumptions that let the model predict anything; and define the next decision. Avoid confusing a convenient dashboard measure with the actual mission variable. Where constraints are hard—such as safety authorization, configuration compatibility or accessibility—treat them as eligibility conditions before computing preferences.

**Deepening the reasoning — Cross-team constraints.** Teams should share versioned interface contracts and integration test capacity plans. A system-level throughput measure prevents local feature velocity from hiding long dependencies. Log technical debt that affects future assurance and change cost.

### Connect the theory to the course vocabulary

- Scaling means maintaining mission coherence, architecture integrity, evidence flow, and decision speed across boundaries. More synchronized meetings can increase delay without improving coherence.
- Portfolio governance should set outcomes, constraints, investment, risk appetite, and threshold decisions while teams decide within bounded authority.
- Interactive technical oversight uses current evidence, queries, demonstrations, and dialogue instead of episodic document transfer.
- Contracts and supplier interfaces should align deliverables, data rights, integration assets, acceptance evidence, and change mechanisms with learning intervals.
- Long-lead work needs options, emulators, interface contracts, early evidence, and explicit commitment decisions.
- Transformation measures must combine response, flow, quality, outcome, workforce, architecture, and economic indicators. Velocity comparison across teams is prohibited.

The statements above originate in the assigned course guide; use them as technical claims to test against a specific scenario, not as terminology to memorize. For each claim, ask what observation could show that it is false or inapplicable in the current design. The same discipline distinguishes an engineering argument from a procedural assertion.

### Scaling agile work changes the coordination problem

Local team autonomy can improve adaptation, but the system may depend on shared resources, interfaces, certification evidence, and supplier deliveries. These dependencies create a coordination problem that cannot be solved solely by increasing the number of independent backlogs. A team's locally sensible priority may delay a system-level integration milestone or invalidate another team's test baseline.

Scaling therefore requires a distinction between decisions that can remain local and decisions that must be coordinated at system level. Interface standards, hazard controls, shared test environments and configuration releases often need common governance. The goal is not maximal centralization, which can slow learning, nor maximal autonomy, which can fragment the design. It is a deliberately chosen distribution of authority, with evidence showing when local changes may cross boundaries and who must respond.

## Deeper theory: scaling agility is a coordination problem with nonlocal risk

### Coordination load grows with dependency structure

Adding teams increases both work capacity and potential coordination burden. With `n` teams, a fully connected communication pattern has `n(n−1)/2` possible pairwise relationships; real engineering programs are not fully connected, but tightly coupled interfaces can approach that burden. A scaling strategy should therefore simplify the dependency network, not require every team to attend more shared meetings. Shared interface standards, common evidence definitions, stable integration points, and clearly allocated decision rights reduce the need for repeated negotiation. A central approval board may reduce local variance while increasing queueing delays and hiding conflicts until a late integration milestone.

Distinguish *local autonomy* from *system-level independence*. Teams can decide implementation details where contracts preserve common behavior, but cannot independently change safety assumptions or mission semantics relied upon by other constituents. At Northstar, the dispatch team and vehicle supplier may work in different increments yet remain coupled through message timing and fallback behavior. The relevant boundary is not the organizational chart; it is where a local action can impose unrecognized cost or danger on another party.

### Flow efficiency and utilization lead to different incentives

A resource can be highly utilized while the end-to-end system delivers slowly. When specialized reviewers or integration facilities operate close to capacity, variability makes waiting times rise sharply. Little's law, `WIP = throughput × lead_time`, illustrates that adding work in progress without increasing throughput generally extends lead time. Incentives that reward each supplier for keeping its staff fully occupied can therefore lengthen total integration time, amplify multitasking, and create rework. An apparently efficient organization can be globally inefficient when teams optimize their queues independently.

A more informative measure is the time from a validated need to operationally accepted capability. Track blocked work, age of unresolved cross-team decisions, integration failure rates, and evidence debt. Some slack capacity at shared test facilities and safety-review gates may reduce total delivery time and improve the ability to respond to surprises. This is not an argument for unlimited idle resources; it is recognition that variability, bottlenecks, and rare high-consequence issues change the economics of utilization.

### Contracts shape behavior more powerfully than ceremonies

A supplier paid solely for local milestone completion has weak incentives to expose interface uncertainty early if doing so threatens payment. An acquisition system that penalizes honest negative test results encourages late discovery and optimistic reporting. Governance should align commercial incentives with joint outcomes through objective integration evidence, shared risk registers, transparent configuration baselines, and explicit responsibility for cross-boundary failures. However, joint incentives must not erase supplier accountability or make safety requirements negotiable. The contract should protect the opportunity to learn while preserving independently verifiable obligations.

Decision rights should distinguish reversible local changes, architectural changes with network effects, and safety- or mission-critical releases. Escalate by the consequence and coupling of the decision, not by the seniority of the person proposing it. An interface owner may authorize routine compatible changes; a safety authority may need to approve modifications to hazard controls. Recording the rationale and affected configuration reduces repeated debates as personnel change.

### Scaling requires a multi-rate operating system

Operational incidents may demand decisions in minutes, software releases in days, vehicle modifications in months, and infrastructure procurement in years. One universal cadence cannot govern these processes coherently. Create linked planning horizons with explicit handoffs: short-cycle experiments generate evidence; system integration tests challenge cross-team assumptions; longer-cycle portfolio reviews authorize option exercise and funding changes. The goal is not rigid synchronization, but predictable points at which local learning affects shared commitments.

Judge the scaling system by resilience to change: can it incorporate a new constituent, discover an unsafe assumption, or redirect capacity without losing traceability or accountability? If expanding the program causes review queues to grow faster than engineering throughput, the architecture of governance is itself a system constraint. Scaling agility means controlling cross-boundary risk while retaining local learning speed, rather than reproducing team-level rituals at enterprise size.

## Worked Northstar engineering case

NMS plans to scale from one campus team to three sites. A single centralized architecture board would become a queue, while independent site teams would fragment interfaces. The proposed model uses a common platform and interface council with published constraints and automated conformance, site capability teams with bounded variant authority, a quarterly portfolio outcome review, and event-driven escalation for threshold risks. Supplier contracts include interface simulators, data/provenance, incremental evidence, and change-response expectations.

Team A delivers the new API in a sprint, but Team B’s safety harness is unavailable for six weeks. Report the actual time to verified system increment, not Team A’s sprint completion.

Work the example in two passes. First compute or trace the nominal outcome, naming the unit of analysis, released configuration and decision threshold. Second change one assumption, such as a degraded interface, heavier demand, invalid provenance or altered stakeholder constraint. If the recommendation changes, identify the threshold at which it changes; if it does not, explain the margin of robustness. A conclusion without its assumptions is not a reusable engineering result.

## A systematic check before accepting the result

1. **Boundary:** What part of the program, process or mission is represented, and what has been excluded?
2. **Evidence:** Which observations, versions, or tests support the premises, and what remains hypothetical?
3. **Causality:** Could the proposed mechanism be confused with a proxy, confounding condition or common dependency?
4. **Decision:** Which alternatives are still feasible, who has authority, and what would force reconsideration?
5. **Assurance:** Which safety, security, accessibility or quality guardrail cannot be traded for speed or convenience?

### Counterexample and failure boundary

More synchronized meetings cannot repair an undefined interface or missing test authority.

Notice that a mathematically consistent answer can still be an inappropriate decision if the intended use or validity assumptions have changed. In professional work, a responsible engineer records that limitation alongside the nominal result.

## Step 03 integrated NS-ASE15.1 technical management laboratory: Week 11

### Scaling across suppliers and shared integration resources

NMS comprises a vehicle supplier, software team, systems/interface authority, campus operations and independent assurance. Each can optimize its own delivery calendar while making integrated releases slower. The unit of scaling is a **dependency-resolved system increment**, not an aggregate of team-level velocities.

Represent cross-team work as a precedence directed acyclic graph. In a simple illustrative upgrade, a supplier hardware adapter takes 20 business days while software integration code takes 12 in parallel. The integration test then takes 6 days and independent assurance 8 days. The earliest eligible delivery is

\[T_{\mathrm{critical}}=\max(20,12)+6+8=34\;\text{business days}.\]

Adding five software engineers to reduce code work from 12 to 8 days does **not** shorten the 34-day critical path. A validated emulator that helps reduce the hardware critical activity from 20 to 15 days *could* shorten it to \(15+6+8=29\) days, provided final hardware integration still receives required evidence. This is a project precedence calculation, not a fleet availability forecast.

The [supplier-disruption scenario](../case/scenario-summary.csv) changes the model's illustrative supplier integration delay from 3 to 15 days while retaining the assured-learning capacities. Accepted releases fall from 47 to 31 within the 100-day window, and two critical items remain unresolved. The purpose is to expose the fragility of scale plans that assume instant supplier feedback. It does not imply every real supplier has a fixed delay or that the response distribution is known.

**Governance contract.** At every supplier interface define schema/version responsibilities, required delivery evidence, acceptance test ownership, long-lead procurement authority, escalation latency, cybersecurity provenance, data rights and permitted changes. Portfolio management allocates investments and exposure limits; local teams schedule work inside these constraints; safety and release authorities retain independent gates.

**Economic interpretation.** Shared benches and reviewers are system constraints. Funding separate program dashboards does not create physical integration capacity. If a supplier offers a cheaper module but omits configuration provenance, the proposal may be **ineligible** regardless of local cost advantage. A bounded experiment can acquire an emulator, study test fidelity and pre-plan a second-source option, but replacement authorization requires compatibility and assurance evidence.

**Explained exercise.** Integration takes 6 days after the slower of (i) a 25-day hardware path and (ii) a 14-day software path; assurance takes 7 days afterwards. Current critical path: \(25+6+7=38\) days. Improving software to 7 days yields no improvement. Improving validated hardware readiness to 18 yields \(18+6+7=31\) days. State what might invalidate this arithmetic: correlated rework, bench capacity contention, supplier acceptance failure, or nonsequential review.
