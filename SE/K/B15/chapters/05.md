## The engineering situation

Traditional plans often disguise uncertain assumptions as fixed tasks. Agile planning makes learning explicit while still controlling commitments. The essential question is: **What must be learned next, what evidence is sufficient, and which commitment should that learning enable or prevent?**

The central idea of this chapter is **planning is sequential reduction of uncertainty**. Before a method is selected, the engineer must distinguish the decision being made, what evidence could falsify the leading approach, and which obligations cannot be relaxed. In the Northstar case, engineers, operators, safety leads and suppliers are accountable for different portions of one mission result. A locally successful artifact is not sufficient if the complete outcome is unsupported.

## Explanation: why the method works

Replace a false-precision twelve-month feature plan with a sequence of learning commitments. Express each uncertain assumption as a falsifiable hypothesis, choose the cheapest experiment that could disconfirm it, and identify the decision that follows each possible outcome. An option is valuable because it delays irreversible commitment until evidence is sufficient.

A useful way to understand the mechanism is to follow the causal chain explicitly. Start with a stimulus or observed condition; ask which model connects it to the engineering outcome; identify the assumptions that let the model predict anything; and define the next decision. Avoid confusing a convenient dashboard measure with the actual mission variable. Where constraints are hard—such as safety authorization, configuration compatibility or accessibility—treat them as eligibility conditions before computing preferences.

**Deepening the reasoning — Hypothesis and stopping rule.** An option is not simply work deferred: it is a preserved feasible alternative with an exercise price and expiration conditions. Define what the experiment can reveal, what remains uncertain, and which alternatives are still available after the result.

### Connect the theory to the course vocabulary

- A learning interval is a bounded cycle from question to evidence to decision, not simply a sprint label.
- Hypotheses should predict observable behavior and define what result would change the plan.
- Minimum viable evidence is the least credible evidence needed for a specific decision—not the least work the team can demonstrate.
- Options have value when uncertainty is high and commitment is expensive, but maintaining every option creates cost and integration complexity.
- Rolling-wave plans increase detail as uncertainty falls. They do not excuse invisible dependencies or unbudgeted long-lead work.
- Each interval needs entry conditions, question, planned increment/experiment, integration target, evidence, decision owner, exit criteria, and baseline disposition.

The statements above originate in the assigned course guide; use them as technical claims to test against a specific scenario, not as terminology to memorize. For each claim, ask what observation could show that it is false or inapplicable in the current design. The same discipline distinguishes an engineering argument from a procedural assertion.

### Rolling commitment preserves options while reducing uncertainty

A plan is a hypothesis about future coordination, not a revelation of future events. Rolling-wave planning commits in detail where information is credible and keeps other choices provisional. Its value arises from the asymmetry between inexpensive early learning and costly late rework. But perpetual postponement is not agility: options expire, suppliers need commitments, and teams need stable interfaces to integrate.

The useful distinction is between **reversible decisions** and **commitments that lock in architecture, cost, or assurance claims**. An experiment should be designed to close a specific uncertainty before a commitment deadline. If no plausible observation could change the plan, the experiment is merely ceremonial. Agile engineering thus requires explicit learning hypotheses and criteria for converting new evidence into a decision, not only a calendar of short meetings.

## Graduate depth: rolling commitment as sequential decision-making under uncertainty

### Plans purchase information as well as deliverables

Traditional schedules often treat unknowns as if they are merely uncompleted tasks. In novel engineering, work may reveal that a planned architecture is infeasible. A rolling commitment approach distinguishes an irreversible decision from an affordable learning step. Committing too early can lock in a costly design; delaying every choice can sacrifice supplier slots, certification lead time, and market opportunity. The economic objective is neither maximum flexibility nor maximum speed, but appropriately timed commitment in light of information gained and options lost.

Represent the program state at review time `t` by `s_t` and a decision by `a_t`. A simple sequential formulation is `V_t(s) = max_a {r(s,a) + E[V_{t+1}(s_next) | s,a]}`, subject to safety, resource, schedule, and contractual constraints. This is a conceptual decision model: the transition probabilities and future rewards are rarely known precisely. It clarifies that today's experiment has value partly because it changes tomorrow's options. A prototype that tests an interface assumption can be worth undertaking even if it delivers little immediate mission capability.

### Hypotheses need observable disconfirmation

A learning interval should specify a falsifiable engineering claim, such as 'the candidate charger-controller interface maintains safe isolation after communication loss under the stated fault model'. It needs a measurement plan, test configuration, acceptance boundaries, and explicit consequences for failure. Calling a demonstration successful because the team completed planned tasks confuses activity with evidence. Conversely, an experiment that disproves an attractive design may create significant value by avoiding downstream investment.

A rough expected value of information is the expected loss avoided by making a later choice with improved evidence, minus the cost and delay of acquiring that evidence. It is not the number of experiments performed. When an experiment cannot change the decision—for example because a mandatory requirement already rules out an option—its decision value may be near zero. When an uncertainty is both consequential and resolvable, a focused test may dominate broad documentation effort.

### Reversible architecture and commitment deadlines

An option exists only if the organization preserves a feasible path to exercise it. Modularity, contract clauses, qualified suppliers, data rights, and interface stability can make a later change possible. A software feature flag does not make a redesigned physical connector reversible after manufacturing tooling is committed. Identify each decision's last responsible moment, the conditions for making it, and the cost of waiting beyond it. The last responsible moment is a risk-informed decision point, not a universal delay tactic.

For Northstar, purchasing a full vehicle fleet before verifying charging interoperability risks a high-cost failure. A staged order with a small representative integration trial could reduce that risk, provided supplier terms protect later capacity and the pilot is operationally representative. If the pilot's environment omits peak loads or degraded modes, it may give false assurance. The planning system should therefore track knowledge maturity, option expiration, irreversible expenditures, and evidence quality alongside schedule completion. Agile engineering is rigorous when it links adaptation to disciplined evidence and explicit authority, rather than to a preferred meeting cadence.

## Worked Northstar engineering case

Before committing to a new boarding mechanism, NMS needs to learn whether the proposed design meets accessibility and dwell-time outcomes across users and weather. A six-week interval uses a physical mock-up, human-in-the-loop trials, and a boarding simulation. The decision is not “finish prototype”; it is whether to retain concept A, switch to B, or defer tooling. Minimum viable evidence includes representative participants, defined measures, uncertainty bounds, safety observations, and trace to the decision criteria.

Before purchasing a dispatch server for a 400-request peak hour, Northstar runs load tests at 200, 300 and 450 requests/hour and defines an objective abandonment trigger for the single-server option.

Work the example in two passes. First compute or trace the nominal outcome, naming the unit of analysis, released configuration and decision threshold. Second change one assumption, such as a degraded interface, heavier demand, invalid provenance or altered stakeholder constraint. If the recommendation changes, identify the threshold at which it changes; if it does not, explain the margin of robustness. A conclusion without its assumptions is not a reusable engineering result.

## A systematic check before accepting the result

1. **Boundary:** What part of the program, process or mission is represented, and what has been excluded?
2. **Evidence:** Which observations, versions, or tests support the premises, and what remains hypothetical?
3. **Causality:** Could the proposed mechanism be confused with a proxy, confounding condition or common dependency?
4. **Decision:** Which alternatives are still feasible, who has authority, and what would force reconsideration?
5. **Assurance:** Which safety, security, accessibility or quality guardrail cannot be traded for speed or convenience?

### Counterexample and failure boundary

An experiment with no predefined decision consequence is a demonstration, not disciplined learning.

Learning interval: specify an experiment, threshold, maximum allowed exposure and decision branching for pass/ambiguous/fail. Notice that a mathematically consistent answer can still be an inappropriate decision if the intended use or validity assumptions have changed. In professional work, a responsible engineer records that limitation alongside the nominal result.

## Practise the explanation, not just the procedure

1. **Concept check:** Decompose program uncertainty into decision-relevant learning questions. **Explain:** Give a boundary, an example of evidence, and a concrete case in which the claim would fail.
2. **Concept check:** Write falsifiable technical, operational, stakeholder, and process hypotheses. **Explain:** Give a boundary, an example of evidence, and a concrete case in which the claim would fail.
3. **Concept check:** Design learning intervals that include build, integrate, evaluate, decide, and baseline update. **Explain:** Give a boundary, an example of evidence, and a concrete case in which the claim would fail.
4. **Sensitivity exercise:** Recalculate or re-evaluate the Northstar case after changing one major assumption. State the original and revised decision.
5. **Transfer exercise:** Apply **hypothesis and stopping rule** to a different engineering system (hospital scheduling, power distribution, or emergency communications). Describe what remains transferable and what does not.

### Explained response guide

- A strong answer separates the mission outcome from the internal activity metric, states which evidence is authoritative, and specifies an escalation or stop rule when evidence becomes stale.
- For the sensitivity exercise, **Learning interval: specify an experiment, threshold, maximum allowed exposure and decision branching for pass/ambiguous/fail.** The correct answer is conditional on the chosen assumption and its justification; there is no single universally preferred architecture or process.
- For the transfer exercise, preserve the causal logic but redefine the boundary, user population, constraints, measurements, and physical limitations. Copying Northstar numbers into another domain would be invalid.

## Step 03 integrated NS-ASE15.1 technical management laboratory: Week 05

### Learning intervals as reversible, hypothesis-driven commitments

A sprint promises a time box; a learning interval promises an evidence-bearing decision. NMS should specify the important uncertainty, experiment, predicted observation, invalidation threshold, configuration boundary, and who may change the plan. A deliberate interval ends in a **decision record**, not merely a demonstration of activity.

For example, the transformation hypothesis is: "Given two integration lanes, two assurance lanes, earlier supplier feedback and a 12-item admitted-WIP cap, more than 75% of the 48 synthetic changes will release within 100 business days, the mean latency among released items will not exceed 35 days, and no critical change will remain unresolved." This is a fictional **screening objective**, not an enterprise promise. The [controlled inputs](../case/controlled-inputs.json) and [scenario summary](../case/scenario-summary.csv) make all dimensions inspectable.

The assured-learning run meets this particular numerical screen with 47/48 released, a 31.87-day mean among releases, and zero unresolved critical changes. The flow-only policy releases 42/48 but has 36.57 mean days, missing the time threshold. A supplier disruption releases only 31/48 and leaves two critical changes unresolved. These are deterministic counterfactuals; they have no sampling confidence interval.

**Design an evidence roadmap:** interval 1 maps demand and baseline queues; interval 2 pilots a shared integration slot with immutable configuration capture; interval 3 tests an assurance automation while witnessing high-consequence cases; interval 4 runs a supplier-disruption drill and decides whether to scale, modify or stop. At every interval, state an irreversible commitment (e.g. procurement of a test rig) and which lower-cost information could defer it.

For probabilistic learning, Bayes' rule updates a defect belief based on a diagnostic result:

\[P(D\mid +)=\frac{P(+\mid D)P(D)}{P(+\mid D)P(D)+P(+\mid\neg D)P(\neg D)}.\]

With *assumed* prior defect probability 0.20, sensitivity 0.85 and specificity 0.90, the probability of a positive result is \(0.85(0.20)+0.10(0.80)=0.25\). The posterior defect probability after a positive is \(0.17/0.25=0.68\); after a negative it is \(0.03/0.75=0.04\). The [Python implementation](../tools/step03_book15_flow.py) reproduces these values. This is a lesson in conditional probabilities, not validated safety diagnostic performance.

**Exercise and response.** If the negative result is the only observation, may NMS claim the controller is safe? No. A 4% conditional defect probability is not proof of safety; the prior, failure population, test applicability, consequence and required independent assurance need justification. Repeat the Bayesian calculation at a prior 0.40 to see why a fixed test's interpretation changes with context.

**Failure boundary.** Do not translate these hypothetical posterior probabilities into physical defect rates without calibration and an appropriate sampling frame. A learning interval can improve knowledge while still ending in a **HOLD** decision pending physical verification.
