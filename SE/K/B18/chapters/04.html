The problem you are learning to solve

Imagine the regional mobility council meets after an emergency drill. Agencies individually meet many of their own targets, yet some residents still cannot reach essential care. A sponsor wants a decisive engineering answer and a single performance number. You are responsible for making the inquiry sufficiently precise to support action without pretending away divided responsibility, incomplete evidence or affected people.

This week's question is physical architecture, constituent allocations, interfaces, cots, and legacy. The key conceptual move is physical allocations expose the limits of logical designs. The decision is not merely whether an artifact can be drawn: it is whether the claims made by that artifact survive a realistic change in authority, timing, evidence or public consequences. We therefore work from a real decision question toward an explicit representation, then test where the representation breaks.

The central concept, built carefully

Allocate vehicle location, dispatch decisions, road priority and patient-intake status to actual agency systems. Record owner, software version, physical interface, update frequency and rollback mode rather than treating a box-and-arrow diagram as implementation.

A shuttle can seat eight passengers but only two wheelchairs; an allocation based on total seats can falsely satisfy an accessible-capacity requirement.

The engineering reasoning should move in a disciplined order. First name the observed or desired outcome, the boundary, the actors who have the right to act, and the time horizon. Next identify the mechanism hypothesized to link an intervention to that outcome. Then choose a representation—network, causal loop, interface contract, process model, probability table, capability map or deliberative inquiry—whose assumptions match the phenomenon. Finally specify evidence able to disconfirm the model. That last step matters because a persuasive representation can still be false, incomplete, or legitimate for only a narrow class of decisions.

Foundational distinctions from the course guide

  • Allocation is a decision with evidence, authority, capacity, and lifecycle consequences—not a line on a diagram.
  • Physical alternatives should vary performers, centralization, mediation, fallback, and governance, not just product brands.
  • COTS shifts engineering effort from design control to selection, insight, interface control, qualification, update management, data rights, and exit strategy.
  • Legacy constraints may be stable interfaces, undocumented behavior, safety certification, scarce expertise, or unsupported hardware.
  • Compatibility windows and graceful degradation are part of architecture when synchronized upgrades are impossible.

Graduate depth: constituent allocations and physical SoS feasibility

Logical mission threads must survive physical constraints

A system of systems (SoS) derives capabilities from independently managed constituent systems. A logical mission thread identifies what information and actions must occur; the physical architecture determines which constituents, networks, interfaces, and locations can realize those functions. This translation is not a one-to-one assignment. Multiple organizations may provide the same function in different regions, while one legacy constituent may provide several functions but only during specified operating hours. Physical allocation must respect ownership, capacity, timing, geography, security domains, and limitations of interfaces outside the central program's control.

Represent a mission thread as an ordered dependency graph. Nodes are functions or service outcomes and edges encode prerequisites, information exchanges, or resource dependencies. A physical allocation maps logical nodes to one or more constituents with actual capability envelopes. An edge survives implementation only if the participating constituents share an operationally valid interface: compatible data meaning, latency, authentication, availability, procedures, and authority. A diagram showing connectivity does not prove that an emergency handoff will succeed under degraded network operation.

Reliability depends on dependency structure

For independent serial functions that all must succeed, mission success probability under a simplified binary model is P(success)=prod_i p_i. This multiplication is not valid when constituents share power, networks, weather exposure, or management decisions. A backup path can provide little resilience if it depends on the same damaged communications hub. Minimal cut sets describe combinations of failures that defeat the mission, but their probabilities require a dependence model. Reliability numbers from constituent datasheets are insufficient without evidence about mission duty cycles, maintenance, interface compatibility, and common-cause conditions.

Legacy and commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) systems add practical constraints. Their APIs can be stable while organizational access rights are not; release cycles can be outside SoS governance; data schemas can change; contractual support may not cover emergency load. Build an interface contract that distinguishes what a constituent promises, what the SoS assumes, and what cannot be guaranteed. For example, a hospital information system may expose bed availability on demand but not guarantee the freshness needed for time-critical diversion decisions.

Example: a geographically distributed response network

In fictional Northstar emergency access, a mission thread may require incident detection, eligibility confirmation, vehicle assignment, dispatch acknowledgement, and passenger support. The physical architecture spans a regional dispatch hub, municipal mobility providers, telecom carriers, and clinical services. A fallback provider is not a valid redundancy claim if it receives no accessible-trip attributes or cannot accept the handoff within the mission time budget. Map each hop's data, authority, timing, loss behavior, and operational owner before calculating a whole-mission success rate.

Allocation trade studies should distinguish structural feasibility from governance feasibility. A centralized service may simplify coordination while creating a single point of institutional or technical failure; a federated arrangement may improve autonomy but require stronger interoperability standards and operational agreements. A defensible physical architecture records which assumptions depend on binding agreements, which depend on technical verification, and which remain unresolved. Emergent SoS performance is conditional on all three.

Work the result twice. On the second pass, replace one favorable condition with the adverse circumstance in the next section. Compute the difference when a valid model exists; when the problem is political or ethical rather than computational, produce an explicit comparison of consequences, affected groups, evidence and objections instead of inventing an objective function. Where this method stops working

Buying compatible products does not establish compatibility of revisions, operating procedures or contractual service levels.

A responsible engineer should separate model correctness (the math or logic follows from its premises), model credibility (the premises are supported for this use), and decision legitimacy (the process respects authority, rights and affected parties). Passing the first test does not guarantee the other two. In particular, neither a hypothetical exercise nor an attractive decision matrix certifies an operational regional service.

Reading the broader system

The three Phase 5 books offer complementary lenses. The systems-of-systems lens asks which independently managed constituents must cooperate and what technical evidence can travel between them. The enterprise lens asks how resources, incentives, governance and institutional history shape that cooperation. The critical-complexity lens asks whose interpretation dominates, which boundaries exclude harms and when no single technical objective deserves priority. Use all three when a proposed solution changes both technology and public obligations. Their explanations can disagree without one being automatically mistaken: they may be answering different questions.

Step 03 — NS-SOS18.1 regional emergency access engineering laboratory

Physical allocation, vendor interfaces, and legacy recovery

A physical SoS view translates a mission dependence into independently owned interfaces, endpoints and controllable assumptions. The interface contracts specify sender, receiver, revision, units, freshness, security and degraded behavior. The capacity label is trip slots in four hours, not trips per minute; silently mixing these units can produce a factor-of-60 error even if every packet is syntactically valid. A late COTS firmware upgrade can change field meanings or clock sources and invalidate prior distributed verification.

The status-quo physical baseline allocates 50 shuttle, 35 paratransit, 150 bus, 110 rail, 270 dispatch, 280 hospital and 70 accessible-intake slots. At normal conditions, \(C=\min(270,280)=270\); the hospital is not the normal bottleneck. In a flood, hospital availability is \(280(0.85)=238\) and dispatch is \(270(0.90)=243\), so the hospital becomes the shared bottleneck. The standard path shrinks to \(150(0.55)+110(0.50)=137.5\), a separate loss. The allocation report records both rather than attributing the failure to one vehicle type.

Interoperability testing must include component compatibility and end-to-end effects under realistic delays. Contract acceptance should include schema checks, range and unit constraints, timestamp and time-zone checks, signature verification, old/new version negotiation and documented fail-closed paths. Classify each change as backward compatible, a negotiated transition or a breaking change; do not treat all upgrades as interchangeable.

Calculated counterexample

Exercise. If flood hospital intake is raised from 238 to 280 slots without changing the bus/rail factors, is the standard target reached? Solution. The standard route capacity remains 137.5, below 186. A hospital capacity improvement alone cannot fix a mobility-path cut. Conversely, adding buses alone may leave the hospital or dispatch interface as the bottleneck. A physical architecture decision requires a cross-interface sensitivity calculation and explicit owner approval.
