## The problem you are learning to solve

Imagine the regional mobility council meets after an emergency drill. Agencies individually meet many of their own targets, yet some residents still cannot reach essential care. A sponsor wants a decisive engineering answer and a single performance number. You are responsible for making the inquiry sufficiently precise to support action without pretending away divided responsibility, incomplete evidence or affected people.

This week's question is **managing sos development, governance, acquisition, fielding, and evolution**. The key conceptual move is *governance is an executable constraint, not a chart*. The decision is not merely whether an artifact can be drawn: it is whether the claims made by that artifact survive a realistic change in authority, timing, evidence or public consequences. We therefore work from a real decision question toward an explicit representation, then test where the representation breaks.

## The central concept, built carefully

A regional coordinator funds an interoperable dispatch gateway while constituent agencies retain scheduling sovereignty. A change-control agreement must say who can approve an interface revision, who pays for regression testing, and what happens when a participant opts out.

Separate decision rights into propose, approve, implement, operate and veto; record a matrix by interface and agency. A unanimous rule can protect autonomy yet leave a time-critical change impossible.

The engineering reasoning should move in a disciplined order. First name the observed or desired outcome, the boundary, the actors who have the right to act, and the time horizon. Next identify the mechanism hypothesized to link an intervention to that outcome. Then choose a representation—network, causal loop, interface contract, process model, probability table, capability map or deliberative inquiry—whose assumptions match the phenomenon. Finally specify evidence able to disconfirm the model. That last step matters because a persuasive representation can still be false, incomplete, or legitimate for only a narrow class of decisions.

### Foundational distinctions from the course guide

* Authority must be modeled per decision: architecture standards, interface changes, data sharing, test access, operations, funding, and emergency control may have different owners.
* Governance defines how decisions are made and enforced; collaboration alone is not a control mechanism.
* SoS configuration management often controls agreements, interfaces, mission baselines, and evidence rather than every constituent internal baseline.
* Evolution plans need triggers, windows, compatibility policies, sunset rules, and fallback modes.
* Participation is uncertain. Treat withdrawal, delay, noncompliance, and strategic behavior as scenario variables.

### A system of systems is constrained by independent authority

An ordinary system architecture can assign duties to components through one design authority. A system of systems often cannot. Constituent organizations maintain their own missions, budgets, release schedules, and risk tolerances. Technical interoperability is therefore necessary but insufficient: a shared interface can fail operationally when the organizations disagree about incident priority or who may authorize a degraded mode.

SoS governance should clarify negotiated commitments, escalation procedures, and the consequences of withdrawal or incompatible change. It must tolerate constituent evolution rather than assume that the whole architecture can be frozen. This produces a central tension: the collective mission needs coordination, while constituent autonomy supplies flexibility and local legitimacy. The engineering task is to create contracts and feedback mechanisms that sustain mission outcomes despite that tension, not to pretend all participating systems are under a single owner.

## Graduate synthesis: governing constituent autonomy in evolving systems of systems

### A system of systems has negotiated rather than absolute control

Traditional system decomposition assumes an identifiable authority that can allocate requirements, resolve interface conflicts, and enforce configuration. A system of systems (SoS) often contains independently funded, operated and upgraded constituents. Their owners can rationally refuse an integration request that damages their own mission. Thus a central SoS plan is not a single hierarchical optimization: it is a coordination arrangement among partially aligned decision makers. Northstar's fleet, a hospital triage platform, an emergency authority, and a regional transit network may share an access mission without sharing budgets, release cycles or risk tolerance.

A useful formal abstraction treats each constituent `i` as choosing a feasible action `a_i` to pursue local utility `U_i(a_i, a_-i)`, where `a_-i` describes decisions made by others. An SoS authority may shape incentives, define exchange conditions or provide common infrastructure, but cannot assume every constituent maximizes the same objective. A stable agreement may resemble a negotiated equilibrium rather than a globally optimal design. This explains why an apparently inferior technical architecture may be operationally viable while an elegant centrally controlled architecture is impossible to field.

### Architecture includes rights, obligations, and fallback behavior

Interoperability has at least syntactic, semantic, behavioral and institutional dimensions. Identical data fields do not guarantee that two agencies interpret “available vehicle” the same way; and identical definitions do not guarantee one agency is authorized to disclose vehicle locations. An interface agreement should identify message schema and units, event timing, authentication, owner, privacy limits, failure semantics, and notice period for changes. The architecture must show how service degrades when a constituent withdraws without presuming that withdrawal can be forbidden.

For example, a shared trip service may rely on three components whose independent availability is each 0.98 over a time window. A naive series product gives `0.98^3 ≈ 0.941`, but independence may be false under shared power outages or regional emergencies. Even that calculation misses *organizational availability*: an agency may remain technically online while legally suspending data sharing. A better fault and dependency model distinguishes hardware failure, communications partition, policy veto, and scheduled maintenance. Each failure class requires a different response.

### Evolution is a compatibility problem over time

SoS evolution occurs at multiple speeds. A dispatch provider releases software weekly, an emergency communications standard changes annually, and a hospital's operating policy may change overnight. Represent the fielded baseline at time `t` as a vector `B(t) = (b_1(t),...,b_n(t))`, not a single immutable product revision. A compatibility rule `K(B(t), mission)` evaluates whether a particular combination remains safe and useful. A pairwise version matrix can expose incompatibilities, but it cannot certify all emergent multi-party behaviors; representative end-to-end scenarios and fault injection remain necessary.

Consider a constituent upgrade that changes location messages from seconds to milliseconds without changing the field name. The messages parse but dispatch may misplace vehicles by orders of magnitude. Interface tests should therefore validate units and operational meaning, not only schemas. A controlled *grace period* can support dual interpretation or translation, but a translator itself becomes a monitored and secured constituent.

### Acquisition and governance as engineering mechanisms

Sustainable SoS agreements specify authority to change interfaces, funding for common assurance, escalation paths, evidence sharing, and procedures for unplanned withdrawal. The SoS sponsor may pay a constituent for additional instrumentation that benefits the shared mission but not its local business case. That investment is justified by demonstrated mission benefit and evaluated against alternatives such as additional redundancy or a simpler fallback capability.

The principal limitation is political and epistemic: a federation cannot guarantee that all future owners will retain today's priorities. A credible fielding decision therefore states the minimum participating set, degraded configurations, mission performance under loss, and who may accept residual risk. SoS engineering succeeds when cooperation is possible *without pretending away autonomy*.

### Where this method stops working

A memorandum of understanding is not proof of sustained participation, funded maintenance or exercised emergency authority.

A responsible engineer should separate *model correctness* (the math or logic follows from its premises), *model credibility* (the premises are supported for this use), and *decision legitimacy* (the process respects authority, rights and affected parties). Passing the first test does not guarantee the other two. In particular, neither a hypothetical exercise nor an attractive decision matrix certifies an operational regional service.

## Reading the broader system

The three Phase 5 books offer complementary lenses. The systems-of-systems lens asks which independently managed constituents must cooperate and what technical evidence can travel between them. The enterprise lens asks how resources, incentives, governance and institutional history shape that cooperation. The critical-complexity lens asks whose interpretation dominates, which boundaries exclude harms and when no single technical objective deserves priority. Use all three when a proposed solution changes both technology and public obligations. Their explanations can disagree without one being automatically mistaken: they may be answering different questions.

## Step 03 — NS-SOS18.1 regional emergency access engineering laboratory

### Governance as a dynamic engineering constraint

The [authority registry](../case/authority-registry.csv) records six constituents, the decision owner of each, the interface, nominal change lead time and unresolved obligation. For a multiowner modification, the earliest credible release date is at least the longest mandatory lead time on the critical change path, not the fastest programmer's sprint. If rail requires 21 days, hospital 5 days and regional dispatch 3 days in **parallel**, a critical path cannot finish in fewer than 21 days; if interface validation must follow rail approval and requires 4 more days, the lower bound rises to 25 days. These are illustrative planning parameters, not observed agency performance.

Model an acquisition/fielding commitment as a contract with required parties, acceptance data, revision and revocation rights. An MoU or acquisition agreement is evidence of a negotiated responsibility only within its stated scope. A central requirements matrix cannot compel independent actors to operate beyond their license or service obligations. A useful governance state tuple is \(G=(owner,authority,consent,version,expiry,fallback)\). Do not set `authority=true` just because a member appears in an SoS diagram.

The [controlled case](../case/INDEX.html) sets the joint screening requirement at 95% weighted scenario **coverage**. A policy that passes the arithmetic but lacks an authorized hospital receiving commitment must remain unfielded. Governance therefore acts as a release gate, not as a nontechnical footnote to engineering calculations.

### Scheduling and negotiation exercise with answer

**Exercise.** Rail, dispatch and hospital changes have 21, 3 and 5 day lead times. Assume all can start today, but the cross-owner test takes 4 days *after* all three accept. What is the optimistic completion date in days? **Solution.** \(\max(21,3,5)+4=25\) days. If the hospital additionally refuses service during an incompatible schema cutover, this date is only a lower bound; the agreement itself is an unresolved dependency. Record a contingency such as a supervised voice fallback, then test its throughput, privacy and accessibility implications. An unsigned schedule is not an assurance claim.
