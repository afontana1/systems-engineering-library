Chapter 11 — Decision making, deployment, evolution, and portfolio roadmap

Book 18 · System of Systems Engineering · Week 11
Course alignment: Week 11 assessed guide · Hypothetical Lab 11 · Book contents

Reading contract: This chapter is instructional, not a substitute for the course guide or its required scholarly sources. Northstar Regional Emergency Access is a fictional case. Numbers are synthetic illustrations unless specifically identified otherwise.

The problem you are learning to solve

Imagine the regional mobility council meets after an emergency drill. Agencies individually meet many of their own targets, yet some residents still cannot reach essential care. A sponsor wants a decisive engineering answer and a single performance number. You are responsible for making the inquiry sufficiently precise to support action without pretending away divided responsibility, incomplete evidence or affected people.

This week's question is decision making, deployment, evolution, and portfolio roadmap. The key conceptual move is roadmaps are contingent agreements. The decision is not merely whether an artifact can be drawn: it is whether the claims made by that artifact survive a realistic change in authority, timing, evidence or public consequences. We therefore work from a real decision question toward an explicit representation, then test where the representation breaks.

The central concept, built carefully

Represent rollout options with cost, dependencies, reversibility, learning value and separate agency approvals. Prefer a staged pathway when a small pilot can falsify a critical assumption before irreversible infrastructure changes.

Use a constrained portfolio: option A costs 3 and yields 5 units of mission value; B costs 4 and yields 7; C costs 6 and yields 8, with a budget of 7. Enumerate feasible sets and check complementarity rather than greedily selecting the highest ratio.

The engineering reasoning should move in a disciplined order. First name the observed or desired outcome, the boundary, the actors who have the right to act, and the time horizon. Next identify the mechanism hypothesized to link an intervention to that outcome. Then choose a representation—network, causal loop, interface contract, process model, probability table, capability map or deliberative inquiry—whose assumptions match the phenomenon. Finally specify evidence able to disconfirm the model. That last step matters because a persuasive representation can still be false, incomplete, or legitimate for only a narrow class of decisions.

Foundational distinctions from the course guide

  • A feasible alternative includes authority, funding, agreements, workforce, transition, interfaces, evidence, and operations—not only technical performance.
  • Robustness asks whether an alternative remains acceptable across plausible futures, not whether its expected score is highest.
  • Real options preserve the ability to learn, defer, scale, switch, or abandon at a known cost.
  • Roadmaps should align mission increments, constituent upgrades, interface versions, tests, agreements, training, and decommissioning.
  • Decision records must state what would change the recommendation and who can make that change.

Portfolio and deployment decisions therefore need an evolutionary perspective. Redundancy is meaningful only when alternatives fail sufficiently independently and remain institutionally available during disruption. Interoperability gateways, staged migrations, and fallback procedures can preserve mission continuity while constituents change. The review question is not simply whether a proposed architecture works at one instant, but whether the governance and evidence processes can detect and adapt to consequential changes before they undermine the mission.

A system of systems does not have a single, stable configuration. Constituent upgrades, retirements, changed contracts, and emergent uses continuously alter the dependency graph. A design that is robust against one vehicle failure may become fragile when multiple agencies migrate to the same cloud provider or communications backbone. Local modernization can unintentionally create a common-mode vulnerability at the collective level.

Graduate depth: evolving a federated capability portfolio under uncertainty

A roadmap is a sequence of contingent choices

A systems-of-systems roadmap is more than an ordered set of upgrades. Each constituent can change independently, and the value of one intervention depends on which others are available. A centralized dispatch interface might improve coordination today but lock the federation into a supplier whose protocol becomes obsolete. A roadmap should preserve feasible alternatives at major decision points rather than treating a single forecast as certain. This connects SoS evolution to real options: a staged pilot can buy the ability to observe interoperability behavior before committing the entire network.

Let C_stage be the cost of a pilot and C_lock the cost of an irreversible deployment. The pilot is attractive when its expected value in avoiding a poor deployment or revealing a better option exceeds C_stage, adjusted for delay and other consequences. The calculation depends on credible probabilities or scenarios; it is not true that every additional pilot is worthwhile. Some information arrives too late to change an acquisition, and delayed deployment can have substantial mission cost. Record which decision the pilot can actually influence and the latest date when that information remains useful.

Dependency networks, interfaces, and operational authority

An SoS capability might require an agency data-sharing agreement, interface adapter, identity-management rule, training, operating procedure, and fallback path before it produces any mission benefit. The roadmap should capture logical precedence, organizational commitments, and lead-time uncertainty. A conventional critical path highlights schedule dependency, but a technical dependency graph also identifies failure propagation: if a shared identity service fails, what other services cease to function? A good plan marks single points of failure and places independent recovery capability where mission consequences warrant it.

Constituent autonomy creates configuration diversity. One agency may run schema revision V3 while another remains on V2. Backward compatibility is not merely syntactic: a field's meaning or legal use can change while its name remains the same. Version negotiation, capability discovery, and explicit degradation behavior permit the federation to evolve without assuming instantaneous uniform upgrades. The assurance case should distinguish a tested pair of versions from all theoretical combinations.

Decision-making under stress and changing conditions

Suppose Northstar can invest in regional routing software, independent backup communications, or receiving-site capacity. The ranking depends on the stressor: routing may dominate during ordinary peak demand, communications diversity during wildfire power loss, and receiving-site capacity during a large evacuation. Portfolio choice should consider the distribution of mission outcomes across scenarios, not only expected average throughput. Hard accessibility requirements and minimum continuity thresholds may eliminate options that look cost-effective on nominal demand alone.

Use decision gates to update the roadmap when evidence changes. Each gate records measured capability, unresolved risks, interface readiness, constituent acceptance, funding authority, and the condition for proceeding, redirecting, or stopping. Be explicit about responsibility for cross-constituent failures; federated governance otherwise encourages gaps in ownership. Stress exercises should include withdrawal of a constituent, incompatible updates, erroneous data, and conflicting operational priorities.

The most robust SoS roadmap is not one that predicts the future most precisely. It is one that maintains mission utility while allowing legitimate participants to adapt at different rates. To support that goal, preserve traceability from mission effect to constituent contribution, and maintain a current map of technical dependencies, institutional commitments, and fallback paths.

A reproducible reasoning exercise

Treat the next numerical example or decision matrix as a controlled thought experiment. Draw a table with columns input, meaning and unit, source or fictional assumption, transformation, result, and plausible failure. Populate that table from this scenario:

Use a constrained portfolio: option A costs 3 and yields 5 units of mission value; B costs 4 and yields 7; C costs 6 and yields 8, with a budget of 7. Enumerate feasible sets and check complementarity rather than greedily selecting the highest ratio.

Work the result twice. On the second pass, replace one favorable condition with the adverse circumstance in the next section. Compute the difference when a valid model exists; when the problem is political or ethical rather than computational, produce an explicit comparison of consequences, affected groups, evidence and objections instead of inventing an objective function. Remember that a mathematical result never establishes the validity of assumptions by itself.

Where this method stops working

Maximizing a single numerical score can hide whose rights are ignored, what agreements can expire, or which measurements are untrusted.

A responsible engineer should separate model correctness (the math or logic follows from its premises), model credibility (the premises are supported for this use), and decision legitimacy (the process respects authority, rights and affected parties). Passing the first test does not guarantee the other two. In particular, neither a hypothetical exercise nor an attractive decision matrix certifies an operational regional service.

Reading the broader system

The three Phase 5 books offer complementary lenses. The systems-of-systems lens asks which independently managed constituents must cooperate and what technical evidence can travel between them. The enterprise lens asks how resources, incentives, governance and institutional history shape that cooperation. The critical-complexity lens asks whose interpretation dominates, which boundaries exclude harms and when no single technical objective deserves priority. Use all three when a proposed solution changes both technology and public obligations. Their explanations can disagree without one being automatically mistaken: they may be answering different questions.

Self-check: work before revealing the lab response

  1. Define the model. What is the unit of analysis and why does it match this week's decision? Name a plausible alternative boundary.
  2. Trace the mechanism. Which chain of events, institutions or variables connects intervention to outcome? Name one missing link.
  3. Check the evidence. Which premise is most fragile? What independent observation would falsify it?
  4. Change the case. Suppose a partner withdraws funding for the highest-scoring option. Rework the roadmap and identify the decision owner. Which claims and actors must be reconsidered?
  5. Defend a bounded decision. What can be responsibly recommended today, to whom, with what review trigger and non-claims?

Explanations and answer criteria

A proficient answer identifies a bounded outcome, a model whose assumptions are explicit, a provenance chain, a credible alternative explanation, and a named legitimate decision maker. For Question 4, a single undifferentiated average or a redraw of the same diagram is insufficient: explain which causal or normative premise changed and how that affects the recommendation. For Question 5, separate evidence-backed statements from conditional hypotheses and unresolved stakeholder disputes. If your answer cannot state a stop rule or the consequence of error, revise it before proceeding.

Important failure warnings

  • Comparing technically attractive but governance-infeasible alternatives.
  • Hiding stakeholder conflict in a single unexplained weighted score.
  • No decision-reversal or participation sensitivity.
  • Roadmap without interfaces, evidence, agreements, resources, or retirement.
