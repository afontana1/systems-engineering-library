Chapter 07 — Bayesian networks, causality, and decision uncertainty

Book 18 · System of Systems Engineering · Week 7
Course alignment: Week 7 assessed guide · Hypothetical Lab 07 · Book contents

Reading contract: This chapter is instructional, not a substitute for the course guide or its required scholarly sources. Northstar Regional Emergency Access is a fictional case. Numbers are synthetic illustrations unless specifically identified otherwise.

The problem you are learning to solve

Imagine the regional mobility council meets after an emergency drill. Agencies individually meet many of their own targets, yet some residents still cannot reach essential care. A sponsor wants a decisive engineering answer and a single performance number. You are responsible for making the inquiry sufficiently precise to support action without pretending away divided responsibility, incomplete evidence or affected people.

This week's question is bayesian networks, causality, and decision uncertainty. The key conceptual move is bayesian updates are conditional on a model. The decision is not merely whether an artifact can be drawn: it is whether the claims made by that artifact survive a realistic change in authority, timing, evidence or public consequences. We therefore work from a real decision question toward an explicit representation, then test where the representation breaks.

The central concept, built carefully

Begin with a clearly defined hypothesis such as bridge passable now. Specify prior probability, likelihood of a positive sensor reading if passable, and likelihood if blocked. Update from the likelihood ratio rather than treating the sensor label as the posterior.

If prior passable probability is 0.7, sensitivity 0.9 and false-positive rate 0.2, a positive indication yields posterior 0.63/(0.63+0.06)=0.913. This is a teaching calculation under assumed calibration.

The engineering reasoning should move in a disciplined order. First name the observed or desired outcome, the boundary, the actors who have the right to act, and the time horizon. Next identify the mechanism hypothesized to link an intervention to that outcome. Then choose a representation—network, causal loop, interface contract, process model, probability table, capability map or deliberative inquiry—whose assumptions match the phenomenon. Finally specify evidence able to disconfirm the model. That last step matters because a persuasive representation can still be false, incomplete, or legitimate for only a narrow class of decisions.

Foundational distinctions from the course guide

  • A Bayesian network factorizes a joint distribution according to graph assumptions; arrows do not automatically prove causation.
  • Conditional independence is the source of computational structure and a major source of model error.
  • CPTs may come from data, expert elicitation, physics, simulation, or combinations; provenance and uncertainty matter.
  • Calibration asks whether events assigned a probability occur at that rate in comparable cases.
  • A decision requires utilities, losses, constraints, and authority in addition to posterior probability.

The mechanism beneath the method

Bayesian networks represent conditional relationships among uncertain states and observations for structured inference.

Distinguishing related ideas

A probabilistic edge is not automatically a causal mechanism; intervention decisions require stronger causal assumptions.

Interpretation and boundary conditions

Updating belief after a failed sensor depends on detection capability, prior rates and independence conditions, not only the observation itself.

Deeper theory: Bayesian networks represent conditional beliefs, not automatic causality

Factorization turns a joint uncertainty model into local claims

A Bayesian network is a directed acyclic graph in which each variable is associated with a conditional probability distribution given its parent variables. Under the network's conditional-independence assumptions, the joint distribution factors as P(X_1,...,X_n) = Π_i P(X_i | parents(X_i)). This makes a complex joint model more manageable and explicit, but the graph structure is an assumption requiring justification. If weather affects both road passability and emergency-call volume, a graph that treats those variables as independent can badly underestimate correlated system-wide stress.

The arrows in an ordinary Bayesian belief network do not automatically establish intervention causality. They may encode an efficient probabilistic factorization or expert judgments about information, not mechanisms that remain stable after policy changes. A controller can learn that staffing level predicts delay without proving that hiring more staff will reduce delay: severe incidents may drive both staffing and delay. Causal analysis requires temporal order, plausible mechanisms, confounder treatment, and a clearly defined intervention. Asking P(delay | staffing high) is not the same as asking what delay would be under a deliberate staffing policy.

Updating evidence has a denominator and a dependence condition

For hypothesis H and evidence E, Bayes' rule is P(H|E) = P(E|H)P(H)/P(E). Consider a hypothetical network outage with prior probability 0.1. If a warning indicator appears in 80% of outages but also 20% of nonoutages, the posterior outage probability after a warning is 0.08/(0.08+0.18) ≈ 0.308, not 80%. Sensitivity and specificity alone do not determine posterior risk; prevalence matters. This calculation is a teaching example, not an estimate of any Northstar infrastructure.

Evidence from two warning sources cannot simply be multiplied if they share telemetry, software, or failure causes. A model with assumed independence can transform duplicated evidence into extreme confidence. Conditional probability tables should reflect how information originates, and weakly observed relationships should be documented and varied. Where data are sparse, expert elicitation should specify precisely which conditionals are judged and examine whether the resulting probabilities are coherent. An apparently detailed network can be less credible than a simpler one whose central assumptions have actually been tested.

Diagnostic, predictive, and prescriptive uses differ

A diagnostic query infers likely causes from observed effects; a predictive query estimates outcomes under specified conditions; a prescriptive model evaluates actions and consequences. Bayesian networks are useful for the first two when conditional relationships are defensible. To recommend actions, connect the beliefs to decisions and utility, often through an influence diagram or a causal decision model. The most probable condition is not necessarily the one requiring the most urgent response. A low-probability bridge closure can dominate a safety decision if the consequence of dispatching vehicles into danger is severe.

Time-dependent systems also challenge static networks. Failure risk today depends on whether maintenance was performed, whether a warning persisted, and whether resources are depleted. Dynamic Bayesian networks can link states across intervals, but their transition assumptions, time resolution, and observation delays still matter. If agencies update at different rates, “latest” observations may represent different physical times. A decision model should preserve timestamps and distinguish absence of evidence from evidence of absence.

Validation should test decisions as well as probability fit

Probability calibration asks whether events assigned roughly 20% probability occur around 20% of the time in comparable observations. Discrimination asks whether higher risk scores distinguish cases; neither alone establishes that using those scores improves mission outcomes. Evaluate performance by scenario, population, and decision threshold. Conduct sensitivity analysis on uncertain priors and conditional tables, especially where a recommendation reverses. A Bayesian network is strongest as a transparent framework for conditional reasoning and evidence revision, not a certificate that every directed edge is causal or that its numerical output is policy-ready.

A reproducible reasoning exercise

Work the result twice. On the second pass, replace one favorable condition with the adverse circumstance in the next section. Compute the difference when a valid model exists; when the problem is political or ethical rather than computational, produce an explicit comparison of consequences, affected groups, evidence and objections instead of inventing an objective function. Remember that a mathematical result never establishes the validity of assumptions by itself.

Where this method stops working

A causal arrow, a statistical association and a data lineage link are different things. Repeated correlated alerts cannot be multiplied as independent evidence.

A responsible engineer should separate model correctness (the math or logic follows from its premises), model credibility (the premises are supported for this use), and decision legitimacy (the process respects authority, rights and affected parties). Passing the first test does not guarantee the other two. In particular, neither a hypothetical exercise nor an attractive decision matrix certifies an operational regional service.

Reading the broader system

The three Phase 5 books offer complementary lenses. The systems-of-systems lens asks which independently managed constituents must cooperate and what technical evidence can travel between them. The enterprise lens asks how resources, incentives, governance and institutional history shape that cooperation. The critical-complexity lens asks whose interpretation dominates, which boundaries exclude harms and when no single technical objective deserves priority. Use all three when a proposed solution changes both technology and public obligations. Their explanations can disagree without one being automatically mistaken: they may be answering different questions.

Self-check: work before revealing the lab response

  1. Define the model. What is the unit of analysis and why does it match this week's decision? Name a plausible alternative boundary.
  2. Trace the mechanism. Which chain of events, institutions or variables connects intervention to outcome? Name one missing link.
  3. Check the evidence. Which premise is most fragile? What independent observation would falsify it?
  4. Change the case. Suppose the same bridge camera feed is rebroadcast through three agencies. Calculate the incorrect triple-count result and explain the correct evidence treatment. Which claims and actors must be reconsidered?
  5. Defend a bounded decision. What can be responsibly recommended today, to whom, with what review trigger and non-claims?

Explanations and answer criteria

A proficient answer identifies a bounded outcome, a model whose assumptions are explicit, a provenance chain, a credible alternative explanation, and a named legitimate decision maker. For Question 4, a single undifferentiated average or a redraw of the same diagram is insufficient: explain which causal or normative premise changed and how that affects the recommendation. For Question 5, separate evidence-backed statements from conditional hypotheses and unresolved stakeholder disputes. If your answer cannot state a stop rule or the consequence of error, revise it before proceeding.

Important failure warnings

  • Using arrows as evidence of causation.
  • Invalid or undocumented CPTs.
  • Double counting correlated evidence.
  • Reporting posterior precision unsupported by data/model quality.
  • Making a safety-critical decision without losses, thresholds, and authority.
