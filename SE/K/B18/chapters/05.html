Chapter 05 — Information architecture and multidimensional interoperability

Book 18 · System of Systems Engineering · Week 5
Course alignment: Week 5 assessed guide · Hypothetical Lab 05 · Book contents

The problem you are learning to solve

Imagine the regional mobility council meets after an emergency drill. Agencies individually meet many of their own targets, yet some residents still cannot reach essential care. A sponsor wants a decisive engineering answer and a single performance number. You are responsible for making the inquiry sufficiently precise to support action without pretending away divided responsibility, incomplete evidence or affected people.

This week's question is information architecture and multidimensional interoperability. The key conceptual move is interoperability has multiple dimensions. The decision is not merely whether an artifact can be drawn: it is whether the claims made by that artifact survive a realistic change in authority, timing, evidence or public consequences. We therefore work from a real decision question toward an explicit representation, then test where the representation breaks.

The central concept, built carefully

Syntactic compatibility means two services parse the same fields; semantic compatibility means both interpret the fields the same way; pragmatic compatibility means operators use the data for the same action. A message saying READY may mean dispatched, at curb, or available for assignment.

Build an interface test matrix across transport, syntax, meaning, timing, security, policy and human workflow. Count only tested dimensions; a green network ping covers just one row.

The engineering reasoning should move in a disciplined order. First name the observed or desired outcome, the boundary, the actors who have the right to act, and the time horizon. Next identify the mechanism hypothesized to link an intervention to that outcome. Then choose a representation—network, causal loop, interface contract, process model, probability table, capability map or deliberative inquiry—whose assumptions match the phenomenon. Finally specify evidence able to disconfirm the model. That last step matters because a persuasive representation can still be false, incomplete, or legitimate for only a narrow class of decisions.

Foundational distinctions from the course guide

  • Information architecture begins with decisions and mission consequences, not a data catalog.
  • An exchange contract should define producer, consumer, concept, schema, units, identity, timestamp, latency, freshness, confidence, provenance, security, policy, error behavior, and versioning.
  • Authoritative does not mean perfect; it means the recognized source for a stated element and context.
  • Reconciliation must preserve disagreement and provenance when sources cannot be safely collapsed.
  • Interoperability monitoring is operational engineering: version drift, latency, missingness, and semantic exceptions require detection and ownership.

Follow the supplied worked case

A rail system reports “available capacity” as standing plus seated riders; a paratransit provider reports only safely serviceable wheelchair positions; a hospital interprets capacity as medically suitable transport. All use an integer field named capacity. Syntactic interoperability exists, but semantic mismatch could create unsafe evacuation assignments.

Interoperability analysis should therefore identify the operational decision enabled by each exchange. The interface contract needs data definitions, provenance, latency, freshness, failure behavior, and responsibility for reconciliation. More data is not always better: contradictory or obsolete observations can increase uncertainty when their quality is not represented. A dependable SoS information architecture is an agreement about meaning, authority, and time, not simply a network diagram.

Two systems can exchange data successfully while failing to cooperate. Syntax compatibility means messages can be parsed; semantic compatibility means fields represent the same concepts; temporal compatibility means information arrives in time to be useful; organizational compatibility means parties are authorized and prepared to act on it. A regional emergency service can receive a perfectly formatted vehicle-status message yet make the wrong decision if the “available” state has different definitions across agencies.

Interoperability has multiple dimensions of meaning

Deeper theory: interoperability is a property of a mission relationship

Systems of systems are composed of constituents that retain their own missions, owners, configurations, and incentives. Two constituents can each satisfy their own specifications and still fail when cooperating. The reason is that interoperability concerns the meaning and consequences of an interaction across boundaries. A data packet arriving correctly is only the beginning of the argument.

Five distinct layers of interoperability

At the physical layer, systems must be capable of connecting or exchanging signals. At the syntactic layer, they must agree on message structures and encodings. At the semantic layer, they must agree on what fields and events mean. At the temporal or behavioral layer, they must agree on ordering, freshness, and permissible reactions. At the organizational layer, they must have compatible authority and incentives to carry out the coordinated mission.

Consider regional emergency access. An ambulance dispatch system may send a request with a location and urgency code. A mobility coordinator may parse the message flawlessly while interpreting the urgency code differently. Even if the meaning agrees, the request may be stale by the time the receiving service acts. And even if the technical information is current, the receiving organization may not be authorized to dispatch a vehicle outside its contracted region. Each layer creates a separate possibility of mission failure.

Ontologies and the difference between labels and meanings

A shared data dictionary helps but does not guarantee semantic agreement. A field labeled “available vehicle” may mean not currently assigned, mechanically serviceable, accessible for a particular passenger, or deployable under local law. Those meanings cannot be made equivalent by using the same string name. An ontology or information model makes classes, properties, relations, and restrictions explicit so participants can reason about which translations preserve meaning.

A mapping between two constituent schemas must carry assumptions. For example, converting local road closures into a common status code may lose whether a route is closed to all vehicles or only heavy vehicles. If this distinction affects emergency access, a lossy translation is a mission hazard. Versioned mapping rules, validation examples, and documented unknown values may be more important than the sheer number of fields shared.

Trust and data provenance cross the boundary too

Suppose one agency receives a travel-time estimate from another. It needs to know when it was generated, which route and vehicle class it refers to, what model produced it, and whether it can be relied on for the requested decision. Provenance does not prove accuracy, but it makes accuracy claims inspectable. A signed message can establish the sender and integrity of bytes while still being out of date, miscalibrated, or interpreted outside its intended use. Security authenticity and engineering validity are complementary, not interchangeable.

In a loosely coupled system of systems, no single actor can assume continuous authority to change every participant. The interoperability architecture therefore requires governance contracts: version-change notice periods, backward-compatibility rules, error handling, fail-safe behavior, and explicit escalation paths. What happens when a participant disconnects is part of the intended interface behavior, not merely an exceptional implementation detail.

Mission threads reveal dependencies hidden by interface lists

An interface inventory may show all connections as green even when the mission is fragile. A mission thread traces a complete operational outcome through several participants: detect an emergency, authenticate the request, determine mobility capability, allocate a suitable vehicle, communicate pickup authority, and verify arrival. Each step has assumptions and deadlines. A route that is technically reachable might be unusable after a permission change; a vehicle might be allocated by two dispatch authorities; a successful message exchange may still leave the patient unserved.

Mission analysis should therefore examine not just whether messages are exchanged but whether end-to-end outcomes occur under representative conditions, including degraded constituents. This is stronger than testing each bilateral interface separately. It also illuminates a subtle limit: even a successful mission-thread demonstration establishes only the cases exercised, under the constituent baselines used. Organizational policy changes and software upgrades can invalidate the original result.

Interoperability is consequently best understood as a maintained agreement about shared behavior. It is technical, semantic, temporal, and institutional at once. Without those dimensions, the phrase “all systems are connected” says little about whether the system of systems can accomplish its mission.

Where this method stops working

A perfectly formatted XML or JSON message can still produce a dangerous decision if the status definition differs.

A responsible engineer should separate model correctness (the math or logic follows from its premises), model credibility (the premises are supported for this use), and decision legitimacy (the process respects authority, rights and affected parties). Passing the first test does not guarantee the other two. In particular, neither a hypothetical exercise nor an attractive decision matrix certifies an operational regional service.

Reading the broader system

The three Phase 5 books offer complementary lenses. The systems-of-systems lens asks which independently managed constituents must cooperate and what technical evidence can travel between them. The enterprise lens asks how resources, incentives, governance and institutional history shape that cooperation. The critical-complexity lens asks whose interpretation dominates, which boundaries exclude harms and when no single technical objective deserves priority. Use all three when a proposed solution changes both technology and public obligations. Their explanations can disagree without one being automatically mistaken: they may be answering different questions.

Step 03 — NS-SOS18.1 regional emergency access engineering laboratory

Interoperability is more than transport connectivity

Treat successful exchange as the conjunction \(E=E_{syntax}\land E_{semantic}\land E_{temporal}\land E_{behavior}\land E_{security}\land E_{policy}\land E_{organization}\). Failure in any dimension defeats a use-specific assurance claim even when messages arrive. In this teaching case, a valid trip-slot number must represent a four-hour capacity for a named region and class, carry its revision, have a trustworthy owner signature, be sufficiently fresh, and be acknowledged under an interagency agreement. The interface contract artifact states those rules; its untested assertions remain open.

The model evaluates a maximum information age of 5 minutes. Under the unmitigated communications event, the message age is 12 minutes and dispatch effectiveness is 0.66. Even if capacity had been numerically sufficient, \(12>5\) would independently fail the information screen. The data-federation policy stipulates age 2 minutes and dispatch factor .98 in this scenario; it passes the arithmetic only because that mitigation is assumed, not because its protocol has been implemented.

A protocol can also be compatible at the data dictionary but incompatible with a receiving workflow. For example, an available_slots response might mean firmly committed for the hospital and forecast spare capacity for the rail operator. Standardizing the field name without standardizing that semantics can create a dangerous false capacity sum. Keep data semantics, evidence provenance and legal disclosure scope in the interface baseline.

Freshness exercise with solution

Exercise. A dispatch feed was valid when sent at 08:00 but is read at 08:07. The permitted age is 5 minutes. Is it acceptable because the signature validates? Solution. The signature addresses origin/integrity, not freshness: \(7>5\), so the message is stale. The correct degraded behavior is to request fresh confirmation or use an explicitly authorized fallback, not to reset the timestamp locally. Document how a clock skew or a shared time service can make many otherwise independent messages stale simultaneously.
