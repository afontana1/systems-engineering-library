The problem you are learning to solve

Imagine the regional mobility council meets after an emergency drill. Agencies individually meet many of their own targets, yet some residents still cannot reach essential care. A sponsor wants a decisive engineering answer and a single performance number. You are responsible for making the inquiry sufficiently precise to support action without pretending away divided responsibility, incomplete evidence or affected people.

This week's question is cascading risk, cyber resilience, safety, and mission assurance. The key conceptual move is resilience depends on dependencies and adaptive behavior. The decision is not merely whether an artifact can be drawn: it is whether the claims made by that artifact survive a realistic change in authority, timing, evidence or public consequences. We therefore work from a real decision question toward an explicit representation, then test where the representation breaks.

The central concept, built carefully

Trace a cyber incident at a shared credential provider through dispatch, fare validation and hospital access control. Some systems may remain powered yet lose the ability to deliver the mission. Distinguish availability of components from survivability of the mission.

For two paths using one common authorization service, a naïve parallel reliability computation overstates resilience. Add the shared dependency explicitly and compare loss of mission with and without a manual dispatch fallback.

The engineering reasoning should move in a disciplined order. First name the observed or desired outcome, the boundary, the actors who have the right to act, and the time horizon. Next identify the mechanism hypothesized to link an intervention to that outcome. Then choose a representation—network, causal loop, interface contract, process model, probability table, capability map or deliberative inquiry—whose assumptions match the phenomenon. Finally specify evidence able to disconfirm the model. That last step matters because a persuasive representation can still be false, incomplete, or legitimate for only a narrow class of decisions.

Foundational distinctions from the course guide

  • Risk registers organized by constituent hide propagation. Model causal chains, shared dependencies, barriers, recovery resources, and mission effects.
  • Common cause can be technical, supplier, infrastructure, identity, policy, workforce, information, or environmental.
  • Resilience includes anticipate, withstand, recover, adapt, and learn; each needs measurable outcomes.
  • Graceful degradation must define preserved services, priority rules, communication, human authority, and unacceptable states.
  • Residual risk ownership must match actual authority and consequence—not whoever writes the report.

Deeper theory: cascading failure turns local reliability into a network problem

A system-of-systems mission can depend on transportation, communications, power, scheduling, human operations, and institutional authority. If those dependencies are treated as independent blocks in a series reliability diagram, an analyst may calculate an appealing but misleading mission success probability. Real disturbances often strike multiple constituents simultaneously, and one constituent's failure can change the workload and risk profile of others. Reliability becomes network- and state-dependent.

Independence is a hypothesis, not a default truth

For independent constituents with success probabilities \(p_1,\ldots,p_n\), the success probability of a strict series mission is \(\prod_i p_i\). This multiplication is justified only when the model's success definition is appropriate and the relevant failure events are independent. A regional power interruption can disable several communication and charging services at once. In that case, assuming independent failures overstates the benefit of apparent redundancy.

A simple shared-cause model separates the probability \(q\) of a common disruption from independent conditional component failures. Two redundant service nodes may each have conditional availability \(a\), but if both rely on the same unprotected site power, a simplified service availability is \((1-q)[1-(1-a)^2]\), not merely \(1-(1-a)^2\). The result is conditional on this specific fault model; it does not prove that a single parameter captures all relevant dependence. Its value is showing how shared dependencies can dominate local redundancy.

Failure propagation changes the operating state

Suppose one mobility agency loses several vehicles. Another agency accepts overflow demand. Its utilization rises, which increases waiting time and may accelerate maintenance demand. Longer delays can trigger emergency escalations that consume even more shared resources. The first agency's local failure changes the probability of failure in others. Static fault trees can represent some dependency structures, but dynamic models are often needed to capture overload, reallocation, recovery sequencing, and priority rules.

Network cut sets identify collections of nodes or links whose removal disconnects essential mission paths. A minimum cut of size one may reveal a single shared data gateway or authorization service that all otherwise redundant routes require. Yet graph connectivity alone is not operational success. A surviving path may lack capacity, permissions, or timely information. Mission assurance needs network reachability and functional adequacy under the affected scenario.

Resilience describes a trajectory, not a single percentage

Reliability often addresses whether a system performs a required function over a specified interval under stated conditions. Resilience concerns the system's ability to absorb disruption, sustain essential functions, adapt, and recover. A service that fails briefly but recovers safely may have lower conventional uninterrupted availability than one that masks failures while degrading dangerously. For a mission, one may need a time-dependent performance curve \(P(t)\) and a measure of lost mission value over a disruption interval, such as

\[L=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\big(P_{\mathrm{target}}(t)-P(t)\big)_+\,dt.\]

Here the positive part counts shortfalls; its practical units and importance depend on the chosen mission measure. Two systems with the same recovery time can have very different cumulative loss if one preserves critical patients' transport while the other serves low-priority trips first.

Organizational decisions participate in cascades

Independent authorities may prioritize their own stakeholders during disruption. A technically available rescue route may be withheld because its owner has a conflicting mission or no agreement for mutual aid. Conversely, explicit reciprocity agreements can turn otherwise separate capabilities into a recovery resource. These are governance dependencies that should be modeled alongside physical and digital interfaces, rather than being relegated to a generic “organizational risk” paragraph.

A credible systems-of-systems assurance argument therefore follows degraded mission threads. It identifies initiating hazards, shared dependencies, cross-constituent propagation mechanisms, capacity limits, recovery options, and the evidence supporting each scenario. The goal is not to assert that every failure can be prevented. It is to explain how the network will behave when disturbances violate the comfortable independence assumptions of local reliability calculations.

Where this method stops working

A smaller mean recovery time is not enough if the worst-affected users lose service for much longer.

A responsible engineer should separate model correctness (the math or logic follows from its premises), model credibility (the premises are supported for this use), and decision legitimacy (the process respects authority, rights and affected parties). Passing the first test does not guarantee the other two. In particular, neither a hypothetical exercise nor an attractive decision matrix certifies an operational regional service.

Reading the broader system

The three Phase 5 books offer complementary lenses. The systems-of-systems lens asks which independently managed constituents must cooperate and what technical evidence can travel between them. The enterprise lens asks how resources, incentives, governance and institutional history shape that cooperation. The critical-complexity lens asks whose interpretation dominates, which boundaries exclude harms and when no single technical objective deserves priority. Use all three when a proposed solution changes both technology and public obligations. Their explanations can disagree without one being automatically mistaken: they may be answering different questions.

Step 03 — NS-SOS18.1 regional emergency access engineering laboratory

Correlated hazards and mission assurance

Shared grid power, dispatch identity infrastructure and emergency information create common-cause failure paths. A product of independent constituent availabilities is inappropriate unless the independence conditions have evidence. Use conditional scenarios or an explicit common-cause node. In this case, the grid event jointly reduces shuttle to .60, bus to .80, rail to .70 and shared dispatch to .90 of normal, so two apparent route redundancies weaken together.

In an illustrative fault-tree calculation, if a shared event causes mission failure with probability \(p_c\), and independent residual failures each have probability \(p_a,p_b\), a top-event probability may be modeled as \(P(F)=p_c+(1-p_c)p_ap_b\), only if the conditional independence of residual events has been established. At \(p_c=.02,p_a=.10,p_b=.10\), the toy result is \(.02+.98(.01)=.0298\), not .01. It is not a measured frequency or safety claim. Cyber compromise, hospital overload, passenger exposure and equity harms need their own hazard analyses and owners.

Even the integrated policy passes its four weighted normal/grid/flood/comms scenarios yet fails the separately defined compound grid-and-flood screen. Its model gives accessible service only 46.75 trip slots, standard route capacity 90 and shared capacity 175.5 in that stress case, with 9-minute information age. Every route and data gate that matters should be visible in the assurance register, rather than concealed behind the 100% coverage figure.

Risk sensitivity exercise and worked answer

Exercise. What is the effect on the illustrative fault-tree top event if the common-cause term doubles from .02 to .04, holding the residual independent probabilities at .10? Solution. \(P(F)=.04+.96(.01)=.0496\), an increase of .0198 (1.98 percentage points). The result depends on the assumed event structure and probabilities. A good risk review asks which evidence supports the common event, how early warning works, who has acceptance authority, and whether recovery access for vulnerable populations remains available.
