## The problem you are learning to solve

Imagine the regional mobility council meets after an emergency drill. Agencies individually meet many of their own targets, yet some residents still cannot reach essential care. A sponsor wants a decisive engineering answer and a single performance number. You are responsible for making the inquiry sufficiently precise to support action without pretending away divided responsibility, incomplete evidence or affected people.

This week's question is **information fusion, provenance, confidence, and data quality**. The key conceptual move is *evidence fusion must preserve disagreement*. The decision is not merely whether an artifact can be drawn: it is whether the claims made by that artifact survive a realistic change in authority, timing, evidence or public consequences. We therefore work from a real decision question toward an explicit representation, then test where the representation breaks.

## The central concept, built carefully

Combine reports only after defining the measured event, timestamp, observation quality and dependence. Two dispatch screens copying the same database are one underlying source, not two independent confirmations. Preserve the raw events and transformation chain.

A sensor says road open at 10:00 and a crew reports blocked at 10:04. Represent age, source quality and observation scope before computing any confidence score; the newer report is not automatically the more accurate one.

The engineering reasoning should move in a disciplined order. First name the observed or desired outcome, the boundary, the actors who have the right to act, and the time horizon. Next identify the mechanism hypothesized to link an intervention to that outcome. Then choose a representation—network, causal loop, interface contract, process model, probability table, capability map or deliberative inquiry—whose assumptions match the phenomenon. Finally specify evidence able to disconfirm the model. That last step matters because a persuasive representation can still be false, incomplete, or legitimate for only a narrow class of decisions.

### Foundational distinctions from the course guide

* Fusion is a process tied to a decision; there is no universally best fused truth.
* Source dependence must be modeled. Two reports derived from the same upstream feed are not independent corroboration.
* Confidence should be decomposable into source quality, recency, method, consistency, and contextual validity.
* Human judgment can add context but also bias, delay, and nonrepeatability; preserve interventions and rationale.
* Abstention and escalation are valid outputs when evidence is inadequate.

## The mechanism beneath the method

Information fusion combines observations of differing provenance, quality, latency and possible dependence.

### Distinguishing related ideas

Aggregating more sensors does not necessarily increase confidence if they rely on the same upstream source or share systematic bias.

### Interpretation and boundary conditions

Fusion must preserve source identity and conditions so uncertainty is not accidentally counted as multiple independent pieces of evidence.

### Follow the supplied worked case

Traffic sensors, crowdsourced maps, police reports, and bus telemetry disagree about whether a flooded underpass is passable. The fusion design first associates reports to the same segment and time, records shared weather-source dependence, excludes a stale bus report, and returns “uncertain—do not route medically critical transport” rather than a forced binary answer.

## Deeper theory: information fusion must preserve provenance and dependence

### Two reports are not necessarily two independent observations

A system of systems often receives incident reports from dispatch, hospitals, transit agencies, and public communications. Multiple matching records may appear to corroborate one another even when they originate from a single call forwarded among systems. If a fusion engine treats these as independent measurements, it can become overconfident. Represent each observation with its source, time, measurement process, transformations, uncertainty, and known dependencies. Provenance is part of the evidence model, not metadata to discard after an aggregate has been computed.

Suppose two independent unbiased sensors measure a scalar state `x` with Gaussian errors of variances `σ_1²` and `σ_2²`. The minimum-variance linear fused estimate is `(x_1/σ_1² + x_2/σ_2²)/(1/σ_1²+1/σ_2²)`, with variance `1/(1/σ_1²+1/σ_2²)`. This attractive precision gain relies on known uncertainty and independence. If both sensors share calibration error or obtain information from the same upstream database, the covariance matrix is not diagonal. Generalized fusion then depends on inverse covariance weighting, and naïve averaging can dramatically understate uncertainty.

### Identity resolution is itself an uncertain inference

Two agencies may report “patient transfer delayed” with different timestamps and locations. Are they describing the same event, related events, or independent incidents? Entity resolution requires matching identities under uncertainty, including aliases, clock offsets, missing fields, and deliberate data redaction. Incorrectly merging separate events hides demand; failing to merge duplicates inflates demand. A fusion architecture should allow uncertain links and maintain reversible source records rather than replacing them irreversibly with one cleaned aggregate.

Temporal semantics are equally important. The event time, report-creation time, reception time, and analysis time may differ. A late-arriving observation may change the best current estimate of what happened yesterday without changing the operational state now. Systems that simply sort by message arrival can falsely infer sequence or causation. In emergency coordination, distinguish a stale but accurate historical observation from a fresh estimate of current capability. Data latency is an engineering quantity with direct consequences for action.

### Conflicting evidence can be more informative than agreement

Fusion should not silently average away systematic disagreement. If a transit operator reports a route open and a road agency reports the bridge closed, the conflict could arise from different geographic boundaries, update delays, or incompatible definitions of “open.” These are hypotheses to investigate, not statistical noise to suppress. The architecture should retain conflicting assertions with source attribution, confidence, and expiration conditions. A reasoned operational decision can then use a conservative fallback while the evidence is reconciled.

Confidence must be calibrated for the statement being made. A highly reliable sensor of vehicle location may still be weak evidence about passenger accessibility. A frequent source of false certainty is allowing high confidence in a *measurement* to transfer automatically to a more complex *inference*. Document the inference chain: raw observation, normalization, entity matching, contextual interpretation, inferred capability, and operational decision. Errors can compound, and some links may lack quantitative calibration.

### Fusion quality is assessed against mission consequences

An architecture that improves average location accuracy but increases late detection of inaccessible routes may reduce mission performance. Validate data fusion using representative misses, duplicates, synchronization failures, cyber deception, and cross-agency disagreement. Report error asymmetry: a false “road open” message can be more harmful than a temporary false closure. The fusion system should expose confidence and provenance at the level needed by a human or automated decision maker, with a clear policy for using contradictory or expired data. Systems-of-systems evidence is credible when its lineage and uncertainty remain inspectable across organizational boundaries.

### Where this method stops working

Averaging confidence percentages is not an epistemically justified probability model without calibration and dependence assumptions.

A responsible engineer should separate *model correctness* (the math or logic follows from its premises), *model credibility* (the premises are supported for this use), and *decision legitimacy* (the process respects authority, rights and affected parties). Passing the first test does not guarantee the other two. In particular, neither a hypothetical exercise nor an attractive decision matrix certifies an operational regional service.

## Reading the broader system

The three Phase 5 books offer complementary lenses. The systems-of-systems lens asks which independently managed constituents must cooperate and what technical evidence can travel between them. The enterprise lens asks how resources, incentives, governance and institutional history shape that cooperation. The critical-complexity lens asks whose interpretation dominates, which boundaries exclude harms and when no single technical objective deserves priority. Use all three when a proposed solution changes both technology and public obligations. Their explanations can disagree without one being automatically mistaken: they may be answering different questions.
