Chapter 01 — SoS definition, attributes, behavior, and receiving review

Book 18 · System of Systems Engineering · Week 1

Reading contract: This chapter is instructional, not a substitute for the course guide or its required scholarly sources. Northstar Regional Emergency Access is a fictional case. Numbers are synthetic illustrations unless specifically identified otherwise.

The problem you are learning to solve

Imagine the regional mobility council meets after an emergency drill. Agencies individually meet many of their own targets, yet some residents still cannot reach essential care. A sponsor wants a decisive engineering answer and a single performance number. You are responsible for making the inquiry sufficiently precise to support action without pretending away divided responsibility, incomplete evidence or affected people.

This week's question is sos definition, attributes, behavior, and receiving review. The key conceptual move is independence changes the meaning of architecture. The decision is not merely whether an artifact can be drawn: it is whether the claims made by that artifact survive a realistic change in authority, timing, evidence or public consequences. We therefore work from a real decision question toward an explicit representation, then test where the representation breaks.

The central concept, built carefully

A hospital coordinator can route a patient but cannot order a rail operator to cancel trains. Classify each constituent by its ordinary independent mission and by its emergency obligation; a box without an owner and fallback policy is an incomplete constituent definition.

Create a directed capability graph with nodes rail, paratransit, shuttles, EMS and hospital intake. Removing one edge changes whether a wheelchair-accessible origin can reach the hospital even when total vehicle count stays unchanged.

The engineering reasoning should move in a disciplined order. First name the observed or desired outcome, the boundary, the actors who have the right to act, and the time horizon. Next identify the mechanism hypothesized to link an intervention to that outcome. Then choose a representation—network, causal loop, interface contract, process model, probability table, capability map or deliberative inquiry—whose assumptions match the phenomenon. Finally specify evidence able to disconfirm the model. That last step matters because a persuasive representation can still be false, incomplete, or legitimate for only a narrow class of decisions.

Foundational distinctions from the course guide

  • A constituent is not simply a box in a decomposition; it has its own purpose, users, management, lifecycle, and ability to operate outside the SoS mission.
  • SoS classifications describe governance conditions, not maturity levels. A coalition may move among them by mission or period.
  • Emergent behavior can be beneficial, harmful, or ambiguous and may arise from interactions, timing, adaptation, policy, and human response.
  • Boundaries are decision-dependent. Include what must be considered to answer the decision, not everything that exists.
  • Network measures are prompts for investigation. They do not substitute for mission semantics, capacity, quality, authority, or failure behavior.

The mechanism beneath the method

A system of systems combines constituents with independent missions and authorities, creating behavior no one constituent fully controls.

Distinguishing related ideas

A large integrated system under one owner is not necessarily an SoS; operational and managerial independence alter what can be guaranteed.

Interpretation and boundary conditions

The mission depends on negotiating interfaces, information quality and incentives, not merely on a common hardware architecture.

During a hospital evacuation, however, coordinated routing, accessible capacity, traffic priority, and hospital intake status create a capability no constituent can deliver alone. The coalition is initially acknowledged: a regional coordinator has mission responsibility but limited authority over constituent budgets and designs.

Graduate depth: a system of systems is a network of partially independent decisions

Emergence from operational and managerial independence

A system of systems (SoS) is not simply a large engineered system. Its constituents often retain operational missions, management authority, funding, and evolutionary paths independent of the SoS mission. This autonomy changes what the architect can require and what the integrator can verify. A central design authority might mandate a software interface in a conventional system; a regional emergency-access SoS may need agreements, incentives, fallback protocols, and staged adoption because constituent agencies can decline or delay changes.

Emergent behavior arises from interactions among constituent capabilities, constraints, and independent decisions. It can be beneficial, such as coordinated rerouting, or harmful, such as cascading congestion when each agency optimizes its own dispatch. Emergence is not a license for vague explanation. State the interaction mechanism: how one agency's control action changes the inputs or feasible actions of another, with what information delay, and under which boundary conditions. The relevant system property may be unavailable from any one constituent's performance report.

Network structure and common-cause failure

Represent functional dependence as a directed graph with nodes for constituent capabilities and edges for necessary data, resources, or authority. Network topology affects resilience. A central exchange may improve interoperability while creating a single point of failure. Multiple paths can improve recovery only when their failure causes are sufficiently diverse. If all paths depend on the same electrical substation or governance approval, the apparent redundancy is misleading. Graph connectivity is necessary for many mission flows, but connectedness alone does not ensure capacity, latency, trust, or compatible meaning.

For a simple mission chain, the product of component success probabilities is valid only with suitable independence assumptions. When constituents share flood exposure or telecommunications infrastructure, correlated outages can dominate the joint outcome. Model those common causes explicitly or use stress scenarios that disable groups of related constituents. An SoS design decision should consider how mission performance degrades gracefully rather than asking only whether all components pass under nominal conditions.

Governance as part of the technical architecture

A constituent's willingness to share information affects the realized system as surely as network bandwidth does. Authority to change message schemas, rules for emergency override, liability for data use, and incentives to maintain interfaces belong in the architecture description. Otherwise the technical model silently assumes obedience that the real federation does not provide. Governance interfaces should identify decision owners, escalation paths, service expectations, and what happens when participants disagree or disconnect.

In the fictional Northstar region, each agency may achieve its own response target while the combined network fails to serve cross-border accessible requests. The SoS measure must capture completed end-to-end journeys, including transfer and receiving capacity. Local success is not equivalent to mission success. This is why SoS verification and validation demand both constituent evidence and integrated operational scenarios, especially those involving degraded communications and unplanned constituent changes.

SoS engineering therefore combines architecture, network science, economics, and institutional design. It can shape interfaces and incentives without assuming permanent central control. A credible receiving review names the system boundary, constituent independence, common-cause exposures, measurable emergent properties, and claims that cannot be verified until field integration occurs.

Where this method stops working

Network centrality does not measure access equity or legal authority; a hub with high centrality can have no mandate to share data.

A responsible engineer should separate model correctness (the math or logic follows from its premises), model credibility (the premises are supported for this use), and decision legitimacy (the process respects authority, rights and affected parties). Passing the first test does not guarantee the other two. In particular, neither a hypothetical exercise nor an attractive decision matrix certifies an operational regional service.

Reading the broader system

The three Phase 5 books offer complementary lenses. The systems-of-systems lens asks which independently managed constituents must cooperate and what technical evidence can travel between them. The enterprise lens asks how resources, incentives, governance and institutional history shape that cooperation. The critical-complexity lens asks whose interpretation dominates, which boundaries exclude harms and when no single technical objective deserves priority. Use all three when a proposed solution changes both technology and public obligations. Their explanations can disagree without one being automatically mistaken: they may be answering different questions.

Graduate synthesis: governing constituent autonomy in evolving systems of systems

A system of systems has negotiated rather than absolute control

Traditional system decomposition assumes an identifiable authority that can allocate requirements, resolve interface conflicts, and enforce configuration. A system of systems (SoS) often contains independently funded, operated and upgraded constituents. Their owners can rationally refuse an integration request that damages their own mission. Thus a central SoS plan is not a single hierarchical optimization: it is a coordination arrangement among partially aligned decision makers. Northstar's fleet, a hospital triage platform, an emergency authority, and a regional transit network may share an access mission without sharing budgets, release cycles or risk tolerance.

A useful formal abstraction treats each constituent i as choosing a feasible action a_i to pursue local utility U_i(a_i, a_-i), where a_-i describes decisions made by others. An SoS authority may shape incentives, define exchange conditions or provide common infrastructure, but cannot assume every constituent maximizes the same objective. A stable agreement may resemble a negotiated equilibrium rather than a globally optimal design. This explains why an apparently inferior technical architecture may be operationally viable while an elegant centrally controlled architecture is impossible to field.

Architecture includes rights, obligations, and fallback behavior

Interoperability has at least syntactic, semantic, behavioral and institutional dimensions. Identical data fields do not guarantee that two agencies interpret “available vehicle” the same way; and identical definitions do not guarantee one agency is authorized to disclose vehicle locations. An interface agreement should identify message schema and units, event timing, authentication, owner, privacy limits, failure semantics, and notice period for changes. The architecture must show how service degrades when a constituent withdraws without presuming that withdrawal can be forbidden.

For example, a shared trip service may rely on three components whose independent availability is each 0.98 over a time window. A naive series product gives 0.98^3 ≈ 0.941, but independence may be false under shared power outages or regional emergencies. Even that calculation misses organizational availability: an agency may remain technically online while legally suspending data sharing. A better fault and dependency model distinguishes hardware failure, communications partition, policy veto, and scheduled maintenance. Each failure class requires a different response.

Evolution is a compatibility problem over time

SoS evolution occurs at multiple speeds. A dispatch provider releases software weekly, an emergency communications standard changes annually, and a hospital's operating policy may change overnight. Represent the fielded baseline at time t as a vector B(t) = (b_1(t),...,b_n(t)), not a single immutable product revision. A compatibility rule K(B(t), mission) evaluates whether a particular combination remains safe and useful. A pairwise version matrix can expose incompatibilities, but it cannot certify all emergent multi-party behaviors; representative end-to-end scenarios and fault injection remain necessary.

Consider a constituent upgrade that changes location messages from seconds to milliseconds without changing the field name. The messages parse but dispatch may misplace vehicles by orders of magnitude. Interface tests should therefore validate units and operational meaning, not only schemas. A controlled grace period can support dual interpretation or translation, but a translator itself becomes a monitored and secured constituent.

Acquisition and governance as engineering mechanisms

Sustainable SoS agreements specify authority to change interfaces, funding for common assurance, escalation paths, evidence sharing, and procedures for unplanned withdrawal. The SoS sponsor may pay a constituent for additional instrumentation that benefits the shared mission but not its local business case. That investment is justified by demonstrated mission benefit and evaluated against alternatives such as additional redundancy or a simpler fallback capability.

The principal limitation is political and epistemic: a federation cannot guarantee that all future owners will retain today's priorities. A credible fielding decision therefore states the minimum participating set, degraded configurations, mission performance under loss, and who may accept residual risk. SoS engineering succeeds when cooperation is possible without pretending away autonomy.

Graduate depth: mission threads and capability dependency networks

A system of systems supplies outcomes through composition

A system-of-systems mission cannot usually be assigned to one component. The mission is delivered by a temporary or persistent composition of independently managed systems, organizations, networks, data services, and operational decisions. A capability such as evacuating an area depends on sensing, decision authority, communications, transport, power, and human cooperation. Because each constituent has its own lifecycle and incentives, functional allocation is not enough. Architecture must describe dependencies: which capability requires which others, under what timing, and with what acceptable substitutes.

A mission thread is an ordered or partially ordered scenario connecting triggers, actors, information exchanges, decisions, actions, and outcome measures. It is more useful when it includes degraded paths than when it assumes every constituent is available. Each step should record a required service, its provider, interface semantics, timing bound, evidence source, and fallback condition. A diagram that shows connectivity but not latency, compatibility, or authority may overstate operational interoperability.

Reliability is not simply the product of boxes

For independent components arranged in a strict series, mission success probability can be approximated by a product of component success probabilities. But independence is often implausible in an SoS. Two communications paths may share power, spectrum, authentication infrastructure, or a geographic hazard. A product model then exaggerates redundancy. Conversely, not every constituent failure destroys the mission: alternate routes, human workarounds, and graceful degradation can preserve partial capability. The architecture must express conditional dependencies and mission thresholds rather than assuming all components are either fully up or down.

An AND/OR dependency graph can reveal structural alternatives. An AND node requires several services simultaneously; an OR node permits substitutes. Real substitution often carries cost or quality loss: a manual dispatch workaround may restore basic movement but increase response time and operator workload. A dependency model should therefore specify performance states, not only binary existence. It can support scenario analysis that identifies critical shared services and candidate resilience investments.

Authority, information, and temporal compatibility

Constituent managers may agree to exchange data while retaining the right to change their systems independently. The SoS engineer cannot assume authority to freeze their designs. This makes interface contracts, negotiated change notification, interoperability tests, and transition compatibility architectural mechanisms, not paperwork. A sensor update that changes timestamp semantics can corrupt an otherwise functional cross-system fusion process. Likewise, a mission decision may arrive after its operational usefulness has expired even though the message was delivered correctly.

Version relationships matter. A mission thread verified with service versions A, B, and C does not necessarily remain valid after B is changed. Traceability should connect each mission-level claim to participating configuration baselines and the evidence used to establish the claim. Constituent test certificates may support local properties yet leave cross-system failure modes untested. This is the difference between verifying each part and demonstrating the emergent composition.

Interoperability has multiple dimensions of meaning

Deeper theory: interoperability is a property of a mission relationship

Systems of systems are composed of constituents that retain their own missions, owners, configurations, and incentives. Two constituents can each satisfy their own specifications and still fail when cooperating. The reason is that interoperability concerns the meaning and consequences of an interaction across boundaries. A data packet arriving correctly is only the beginning of the argument.

Five distinct layers of interoperability

At the physical layer, systems must be capable of connecting or exchanging signals. At the syntactic layer, they must agree on message structures and encodings. At the semantic layer, they must agree on what fields and events mean. At the temporal or behavioral layer, they must agree on ordering, freshness, and permissible reactions. At the organizational layer, they must have compatible authority and incentives to carry out the coordinated mission.

Consider regional emergency access. An ambulance dispatch system may send a request with a location and urgency code. A mobility coordinator may parse the message flawlessly while interpreting the urgency code differently. Even if the meaning agrees, the request may be stale by the time the receiving service acts. And even if the technical information is current, the receiving organization may not be authorized to dispatch a vehicle outside its contracted region. Each layer creates a separate possibility of mission failure.

Ontologies and the difference between labels and meanings

A shared data dictionary helps but does not guarantee semantic agreement. A field labeled “available vehicle” may mean not currently assigned, mechanically serviceable, accessible for a particular passenger, or deployable under local law. Those meanings cannot be made equivalent by using the same string name. An ontology or information model makes classes, properties, relations, and restrictions explicit so participants can reason about which translations preserve meaning.

A mapping between two constituent schemas must carry assumptions. For example, converting local road closures into a common status code may lose whether a route is closed to all vehicles or only heavy vehicles. If this distinction affects emergency access, a lossy translation is a mission hazard. Versioned mapping rules, validation examples, and documented unknown values may be more important than the sheer number of fields shared.

Trust and data provenance cross the boundary too

Suppose one agency receives a travel-time estimate from another. It needs to know when it was generated, which route and vehicle class it refers to, what model produced it, and whether it can be relied on for the requested decision. Provenance does not prove accuracy, but it makes accuracy claims inspectable. A signed message can establish the sender and integrity of bytes while still being out of date, miscalibrated, or interpreted outside its intended use. Security authenticity and engineering validity are complementary, not interchangeable.

In a loosely coupled system of systems, no single actor can assume continuous authority to change every participant. The interoperability architecture therefore requires governance contracts: version-change notice periods, backward-compatibility rules, error handling, fail-safe behavior, and explicit escalation paths. What happens when a participant disconnects is part of the intended interface behavior, not merely an exceptional implementation detail.

Mission threads reveal dependencies hidden by interface lists

An interface inventory may show all connections as green even when the mission is fragile. A mission thread traces a complete operational outcome through several participants: detect an emergency, authenticate the request, determine mobility capability, allocate a suitable vehicle, communicate pickup authority, and verify arrival. Each step has assumptions and deadlines. A route that is technically reachable might be unusable after a permission change; a vehicle might be allocated by two dispatch authorities; a successful message exchange may still leave the patient unserved.

Mission analysis should therefore examine not just whether messages are exchanged but whether end-to-end outcomes occur under representative conditions, including degraded constituents. This is stronger than testing each bilateral interface separately. It also illuminates a subtle limit: even a successful mission-thread demonstration establishes only the cases exercised, under the constituent baselines used. Organizational policy changes and software upgrades can invalidate the original result.

Interoperability is consequently best understood as a maintained agreement about shared behavior. It is technical, semantic, temporal, and institutional at once. Without those dimensions, the phrase “all systems are connected” says little about whether the system of systems can accomplish its mission.

Deeper theory: information fusion must preserve provenance and dependence

Two reports are not necessarily two independent observations

A system of systems often receives incident reports from dispatch, hospitals, transit agencies, and public communications. Multiple matching records may appear to corroborate one another even when they originate from a single call forwarded among systems. If a fusion engine treats these as independent measurements, it can become overconfident. Represent each observation with its source, time, measurement process, transformations, uncertainty, and known dependencies. Provenance is part of the evidence model, not metadata to discard after an aggregate has been computed.

Suppose two independent unbiased sensors measure a scalar state x with Gaussian errors of variances σ_1² and σ_2². The minimum-variance linear fused estimate is (x_1/σ_1² + x_2/σ_2²)/(1/σ_1²+1/σ_2²), with variance 1/(1/σ_1²+1/σ_2²). This attractive precision gain relies on known uncertainty and independence. If both sensors share calibration error or obtain information from the same upstream database, the covariance matrix is not diagonal. Generalized fusion then depends on inverse covariance weighting, and naïve averaging can dramatically understate uncertainty.

Identity resolution is itself an uncertain inference

Two agencies may report “patient transfer delayed” with different timestamps and locations. Are they describing the same event, related events, or independent incidents? Entity resolution requires matching identities under uncertainty, including aliases, clock offsets, missing fields, and deliberate data redaction. Incorrectly merging separate events hides demand; failing to merge duplicates inflates demand. A fusion architecture should allow uncertain links and maintain reversible source records rather than replacing them irreversibly with one cleaned aggregate.

Temporal semantics are equally important. The event time, report-creation time, reception time, and analysis time may differ. A late-arriving observation may change the best current estimate of what happened yesterday without changing the operational state now. Systems that simply sort by message arrival can falsely infer sequence or causation. In emergency coordination, distinguish a stale but accurate historical observation from a fresh estimate of current capability. Data latency is an engineering quantity with direct consequences for action.

Conflicting evidence can be more informative than agreement

Fusion should not silently average away systematic disagreement. If a transit operator reports a route open and a road agency reports the bridge closed, the conflict could arise from different geographic boundaries, update delays, or incompatible definitions of “open.” These are hypotheses to investigate, not statistical noise to suppress. The architecture should retain conflicting assertions with source attribution, confidence, and expiration conditions. A reasoned operational decision can then use a conservative fallback while the evidence is reconciled.

Confidence must be calibrated for the statement being made. A highly reliable sensor of vehicle location may still be weak evidence about passenger accessibility. A frequent source of false certainty is allowing high confidence in a measurement to transfer automatically to a more complex inference. Document the inference chain: raw observation, normalization, entity matching, contextual interpretation, inferred capability, and operational decision. Errors can compound, and some links may lack quantitative calibration.

Fusion quality is assessed against mission consequences

An architecture that improves average location accuracy but increases late detection of inaccessible routes may reduce mission performance. Validate data fusion using representative misses, duplicates, synchronization failures, cyber deception, and cross-agency disagreement. Report error asymmetry: a false “road open” message can be more harmful than a temporary false closure. The fusion system should expose confidence and provenance at the level needed by a human or automated decision maker, with a clear policy for using contradictory or expired data. Systems-of-systems evidence is credible when its lineage and uncertainty remain inspectable across organizational boundaries.

Deeper theory: Bayesian networks represent conditional beliefs, not automatic causality

Factorization turns a joint uncertainty model into local claims

A Bayesian network is a directed acyclic graph in which each variable is associated with a conditional probability distribution given its parent variables. Under the network's conditional-independence assumptions, the joint distribution factors as P(X_1,...,X_n) = Π_i P(X_i | parents(X_i)). This makes a complex joint model more manageable and explicit, but the graph structure is an assumption requiring justification. If weather affects both road passability and emergency-call volume, a graph that treats those variables as independent can badly underestimate correlated system-wide stress.

The arrows in an ordinary Bayesian belief network do not automatically establish intervention causality. They may encode an efficient probabilistic factorization or expert judgments about information, not mechanisms that remain stable after policy changes. A controller can learn that staffing level predicts delay without proving that hiring more staff will reduce delay: severe incidents may drive both staffing and delay. Causal analysis requires temporal order, plausible mechanisms, confounder treatment, and a clearly defined intervention. Asking P(delay | staffing high) is not the same as asking what delay would be under a deliberate staffing policy.

Updating evidence has a denominator and a dependence condition

For hypothesis H and evidence E, Bayes' rule is P(H|E) = P(E|H)P(H)/P(E). Consider a hypothetical network outage with prior probability 0.1. If a warning indicator appears in 80% of outages but also 20% of nonoutages, the posterior outage probability after a warning is 0.08/(0.08+0.18) ≈ 0.308, not 80%. Sensitivity and specificity alone do not determine posterior risk; prevalence matters. This calculation is a teaching example, not an estimate of any Northstar infrastructure.

Evidence from two warning sources cannot simply be multiplied if they share telemetry, software, or failure causes. A model with assumed independence can transform duplicated evidence into extreme confidence. Conditional probability tables should reflect how information originates, and weakly observed relationships should be documented and varied. Where data are sparse, expert elicitation should specify precisely which conditionals are judged and examine whether the resulting probabilities are coherent. An apparently detailed network can be less credible than a simpler one whose central assumptions have actually been tested.

Diagnostic, predictive, and prescriptive uses differ

A diagnostic query infers likely causes from observed effects; a predictive query estimates outcomes under specified conditions; a prescriptive model evaluates actions and consequences. Bayesian networks are useful for the first two when conditional relationships are defensible. To recommend actions, connect the beliefs to decisions and utility, often through an influence diagram or a causal decision model. The most probable condition is not necessarily the one requiring the most urgent response. A low-probability bridge closure can dominate a safety decision if the consequence of dispatching vehicles into danger is severe.

Time-dependent systems also challenge static networks. Failure risk today depends on whether maintenance was performed, whether a warning persisted, and whether resources are depleted. Dynamic Bayesian networks can link states across intervals, but their transition assumptions, time resolution, and observation delays still matter. If agencies update at different rates, “latest” observations may represent different physical times. A decision model should preserve timestamps and distinguish absence of evidence from evidence of absence.

Validation should test decisions as well as probability fit

Probability calibration asks whether events assigned roughly 20% probability occur around 20% of the time in comparable observations. Discrimination asks whether higher risk scores distinguish cases; neither alone establishes that using those scores improves mission outcomes. Evaluate performance by scenario, population, and decision threshold. Conduct sensitivity analysis on uncertain priors and conditional tables, especially where a recommendation reverses. A Bayesian network is strongest as a transparent framework for conditional reasoning and evidence revision, not a certificate that every directed edge is causal or that its numerical output is policy-ready.

Deeper theory: cascading failure turns local reliability into a network problem

A system-of-systems mission can depend on transportation, communications, power, scheduling, human operations, and institutional authority. If those dependencies are treated as independent blocks in a series reliability diagram, an analyst may calculate an appealing but misleading mission success probability. Real disturbances often strike multiple constituents simultaneously, and one constituent's failure can change the workload and risk profile of others. Reliability becomes network- and state-dependent.

Independence is a hypothesis, not a default truth

For independent constituents with success probabilities \(p_1,\ldots,p_n\), the success probability of a strict series mission is \(\prod_i p_i\). This multiplication is justified only when the model's success definition is appropriate and the relevant failure events are independent. A regional power interruption can disable several communication and charging services at once. In that case, assuming independent failures overstates the benefit of apparent redundancy.

A simple shared-cause model separates the probability \(q\) of a common disruption from independent conditional component failures. Two redundant service nodes may each have conditional availability \(a\), but if both rely on the same unprotected site power, a simplified service availability is \((1-q)[1-(1-a)^2]\), not merely \(1-(1-a)^2\). The result is conditional on this specific fault model; it does not prove that a single parameter captures all relevant dependence. Its value is showing how shared dependencies can dominate local redundancy.

Failure propagation changes the operating state

Suppose one mobility agency loses several vehicles. Another agency accepts overflow demand. Its utilization rises, which increases waiting time and may accelerate maintenance demand. Longer delays can trigger emergency escalations that consume even more shared resources. The first agency's local failure changes the probability of failure in others. Static fault trees can represent some dependency structures, but dynamic models are often needed to capture overload, reallocation, recovery sequencing, and priority rules.

Network cut sets identify collections of nodes or links whose removal disconnects essential mission paths. A minimum cut of size one may reveal a single shared data gateway or authorization service that all otherwise redundant routes require. Yet graph connectivity alone is not operational success. A surviving path may lack capacity, permissions, or timely information. Mission assurance needs network reachability and functional adequacy under the affected scenario.

Resilience describes a trajectory, not a single percentage

Reliability often addresses whether a system performs a required function over a specified interval under stated conditions. Resilience concerns the system's ability to absorb disruption, sustain essential functions, adapt, and recover. A service that fails briefly but recovers safely may have lower conventional uninterrupted availability than one that masks failures while degrading dangerously. For a mission, one may need a time-dependent performance curve \(P(t)\) and a measure of lost mission value over a disruption interval, such as

\[L=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\big(P_{\mathrm{target}}(t)-P(t)\big)_+\,dt.\]

Here the positive part counts shortfalls; its practical units and importance depend on the chosen mission measure. Two systems with the same recovery time can have very different cumulative loss if one preserves critical patients' transport while the other serves low-priority trips first.

Organizational decisions participate in cascades

Independent authorities may prioritize their own stakeholders during disruption. A technically available rescue route may be withheld because its owner has a conflicting mission or no agreement for mutual aid. Conversely, explicit reciprocity agreements can turn otherwise separate capabilities into a recovery resource. These are governance dependencies that should be modeled alongside physical and digital interfaces, rather than being relegated to a generic “organizational risk” paragraph.

A credible systems-of-systems assurance argument therefore follows degraded mission threads. It identifies initiating hazards, shared dependencies, cross-constituent propagation mechanisms, capacity limits, recovery options, and the evidence supporting each scenario. The goal is not to assert that every failure can be prevented. It is to explain how the network will behave when disturbances violate the comfortable independence assumptions of local reliability calculations.

Graduate depth: verifying emergent outcomes in distributed integration

A local subsystem's timing test supports part of a latency budget, but end-to-end latency includes queues, networks, clocks, human approvals, and retries. Contracts, versions, and the integration envelope

An integration contract should cover syntax, semantic meaning, units, timing, error behavior, security assumptions, and compatible versions. Tests must include negative cases: missing fields, stale timestamps, reordered messages, loss of a participant, partitioned networks, and conflicting updates. A distributed transaction rarely behaves like an instantaneous local procedure. Depending on architecture, constituents may temporarily disagree about the current state. Design should specify what inconsistency is tolerable, how conflicts are detected, and which actions must fail safe until reconciliation.

Constituent evolution creates a combinatorial challenge. If ten constituents each maintain several active versions, testing every combination may be impossible. The appropriate response is risk-based configuration coverage, not pretending the combinations do not exist. Prioritize changes to mission-critical services, shared interfaces, and common failure mechanisms. Use contract tests, simulation, emulation, SIL/HIL, field exercises, and operational observation as complementary evidence levels; state which claims each level can and cannot support.

Test credibility depends on the threats addressed, referent data, synchronization contracts, and documented extrapolation limits.

Observability is also a design constraint. To diagnose a failed cross-constituent mission thread, a team may need a common timeline, trace identifiers, message provenance, version records, and access to distributed logs. These cannot always be collected after the failure, especially when constituent organizations control their own systems. Agreements on minimum instrumentation and evidence sharing should precede major integration events. Otherwise the final report may say only that the mission failed, without an actionable explanation.

System-of-systems mission dependencies and distributed assurance

Graduate study for Book 18 — System of Systems Engineering. Companion to the existing course materials; the definitions, mathematics and decisions below are educational rather than operational approvals.

The governing question

How can every constituent system meet its local target while the joint mission fails?

Theory and conceptual structure

A system of systems (SoS) comprises independently managed constituent systems whose coordination produces mission capabilities that no single participant supplies alone. Interoperability is not merely a data-format problem. It requires shared operational semantics, timing assumptions, responsibility for failure recovery, authority for changes and a mechanism for resolving conflicting objectives.

Represent a mission as a dependency network with paths, gates and minimal cut sets. A minimal cut set is a smallest set of failures sufficient to disable a mission claim. It tells decision makers where redundancy, monitoring or governance agreements may be valuable. The representation must distinguish logical dependency from probabilistic independence. Constituent systems may share facilities, providers, time synchronization or a cyber identity system; naive multiplication of local availabilities overstates mission reliability.

Assurance must be distributed. A central integrator may have a mission-level requirement but cannot unilaterally redesign a hospital, emergency agency or transit operator. Verification evidence therefore includes constituent promises, interface contracts, test windows, change notifications and consequences when an external participant withdraws service. The confidence placed in an external assurance claim should be conditional on provenance and independent challenge.

SoS behavior is evolutionary. New participants change network topology, demand and incentives. An optimized interface arrangement can become brittle when governance changes. Scenarios should include late data, conflicting jurisdictional rules, service rationing and authority disputes—not just component outages.

Formal model and derivation

\[M=(A\land B)\lor C.\]
If path \(A\land B\) and backup \(C\) are independent, \(P(M)=1-(1-p_Ap_B)(1-p_C)\). If paths share a provider, independence is false; analyze shared-cause scenarios separately. Minimal cut sets for this structure include \(\{A,C\}\) and \(\{B,C\}\).

The formalism is useful only inside an explicit model contract: a defined system boundary, input ranges, configuration, assumptions, uncertainty sources, and an intended decision. Check both units and the semantics of the variables before executing the arithmetic. The result is a conditional engineering claim, not an unconditional guarantee.

Fully worked Northstar case

Suppose two jointly required constituents A and B each have availability 0.95, and an independent backup C has 0.80. The mission succeeds if both A and B work or C works. Under independence, P(M) = 1 − (1 − 0.9025)×(1 − 0.80) = 0.9805. Now suppose all three rely on one identity provider with a 3% shared outage probability, otherwise behaving independently. Total availability becomes approximately 0.97×0.9805 = 0.9511. The original 98.05% claim hid a shared dependency that no local participant owned as a mission risk.

Counterexample and critical review

Counterexample: Two agencies agree to use the same JSON message field priority, but one interprets 1 as urgent and the other interprets 1 as routine. Syntactic interoperability holds, operational semantics fail. Contract testing requires controlled examples and end-to-end mission observation.

An independent reviewer should be able to reproduce the favorable result and find at least one material scenario in which it changes. A sensitivity study that does not touch an important assumption can be technically reproducible but decision-irrelevant. Record the source of every number, identify the accountable acceptance authority, and state what the evidence cannot establish.
