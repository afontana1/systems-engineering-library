Chapter 06 — Process capability assessment, root causes, and midcourse exam

Book 17: Systems Engineering Process Improvement · Course EN.645.783 · Week 6
Companion to: Original weekly course guide · Hypothetical laboratory · Book contents

Teaching purpose. The guide defines the assessed engineering deliverable. This chapter builds the underlying mental model and practices reasoning before the lab. All Northstar program details and calculations below are fictional teaching cases, not operational claims.

The engineering situation

The current process has documented procedures and many review controls, yet performance is unstable and evidence escapes occur. The essential question is: What can the process reliably achieve, why does it behave this way, and which findings are strong enough to justify redesign?

The central idea of this chapter is use root-cause hypotheses, not blame stories. Before a method is selected, the engineer must distinguish the decision being made, what evidence could falsify the leading approach, and which obligations cannot be relaxed. In the Northstar case, engineers, operators, safety leads and suppliers are accountable for different portions of one mission result. A locally successful artifact is not sufficient if the complete outcome is unsupported.

Explanation: why the method works

A fishbone or five-whys session generates hypotheses; it does not identify causes by itself. Triangulate process maps, event logs, interviews, defect records and direct observation. Assess process capability relative to explicitly stated requirements while separating special causes from ordinary variation.

A useful way to understand the mechanism is to follow the causal chain explicitly. Start with a stimulus or observed condition; ask which model connects it to the engineering outcome; identify the assumptions that let the model predict anything; and define the next decision. Avoid confusing a convenient dashboard measure with the actual mission variable. Where constraints are hard—such as safety authorization, configuration compatibility or accessibility—treat them as eligibility conditions before computing preferences.

Deepening the reasoning — Root-cause triangulation. A five-whys chain is a possible causal explanation; look for disconfirming cases, workload severity, rework loops and interface defects. Root cause can be multi-factorial and evolving.

Connect the theory to the course vocabulary

  • Assessment should answer a decision question and use defined criteria, sampling, evidence, and limitations.
  • Capability ratings summarize process attributes; they do not explain every performance result.
  • Root-cause analysis is hypothesis generation and testing, not a ritual that guarantees one “root.”
  • Causal loops reveal reinforcing behavior such as backlog → schedule pressure → bypass → rework → backlog.
  • Findings should state condition, criterion, evidence, consequence, confidence, and recommended next analysis.

The statements above originate in the assigned course guide; use them as technical claims to test against a specific scenario, not as terminology to memorize. For each claim, ask what observation could show that it is false or inapplicable in the current design. The same discipline distinguishes an engineering argument from a procedural assertion.

The mechanism beneath the method

Root-cause analysis should discriminate among mechanisms rather than merely attach a familiar label to variation.

Distinguishing related ideas

A correlation between late reviews and delays does not show whether reviews cause delay or reveal upstream defects.

Interpretation and boundary conditions

Use process traces, temporal ordering and controlled changes to test causal hypotheses.

Graduate depth: capability assessment, causal diagnosis, and regression to the mean

Capability is defined relative to a stable measurement process

Process capability compares the behavior of a process to a stated requirement or tolerance. It is not the same as process control: a process can be statistically stable yet chronically miss customer needs, or can temporarily meet a target while changing unpredictably. For a stable approximately normal process with specification limits LSL and USL, indices such as Cp=(USL-LSL)/(6 sigma) and Cpk=min[(USL-mu)/(3 sigma),(mu-LSL)/(3 sigma)] summarize spread and centering. They are conditional descriptors. They are not meaningful for arbitrary categorical metrics, severely nonnormal outputs, censored observations, or mixed populations without careful treatment.

Engineering process measurements often have more complicated distributions than manufactured dimensions. Review lead time is nonnegative and right-skewed; escaped-defect counts depend on opportunities and detection effort; compliance percentages can conceal different case severities. Use control charts and stratification suited to the data-generating process, and verify that the measurement system itself is trustworthy. A change in ticket classification or a new test harness can change the reported defect rate without changing underlying quality.

Distinguish association from a mechanism of improvement

A root-cause workshop may find that late integration defects occur in teams with low automated-test coverage. This correlation does not prove that coverage caused the delays. Highly coupled subsystems may both resist automated testing and produce more late defects. A causal hypothesis should identify the pathway, confounding variables, and predicted observations. Directed causal sketches, process traces, and limited experiments can improve reasoning; a polished fishbone diagram cannot establish causality on its own.

Regression to the mean is another risk. An unusually poor month often improves even without intervention because extreme observed values contain random variation. If a pilot is launched immediately after the worst month and the next month is compared only to that extreme, the apparent gain may be overstated. Use a pre-specified baseline window, appropriate comparison group where feasible, and time-series evidence. Record simultaneous policy, staffing, and tool changes, since the organizational environment rarely remains static.

Countermeasure selection is a portfolio decision

Suppose Northstar's interface-review cycle time is averaging 18 days. Interviews suggest ambiguous ownership, while timestamps reveal most waiting occurs before an independent safety reviewer receives a complete evidence package. Proposed countermeasures include revising templates, expanding reviewer capacity, and clarifying review thresholds. The first might reduce rework, the second shortens a capacity queue, and the third changes which cases need review. Their effects can interact. A pilot should therefore specify the mechanism expected to change, leading and lagging measures, and guardrails against weakening independent assurance.

A successful capability assessment yields a defensible explanation of where variation originates and which parts are controllable, not merely a maturity label. Prioritize actions by plausible causal effect, implementation risk, ethical consequences, and measurement quality. Reassess the process after it changes; capability estimated under the old work mix cannot automatically be inherited by the new one.

Worked Northstar engineering case

The initial cause statement blames slow reviewers. Data shows reviewer touch time is modest; the dominant delay is waiting for complete impact evidence. A causal analysis finds unclear evidence requirements, fragmented repositories, late supplier input, batch board cadence, and incentives to submit early. The improvement target shifts from reviewer utilization to evidence readiness and flow.

The apparent “slow reviewer” has a queue of incomplete change packages. Measure input quality and arrival rate before adding reviewers.

Work the example in two passes. First compute or trace the nominal outcome, naming the unit of analysis, released configuration and decision threshold. Second change one assumption, such as a degraded interface, heavier demand, invalid provenance or altered stakeholder constraint. If the recommendation changes, identify the threshold at which it changes; if it does not, explain the margin of robustness. A conclusion without its assumptions is not a reusable engineering result.

A systematic check before accepting the result

  1. Boundary: What part of the program, process or mission is represented, and what has been excluded?
  2. Evidence: Which observations, versions, or tests support the premises, and what remains hypothetical?
  3. Causality: Could the proposed mechanism be confused with a proxy, confounding condition or common dependency?
  4. Decision: Which alternatives are still feasible, who has authority, and what would force reconsideration?
  5. Assurance: Which safety, security, accessibility or quality guardrail cannot be traded for speed or convenience?

Counterexample and failure boundary

Correlation between role assignment and delay can arise from hard cases being assigned to experts.

Build two competing causes for late reviews and identify an observation that would separate them. Notice that a mathematically consistent answer can still be an inappropriate decision if the intended use or validity assumptions have changed. In professional work, a responsible engineer records that limitation alongside the nominal result.

Practise the explanation, not just the procedure

  1. Concept check: Construct a bounded capability and maturity assessment without claiming formal appraisal status. Explain: Give a boundary, an example of evidence, and a concrete case in which the claim would fail.
  2. Concept check: Rate evidence quality and confidence for process findings. Explain: Give a boundary, an example of evidence, and a concrete case in which the claim would fail.
  3. Concept check: Use Pareto, cause-and-effect, 5-why, fault-tree, and causal-loop reasoning appropriately. Explain: Give a boundary, an example of evidence, and a concrete case in which the claim would fail.
  4. Sensitivity exercise: Recalculate or re-evaluate the Northstar case after changing one major assumption. State the original and revised decision.
  5. Transfer exercise: Apply root-cause triangulation to a different engineering system (hospital scheduling, power distribution, or emergency communications). Describe what remains transferable and what does not.

Explained response guide

  • A strong answer separates the mission outcome from the internal activity metric, states which evidence is authoritative, and specifies an escalation or stop rule when evidence becomes stale.
  • For the sensitivity exercise, Build two competing causes for late reviews and identify an observation that would separate them. The correct answer is conditional on the chosen assumption and its justification; there is no single universally preferred architecture or process.
  • For the transfer exercise, preserve the causal logic but redefine the boundary, user population, constraints, measurements, and physical limitations. Copying Northstar numbers into another domain would be invalid.
