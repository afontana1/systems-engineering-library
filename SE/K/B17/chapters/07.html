Chapter 07 — Process modeling and simulation for improvement decisions

Book 17: Systems Engineering Process Improvement · Course EN.645.783 · Week 7
Companion to: Original weekly course guide · Hypothetical laboratory · Book contents

Teaching purpose. The guide defines the assessed engineering deliverable. This chapter builds the underlying mental model and practices reasoning before the lab. All Northstar program details and calculations below are fictional teaching cases, not operational claims.

The engineering situation

The team proposes more reviewers, continuous review, automated evidence checks, and smaller change batches. The essential question is: Which interventions should be tested in a model, and is the model credible enough for that decision?

The central idea of this chapter is simulations are experiments on assumptions. Before a method is selected, the engineer must distinguish the decision being made, what evidence could falsify the leading approach, and which obligations cannot be relaxed. In the Northstar case, engineers, operators, safety leads and suppliers are accountable for different portions of one mission result. A locally successful artifact is not sufficient if the complete outcome is unsupported.

Explanation: why the method works

A queue or event simulation lets the organization test WIP limits, extra reviewers and batching without disrupting production. Calibration and validation require comparing output distributions with observed case data, not just matching the average. Track utilization, service-time variability and feedback-induced demand.

A useful way to understand the mechanism is to follow the causal chain explicitly. Start with a stimulus or observed condition; ask which model connects it to the engineering outcome; identify the assumptions that let the model predict anything; and define the next decision. Avoid confusing a convenient dashboard measure with the actual mission variable. Where constraints are hard—such as safety authorization, configuration compatibility or accessibility—treat them as eligibility conditions before computing preferences.

Deepening the reasoning — Simulation credibility. Process simulations require input rates, service-time distributions, routing rules and rework. Compare simulated lead-time distributions with observed logs before trusting intervention forecasts.

Connect the theory to the course vocabulary

  • The model should be no more complex than required for the stated improvement decision.
  • Queues, priorities, calendars, batching, resource skills, rework, blocked states, and evidence dependencies often dominate engineering-process behavior.
  • Historical fit is not proof that an intervention forecast is valid.
  • Verification includes code review, event traces, conservation checks, extreme conditions, and deterministic test cases.
  • Validation includes stakeholder face validity, distribution/behavior comparison, holdout cases, and limitation statements.

The statements above originate in the assigned course guide; use them as technical claims to test against a specific scenario, not as terminology to memorize. For each claim, ask what observation could show that it is false or inapplicable in the current design. The same discipline distinguishes an engineering argument from a procedural assertion.

The mechanism beneath the method

Process simulation connects arrival patterns, resources, decision rules and rework to predicted flow outcomes.

Distinguishing related ideas

A capacity increase at a non-bottleneck step may leave end-to-end delay unchanged or even increase downstream queues.

Interpretation and boundary conditions

Validate the model against observed distributions and behavior under stress before using it to choose a redesign.

Graduate depth: process simulation, causal policies, and experimental validity

Represent both flow and behavioral response

Process simulation can test policies before implementation, but it must distinguish the rules imposed by management from the reactions of the people doing the work. A discrete-event model represents item arrivals, queues, resource assignments, reviews, exceptions, and rework; a system-dynamics representation can capture slower feedback such as staffing, learning, trust, and pressure to bypass controls. Using only event logic may miss the way reviewers adapt priorities, while using only aggregate feedback can miss queue discipline and individual priority classes. The choice of method depends on the question, not tool preference.

For a process step, an observed cycle time often combines work time, waiting, and rework. A model calibrated to overall cycle times can fit historical data while allocating time to the wrong mechanism. For example, long approval delays could arise from scarce approvers, batch review scheduling, or repeatedly incomplete submissions. These generate different predictions when staffing changes. Calibration should therefore use mechanism-level observations where feasible, and the model should be challenged against data not used to tune it.

Queue discipline and intervention effects

A proposed priority rule may lower mean response time for urgent changes while worsening the tail for routine changes. With non-preemptive priorities, a high-priority request may still wait for a review already in progress; with preemption, urgent work may interrupt a lower-priority review and create context-switching losses. The distinction belongs in the model. Average resource utilization alone cannot establish acceptable performance because the delay distribution depends on arrival variability, service variability, number of parallel servers, and scheduling policy.

An improvement scenario should specify its mechanism. 'Add an automated checker' might reduce repeated manual review, but it may also increase false-positive flags that require specialist resolution. Model the false-positive rate and review burden rather than assuming that automation replaces all manual work. If the tool changes submission behavior, treat that as a plausible adaptation to be investigated. The simulation's boundary determines which indirect effects are visible.

Design, validation, and decision risk

Use paired demand streams for policy comparisons when that pairing is coherent, and replicate each candidate to estimate variability. Random events must be separated from uncertain structural parameters. A common error is to run thousands of replications with one fixed, poorly estimated service-time distribution and report narrow intervals. Those intervals quantify only simulation noise conditional on the chosen distribution, not uncertainty about how the real process operates.

Historical validation is necessary but incomplete. A policy that changes incentives may invalidate the behavioral patterns used for calibration. Thus the credibility claim should name the allowed degree of behavioral change and state what will be monitored after introduction. A design that looks best in a static process model may be fragile when work demand responds to the new policy. Robustness checks can vary arrival rates, staff absence, rework probability, exception volume, and reporting practices together when plausible dependencies exist.

Northstar controlled-review comparison

Suppose Northstar considers three alternatives for its technical-change board: daily synchronous review, asynchronous authorization with risk-based routing, and adding one specialist reviewer. A simulator estimates lead-time distributions by change class. But the preferred alternative must also satisfy evidence integrity and authority constraints. An asynchronous approval route that is fast but loses traceability is ineligible, even if it dominates on average lead time.

Use the model to forecast not just mean time but backlog, high-percentile delay, rework, and safety-review escapes, then pilot with independent monitoring of the same outcomes. If observed performance diverges, diagnose whether assumptions about arrivals, queue discipline, staff response, or data collection were wrong. Process simulation provides a disciplined counterfactual under specified behavior; real improvement must be evaluated against measured outcomes and accountable constraints.

Worked Northstar engineering case

A spreadsheet capacity ratio predicts that adding one reviewer will cut lead time by 25%. The DES model shows little benefit because cases wait for incomplete supplier and test evidence, not reviewer capacity. An automated evidence-readiness check plus smaller review batches performs better, but benefits depend on adoption and false-positive rates.

A two-reviewer queue receives eight cases/week and processes ten/week in total. Stability is possible, but heavy-tailed complexity can still produce long p90 lead times; run replications and report uncertainty.

Work the example in two passes. First compute or trace the nominal outcome, naming the unit of analysis, released configuration and decision threshold. Second change one assumption, such as a degraded interface, heavier demand, invalid provenance or altered stakeholder constraint. If the recommendation changes, identify the threshold at which it changes; if it does not, explain the margin of robustness. A conclusion without its assumptions is not a reusable engineering result.

A systematic check before accepting the result

  1. Boundary: What part of the program, process or mission is represented, and what has been excluded?
  2. Evidence: Which observations, versions, or tests support the premises, and what remains hypothetical?
  3. Causality: Could the proposed mechanism be confused with a proxy, confounding condition or common dependency?
  4. Decision: Which alternatives are still feasible, who has authority, and what would force reconsideration?
  5. Assurance: Which safety, security, accessibility or quality guardrail cannot be traded for speed or convenience?

Counterexample and failure boundary

A simulated efficiency improvement can fail if the model omits rework and reassignment loops.

Model a two-reviewer queue with demand uncertainty and assess both mean and upper-tail lead time. Notice that a mathematically consistent answer can still be an inappropriate decision if the intended use or validity assumptions have changed. In professional work, a responsible engineer records that limitation alongside the nominal result.

Practise the explanation, not just the procedure

  1. Concept check: Define process-model intended use, boundary, inputs, outputs, assumptions, and acceptance criteria. Explain: Give a boundary, an example of evidence, and a concrete case in which the claim would fail.
  2. Concept check: Implement a discrete-event or hybrid process model with queues, resources, rework, prioritization, and variants. Explain: Give a boundary, an example of evidence, and a concrete case in which the claim would fail.
  3. Concept check: Verify model logic, units, event sequencing, and reproducibility. Explain: Give a boundary, an example of evidence, and a concrete case in which the claim would fail.
  4. Sensitivity exercise: Recalculate or re-evaluate the Northstar case after changing one major assumption. State the original and revised decision.
  5. Transfer exercise: Apply simulation credibility to a different engineering system (hospital scheduling, power distribution, or emergency communications). Describe what remains transferable and what does not.

Explained response guide

  • A strong answer separates the mission outcome from the internal activity metric, states which evidence is authoritative, and specifies an escalation or stop rule when evidence becomes stale.
  • For the sensitivity exercise, Model a two-reviewer queue with demand uncertainty and assess both mean and upper-tail lead time. The correct answer is conditional on the chosen assumption and its justification; there is no single universally preferred architecture or process.
  • For the transfer exercise, preserve the causal logic but redefine the boundary, user population, constraints, measurements, and physical limitations. Copying Northstar numbers into another domain would be invalid.
