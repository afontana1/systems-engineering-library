## The engineering situation

Documented procedures often differ from work as actually performed. The essential question is: **What process is truly operating, including variants, queues, rework, workarounds, and invisible information work?**

The central idea of this chapter is **an event log can show what actually happens**. Before a method is selected, the engineer must distinguish the decision being made, what evidence could falsify the leading approach, and which obligations cannot be relaxed. In the Northstar case, engineers, operators, safety leads and suppliers are accountable for different portions of one mission result. A locally successful artifact is not sufficient if the complete outcome is unsupported.

## Explanation: why the method works

A process model is a claim; an event log is a partial observation. Map each case ID to time-stamped events, activities and actors. Compare expected vs observed sequences while considering incomplete logs, time-zone errors, parallel work and legitimate exceptions.

A useful way to understand the mechanism is to follow the causal chain explicitly. Start with a stimulus or observed condition; ask which model connects it to the engineering outcome; identify the assumptions that let the model predict anything; and define the next decision. Avoid confusing a convenient dashboard measure with the actual mission variable. Where constraints are hard—such as safety authorization, configuration compatibility or accessibility—treat them as eligibility conditions before computing preferences.

**Deepening the reasoning — Event-log integrity.** Process-mining claims depend on consistent case IDs, clocks and recorded actors. An activity appearing out of order may reflect batching, synchronization, parallel execution or a real bypass. Investigate before correcting the process.

### Connect the theory to the course vocabulary

- Use maps for different purposes: context, value stream, decision flow, responsibility, and executable behavior.
- An event log should have stable case identifiers, activity semantics, timestamps, actors or systems, and provenance.
- Process mining can expose paths and variants, but poor event semantics produce false precision.
- Conformance gaps may represent noncompliance, justified tailoring, tool behavior, emergency procedure, or bad data.
- Validate with data, artifacts, observation/interviews, and exception cases rather than one workshop.

## The mechanism beneath the method

Event logs reconstruct actual process behavior only if timestamps, event meanings and identities are reliable.

### Distinguishing related ideas

A flowchart documents intended work; a trace reveals what happened, including loops and exception paths.

### Interpretation and boundary conditions

Data-cleaning decisions can change apparent bottlenecks, so conformance analysis needs explicit semantics and provenance.

## Deeper theory: event logs are partial observations of a working process

### Process maps and event traces answer different questions

A procedure map describes the intended ordering of activities and decision responsibilities. An event log records selected events produced by the actual work system. To reconstruct cases, events need at least a case identifier, activity label, and timestamp; resource identity, event lifecycle state, and outcome fields can enrich analysis. The meaning of the *case* is foundational. For Northstar, one case could be a change request, an interface defect, a vehicle maintenance action, or a critical passenger incident. Mixing these entities under one identifier produces sequences that no single case actually experienced.

The log is not a perfect mirror. People work in spreadsheets and meetings, records are entered late, and some activities generate no digital trace. Different systems timestamp creation, approval, and completion differently. Clock skew may create negative apparent durations. A process-mining model that precisely reproduces the log can still misrepresent invisible rework or informal waiting. Document logging coverage and reconstructability before claiming what staff “actually do.” Observation bias and record-creation incentives are engineering risks, not peripheral data-cleaning details.

### Conformance is a relationship between traces and normative behavior

Given an observed trace `σ` and a reference process model `M`, conformance analysis asks whether `σ` can be replayed within `M` and where it deviates. Token-based replay, alignments, and other techniques can classify violations, but their results depend on model expressiveness, log quality, and the cost assigned to different mismatches. An unexpected activity may be a harmful bypass, an approved exception, or legitimate local adaptation. Counting deviations without classifying consequences can reward brittle bureaucracy over safe and effective performance.

A graph showing that review A usually precedes approval B is descriptive; it does not establish that A caused B or that enforcing that order would improve outcomes. Cases differ in risk, complexity, and organizational context. High-severity defects may require more reviews and also take longer, making review count positively associated with delay even when the reviews prevent further loss. Causal assessment requires comparison of relevant case types, time ordering, plausible confounders, and ideally intervention evidence. Process mining supplies hypotheses, not causal proof.

### Queueing and rework reveal where elapsed time is spent

Cycle time includes active processing, waiting, rework, and external holds. Little's law connects average work in process and throughput to lead time when the system is sufficiently stable, but it does not identify which activity is the bottleneck. For that, combine transition traces with arrival rates, resource calendars, case mix, and repeated loops. An engineering change can be approved quickly in isolation while spending weeks awaiting a shared cybersecurity reviewer. Raising throughput at an upstream stage may increase the downstream queue if the actual bottleneck remains unchanged.

Rework is especially informative. A trace `draft → review → revise → review → approve` may indicate healthy scrutiny, poor requirements, unclear authority, or an inadequate interface contract. Analyze reasons, not only loop count. Compare rework burden by defect class and supplier, and relate it to escaped defects and downstream quality. A program that eliminates review loops while increasing field failures has not necessarily improved its process.

### Responsible process discovery includes people and counterfactuals

Logs may reveal individuals' activity and performance; this creates privacy, fairness, and gaming risks. Differences in timestamps can reflect case complexity or uneven access to systems rather than individual competence. Use appropriate aggregation, governance, and consultation with workers who understand operational context. Before redesigning the process, articulate what mechanism the change is expected to alter, who may bear new work, and what adverse outcome would invalidate the improvement claim. The strongest current-state map combines trace evidence, interviews, physical workflow observation, and explicit uncertainty about what the logging system cannot see.

## Worked Northstar engineering case

The documented process shows one impact-analysis activity followed by a board review. The event data reveals three team variants, repeated “request clarification” loops, pre-board spreadsheet reconciliation, and testing that begins before configuration authorization. The example reconciles these into a canonical model with explicit variants and evidence-quality flags.

Case C4 has events Submitted 09:00, Approved 11:00, SafetyReview 14:00. This is a potential approval-before-safety exception; ask whether the log order reflects reality before concluding misconduct.

Work the example in two passes. First compute or trace the nominal outcome, naming the unit of analysis, released configuration and decision threshold. Second change one assumption, such as a degraded interface, heavier demand, invalid provenance or altered stakeholder constraint. If the recommendation changes, identify the threshold at which it changes; if it does not, explain the margin of robustness. A conclusion without its assumptions is not a reusable engineering result.

## A systematic check before accepting the result

1. **Boundary:** What part of the program, process or mission is represented, and what has been excluded?
2. **Evidence:** Which observations, versions, or tests support the premises, and what remains hypothetical?
3. **Causality:** Could the proposed mechanism be confused with a proxy, confounding condition or common dependency?
4. **Decision:** Which alternatives are still feasible, who has authority, and what would force reconsideration?
5. **Assurance:** Which safety, security, accessibility or quality guardrail cannot be traded for speed or convenience?

### Counterexample and failure boundary

An automatically mined workflow inherits every logging blind spot and misclassification.

Flag any event sequence where safety review is later than approval; describe two alternative explanations. Notice that a mathematically consistent answer can still be an inappropriate decision if the intended use or validity assumptions have changed. In professional work, a responsible engineer records that limitation alongside the nominal result.

4. **Sensitivity exercise:** Recalculate or re-evaluate the Northstar case after changing one major assumption. State the original and revised decision.
5. **Transfer exercise:** Apply **event-log integrity** to a different engineering system (hospital scheduling, power distribution, or emergency communications). Describe what remains transferable and what does not.

### Explained response guide

- A strong answer separates the mission outcome from the internal activity metric, states which evidence is authoritative, and specifies an escalation or stop rule when evidence becomes stale.
- For the sensitivity exercise, **Flag any event sequence where safety review is later than approval; describe two alternative explanations.** The correct answer is conditional on the chosen assumption and its justification; there is no single universally preferred architecture or process.
- For the transfer exercise, preserve the causal logic but redefine the boundary, user population, constraints, measurements, and physical limitations. Copying Northstar numbers into another domain would be invalid.
