Trade studies, AHP and sensitivity without false precision

Conceptual explanation

Separate constraints from preferences

An alternative that cannot meet a mandatory safety constraint is not a poor-scoring option; it is infeasible. Screen alternatives for non-negotiable constraints first. For feasible options define decision criteria with direction (larger or smaller is better), measurement method, evidence source and weight. Avoid double counting correlated criteria—'reliability' and 'uptime' may reflect much of the same advantage.

A weighted additive model is Score(A) = Σ w_i s_i(A), where weights are nonnegative and sum to one, and scores share a consistent preference scale. The numerical result is conditional on the choice of normalization and weights; it does not create objective truth. If cost is reported in dollars and safety is expressed as 1–5 ratings, they must be transformed to comparable utility scores by a defensible rule.

AHP as a structured elicitation tool

Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) can derive relative priorities from pairwise comparisons. It is useful when decision makers can more readily compare pairs than assign direct weights. Its consistency check helps identify contradictory preferences, but a consistent judgment may still be biased or based on poor evidence. Do not use AHP to imply that uncertainty in technical evidence has disappeared.

Sensitivity is part of the result

For each decisive assumption, ask how far it must move for the preferred option to change. Test plausible intervals for weights and performance estimates, including joint changes where appropriate. A decision is robust if the same choice remains preferable over credible variation; otherwise document the switch points and plan information gathering. Distinguish sensitivity to stakeholder value (weight changes) from sensitivity to technical evidence (score changes).

Decision record

Record alternatives, constraint screening, criteria and weighting authority, score transformations, sources, uncertainty, sensitivity results, dissent and conditions for reevaluation. For safety-critical decisions, separate assurance arguments from preference scoring: safety compliance is not something to trade away for a lower acquisition cost.

Worked example: reason through the case

Worked example: Two feasible shuttle concepts are scored from 0–100 on access, cost and maintainability. A scores (90, 60, 70), B scores (70, 85, 80). Weights (0.50, 0.30, 0.20) give A = 45+18+14 = 77, B = 35+25.5+16 = 76.5. A wins by just 0.5; this is a fragile recommendation. If accessibility weight falls to 0.40 and cost rises to 0.40 (maintenance stays 0.20), scores change to A = 74, B = 78. The preferred alternative switches, so decision makers should understand why the original weights are justified.

Retrieval and transfer practice

  1. Which comes first: mandatory accessibility compliance or weighted cost scoring?
  2. Compute scores for A=(90,60,70) and B=(70,85,80) under equal weights.
  3. A high score depends on an untested sensor claim. How should the decision record represent this?

A failure mode to recognize

Mistake: treating tiny differences in weighted totals as decisive proof, or trading a hard constraint as if it were a preference.
