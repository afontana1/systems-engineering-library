Conceptual explanation

Begin with a decision, not an object

A system of interest (SoI) is the set of interacting elements you are deliberately engineering for a stated decision. A bus is not automatically the SoI of a campus shuttle project. If the decision concerns safe transportation of passengers from origin to destination, dispatch, pickup locations, operational rules, passenger information, human supervision, and maintenance may belong in the service boundary. If the decision concerns a vehicle procurement, many of those are external systems connected by interfaces. Neither boundary is universally correct: the right boundary depends on the accountable outcome and decision authority.

Write a boundary statement using four clauses: (1) purpose; (2) included elements and functions; (3) explicitly excluded but dependent systems; and (4) authority to control each interface. An interface is more than a line joining boxes. It is an agreement about what crosses a boundary: passengers, commands, data, energy, material, services, or responsibility. Interface descriptions need direction, trigger, format or units, timing, failure behavior, ownership, and test evidence.

Why a system is more than its parts

A fleet of individually safe vehicles can produce an unsafe transport service if queueing, dispatch rules, pedestrians, weather, and crossing interactions create hazardous conditions. Whole-system properties emerge from interactions: throughput, availability, safety, resilience, and user trust. A component-level pass does not prove a system-level pass. This is why the context model should lead naturally to scenarios and evidence, rather than remaining a static drawing.

Systems thinking also means asking who bears the consequences. A decision that makes the shuttle operator's cost look lower by excluding campus network support may not reduce total service cost. It transfers work to a different organization. Distinguish excluded from the contract from irrelevant to the mission; only the first may be defensible.

A practical modeling sequence

Identify the service outcome, create two plausible SoI boundaries, enumerate external actors and systems, draw directed flows, and walk one normal and one off-nominal scenario across the flows. Mark interfaces without a single accountable owner. Maintain a boundary decision record with assumptions and a reason for choosing the boundary; revisit it when operating concepts change.

Worked example: reason through the case

Illustrative case (invented numbers): A three-stop pilot delivers 240 trips/day. Boundary A includes vehicle and onboard control; dispatch and rider alerts are external. Boundary B includes those services. If dispatch fails for two hours, vehicles may all be operational but delivered trips fall by 25%. Under Boundary A the vehicle supplier can report 100% vehicle availability while the service fails. Boundary B allocates a service-level requirement and escalation route to the operator. Neither supplier report alone demonstrates the user outcome. Draw the dispatch-to-vehicle interface: route command (route ID, timestamp, validity interval, acknowledged receipt), fallback to safe stop on missing commands, and a test where the network is severed.

Retrieval and transfer practice

  1. Draw a context model for a library book-return kiosk, choosing the boundary to cover completed returns rather than only its hardware.
  2. A vehicle meets every onboard test, yet routes are repeatedly canceled. Explain why this is not contradictory.
  3. Write a minimum interface contract for a shuttle receiving dispatch commands.

A failure mode to recognize

Mistake: treating the diagram boundary as an organization chart. A service may cross organizations; governance and interface contracts preserve responsibility where ownership is split.
