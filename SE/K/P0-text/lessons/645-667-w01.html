Governance, decision rights and the PM–SE interface

Conceptual explanation

A project is a temporary authority system

A project manager turns a desired capability into an authorized commitment under finite resources. Systems engineering defines and preserves technical coherence. These roles overlap at reviews, risk decisions, interfaces and baseline changes but are not interchangeable. The PM usually owns the integrated delivery commitment and escalates contractual changes; the chief systems engineer is normally accountable for technical integration and recommended technical decisions. Actual authority depends on the governance charter and contractual context.

A charter establishes why the project exists, boundaries of authority, sponsor, objective, scope, constraints, major milestones, funding assumptions and initial risks. It is neither a fully detailed execution plan nor a license to change customer requirements at will. The crucial governance question is: Who can make which decision, with what evidence and concurrence?

RACI is not sufficient by itself

A responsibility matrix indicates who is responsible, accountable, consulted and informed, but usually omits decision thresholds. Complement it with a decision-rights table: action (approve design change), proposing authority (chief engineer), required concurrence (safety, operations, contracting), threshold (cost/scope/safety effects), decision owner (change board or sponsor), required evidence and appeal/escalation. Never use a RACI to pretend the PM can override a legally designated technical or safety authority.

The management rhythm is an information system

A useful weekly technical-control cycle collects work-package progress, decisions, emerging risks and anomalies. A monthly integrated review reconciles technical maturity, scope, schedule, cost and forecast. Configuration and change boards occur when proposals need decisions, not only because a calendar says so. Each meeting needs pre-read, decision items, owner and output. Reporting must permit bad news to travel upward before options expire.

Handling disagreement

If the PM needs an earlier launch but safety evidence is incomplete, the legitimate options are reduce scope, change schedule/resources, add evidence or restrict operation—not redefine the test as optional. Governance makes such conflicts visible, assigns the decision to the proper authority and preserves a rationale for auditors and operators.

Worked example: reason through the case

Example: A sponsor requests an extra shuttle stop at month four. The SE assesses geofence, interfaces, accessibility, safety and tests. The PM assesses contract scope, work packages, cost and schedule. The delegated customer authority approves any modified commitment. A change record links both impact analyses before approval; an email saying 'sounds good' is not sufficient authority unless the governance framework explicitly makes it so.

Retrieval and transfer practice

  1. Who should assess the technical effects of an added route stop, and who should approve contractual changes?
  2. Write a decision-rights rule for accepting a safety-critical residual risk.
  3. Identify three outputs from a useful monthly integrated review.

A failure mode to recognize

Mistake: distributing responsibility widely while leaving no explicit, empowered decision owner.
