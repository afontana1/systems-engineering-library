## Conceptual explanation

### A schedule is a model of causality
A schedule is more than dates printed next to task names. It represents which outcomes enable later work, how much elapsed time tasks need, what resources they consume and which paths control completion. In an activity-on-node network, each activity has duration and predecessor logic. For a finish-to-start relationship, the earliest start is the maximum earliest finish of its predecessors. Earliest finish = earliest start + duration. Backward calculation from the required finish gives latest allowable dates; *total float* = latest start − earliest start. This uses a consistent time convention and assumes durations and resource constraints are as modeled.

A critical path comprises zero-float activities under the modeled finish constraint, but multiple near-critical paths can matter. Duration estimates are not effort estimates: 40 labor hours might be performed over five days by one person or longer with intermittent access to a shared test facility. Calendars, holidays, approvals, staffing, external deliveries and capacity should be modeled explicitly.

### What makes a schedule trustworthy
Each meaningful activity should have traceable scope, predecessors/successors, realistic duration, responsible resources and status logic. Long chains of mandatory date constraints or arbitrary lags can hide the actual driving path. Schedule metrics should expose unresolved logic, negative float and unusual constraints. Rolling-wave planning may refine distant activities while retaining major scope and milestone dependencies.

### Resource feasibility
Two activities may be logically parallel yet compete for the same single test engineer or facility. The unconstrained network gives a lower bound on elapsed time. Resource leveling can extend the schedule and change the driving path. Distinguish critical path under pure logic from a resource-constrained critical chain. Model a recovery plan's effects on quality and risk, not only nominal days saved.

### Schedule risk
When several uncertain tasks converge before one integration milestone, the finish probability is not derived from a single best-case path. Near-critical paths can become critical. A credible schedule risk review explores duration ranges, dependencies and correlated uncertainties. Do not confuse an earned-value schedule index with a forecast of actual completion time.

## Worked example: reason through the case

**Worked network (days, finish-to-start):** A architecture (3); B procurement (5) after A; C controller design (4) after A; D integration (3) after B and C; E acceptance (2) after D. Paths: A–B–D–E = 3+5+3+2 = **13 days**; A–C–D–E = 3+4+3+2 = **12 days**. Critical path is A–B–D–E, and C has 1 day of total float if no other constraints. If B needs six days, C’s nominal one-day float does not protect the completion milestone.

## Retrieval and transfer practice

1. Recalculate completion time if B falls from 5 to 3 days and C remains 4.
2. Why can a logically valid schedule still be impossible?
3. What does zero total float imply, and what does it NOT imply?

1. A–B–D–E = 11; A–C–D–E = 12. The critical path switches to C and total completion becomes 12 days.

2. Resource contention, availability calendars, external approvals, unrealistic durations or missing work can make the logic-only plan infeasible.

3. Under the stated model, any delay to that activity without recovery impacts the constrained finish; it does not prove the date is statistically likely or that every near-critical path is safe.

## A failure mode to recognize

Mistake: interpreting a critical-path chart as a probability distribution or a guarantee that resources exist.
