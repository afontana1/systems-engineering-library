# EN.645.783 · Week 08 — Future-state alternatives and systems process redesign

## Why this idea matters

A systems engineer must produce a decision that remains defensible when the conditions change. This chapter focuses on **future-state redesign and tradeoffs**: how to reason from purpose and evidence to a bounded action. The objective is not to memorize a term, but to state what it permits, when it fails, and what observable evidence would change your conclusion.

## Build the mental model

Redesign should address the causal bottleneck and preserve necessary controls. Generate at least three alternatives: upstream error prevention, work allocation/parallelism and policy/approval changes. Score them on outcome, implementation cost, adoption burden, reversibility and guardrails. Define how changed procedures are versioned and how older projects transition.

**Useful diagnostic distinction:** Automating an unfit process can make poor decisions faster.

## The underlying mechanisms: lessons to retain

The following instructional notes are adapted from the corresponding original course guide. They form a compact mechanism checklist to use while reading the new explanation and doing the chapter exercise.

* Generate concepts before optimizing details: policy-only, role/flow, digital enablement, and structural redesign are useful contrasting families.
* Lean removes non-value-adding delay and rework; it does not remove assurance that protects mission outcomes.
* Future-state models must include exception and degraded paths, not only an ideal nominal flow.
* Automation needs authority, data quality, false-positive/negative handling, monitoring, fallback, and accountability.
* A process design is incomplete without roles, skills, incentives, workload, repositories, controls, and transition dependencies.

## Worked reasoning: the Northstar mobility case

Northstar compares an evidence-completeness checklist, a daily expedited review channel and full process automation. The checklist may have the highest near-term expected benefit because it attacks repeated returns; automation could harden bad rules. Pilot before committing enterprise-wide.

### Compare with the guide's worked case

The original guide uses the following short case discussion. Reproduce the reasoning with a changed assumption rather than copying its conclusion.

Three alternatives are compared: A) add review capacity and shorten board cadence; B) establish evidence-readiness criteria, cross-functional triage, and continuous low-risk authorization; C) integrate digital-thread impact queries, risk-class routing, and evidence-based release gates. A hybrid B/C performs best, but only if supplier data and accessibility evidence are available earlier.

Reproduce the example using controlled course data, change one assumption, and explain how the result or decision changes.

### How to challenge the example

1. **Boundary:** Name the engineered product, process, actors and environment relevant to this claim. List one important exclusion.
2. **Evidence:** Identify one source that would let an independent reviewer reproduce or falsify the result.
3. **Failure mode:** Test what happens when an input is stale, unavailable, biased, incorrectly configured or in conflict with another artifact.
4. **Decision:** State who can authorize the next action, which guardrail could block it, and how the decision will be recorded.

## Learning objectives from the original guide

* Generate distinct future-state process concepts rather than one preferred map.
* Apply Lean waste, flow, feedback, mistake-proofing, digital-thread, and human-centered principles.
* Define future-state roles, decision rights, information, controls, automation, variants, and exceptions.
* Evaluate alternatives using process simulation, stakeholder criteria, risk, cost, and implementation feasibility.
* Conduct a Future-State Design Review.

## Practice questions with guided answers

   To reveal whether technology is solving a real mechanism or merely adding complexity.

   </details>

2. **What is mistake-proofing?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Designing the process so errors are prevented or detected at the earliest practical point.

   </details>

3. **Why model exception paths?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Real engineering processes must handle urgent, incomplete, high-risk, and degraded conditions.

   </details>

4. **What makes an alternative coherent?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Its roles, information, controls, tools, incentives, and governance work together.

   </details>

   </details>

## Extended transfer exercise

Construct a one-page **claim–evidence–decision map** for this week's topic. Include a claim, current baseline, configuration or version, measurable response, source and timestamp of evidence, alternative explanation, reviewer, unresolved uncertainty and stop/go rule. Then deliberately inject one defect (missing source, wrong version, unexpected consequence or inadequate coverage) and explain how the decision should change.

**Stretch:** Use a second scenario with a different consequence level or stakeholder group. Explain why the same method may require different assurance controls.

## Bridge to the assessed curriculum

The companion explains the concepts; the original guide supplies the complete assignment, sources, rubric and formal mastery gate. This is the guide's weekly deliverable:

**Future-State Design Review Package** containing concepts, maps, roles, controls, information architecture, variants, simulation results, decision criteria, sensitivity, risk, cost, implementation dependencies, preferred concept, findings, and dispositions.

Submit native or textual source, controlled data and transformations, generated views, a change log, and a one-page self-review. Screenshots alone are not acceptable evidence.

**Common critical failures to avoid (from the guide):**

* Presenting one solution as if alternatives were considered.
* Equating fewer approvals with better assurance.
* Automating without authority, data-quality, error, fallback, or accountability design.
* Ignoring workforce burden, incentives, or supplier dependencies.

**Mastery and revision standard:**

Pass the Future-State Design Review with an approved concept, retained alternative, critical controls, pilot assumptions, and unresolved-risk plan.
