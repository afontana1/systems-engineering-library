# EN.645.780 · Week 05 — Plan to discover through learning intervals, hypotheses, options, and rolling commitment

**Phase 4 Companion Textbook · Agile Systems Engineering**  
**Primary concept:** Learning intervals and option-preserving plans  
**Status:** Original companion explanation plus adapted excerpts from the supplied Week 5 learning guide.  
**Data:** Worked numerical values are synthetic teaching illustrations, not field observations.

## Why this idea matters

A systems engineer must produce a decision that remains defensible when the conditions change. This chapter focuses on **learning intervals and option-preserving plans**: how to reason from purpose and evidence to a bounded action. The objective is not to memorize a term, but to state what it permits, when it fails, and what observable evidence would change your conclusion.

## Build the mental model

An iteration should retire a named uncertainty. For each learning interval record: assumption, prediction, minimum experiment, acceptance threshold, evidence owner, deadline, and next irreversible commitment. A prototype is useful when the observation would change a decision. Rolling-wave planning gives detailed commitments near term while preserving flexibility in later work. Compare the expected cost of learning with the possible cost of a premature commitment.

**Useful diagnostic distinction:** “We held a sprint review” is not evidence that a meaningful uncertainty was reduced.

## The underlying mechanisms: lessons to retain

The following instructional notes are adapted from the corresponding original course guide. They form a compact mechanism checklist to use while reading the new explanation and doing the chapter exercise.

* A learning interval is a bounded cycle from question to evidence to decision, not simply a sprint label.
* Hypotheses should predict observable behavior and define what result would change the plan.
* Minimum viable evidence is the least credible evidence needed for a specific decision—not the least work the team can demonstrate.
* Options have value when uncertainty is high and commitment is expensive, but maintaining every option creates cost and integration complexity.
* Rolling-wave plans increase detail as uncertainty falls. They do not excuse invisible dependencies or unbudgeted long-lead work.
* Each interval needs entry conditions, question, planned increment/experiment, integration target, evidence, decision owner, exit criteria, and baseline disposition.

## Worked reasoning: the Northstar mobility case

Northstar is unsure whether a second charging bay is needed. Instead of buying construction equipment now, run instrumented demand and charger-availability experiments for two weeks. Define beforehand the congestion threshold that would justify design investment. A result without a predeclared action rule is interesting but not decision-ready.

### Compare with the guide's worked case

The original guide uses the following short case discussion. Reproduce the reasoning with a changed assumption rather than copying its conclusion.

Before committing to a new boarding mechanism, NMS needs to learn whether the proposed design meets accessibility and dwell-time outcomes across users and weather. A six-week interval uses a physical mock-up, human-in-the-loop trials, and a boarding simulation. The decision is not “finish prototype”; it is whether to retain concept A, switch to B, or defer tooling. Minimum viable evidence includes representative participants, defined measures, uncertainty bounds, safety observations, and trace to the decision criteria.

### How to challenge the example

1. **Boundary:** Name the engineered product, process, actors and environment relevant to this claim. List one important exclusion.
2. **Evidence:** Identify one source that would let an independent reviewer reproduce or falsify the result.
3. **Failure mode:** Test what happens when an input is stale, unavailable, biased, incorrectly configured or in conflict with another artifact.
4. **Decision:** State who can authorize the next action, which guardrail could block it, and how the decision will be recorded.

## Learning objectives from the original guide

* Decompose program uncertainty into decision-relevant learning questions.
* Write falsifiable technical, operational, stakeholder, and process hypotheses.
* Design learning intervals that include build, integrate, evaluate, decide, and baseline update.
* Distinguish minimum viable product, minimum viable capability, minimum viable experiment, and minimum viable evidence.
* Create rolling-wave plans that preserve options while exposing schedule, cost, and long-lead commitments.

## Practice questions with guided answers

Attempt the questions before expanding each answer. These five knowledge checks and their answer guidance are **adapted from the supplied Week 5 course guide**; their inclusion does not replace the larger independent assignment.

1. **What is a learning interval?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   A bounded question-build-integrate-evaluate-decide-update cycle with explicit evidence and exit criteria.

   </details>

2. **What is minimum viable evidence?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   The least credible evidence sufficient for a named decision and consequence, not the smallest demonstration.

   </details>

3. **Why preserve options?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   To avoid premature costly commitment while uncertainty is decision-relevant.

   </details>

4. **What makes a hypothesis falsifiable?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   It predicts an observable result and states what evidence would count against it.

   </details>

5. **What is rolling-wave planning?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Planning near-term work in detail and later work at an appropriate level, updating as evidence reduces uncertainty.

   </details>

## Extended transfer exercise

Construct a one-page **claim–evidence–decision map** for this week's topic. Include a claim, current baseline, configuration or version, measurable response, source and timestamp of evidence, alternative explanation, reviewer, unresolved uncertainty and stop/go rule. Then deliberately inject one defect (missing source, wrong version, unexpected consequence or inadequate coverage) and explain how the decision should change.

**Stretch:** Use a second scenario with a different consequence level or stakeholder group. Explain why the same method may require different assurance controls.

## Bridge to the assessed curriculum

The companion explains the concepts; the original guide supplies the complete assignment, sources, rubric and formal mastery gate. This is the guide's weekly deliverable:

**Learning-Interval Roadmap and Evidence Plan** with uncertainty map, decision calendar, option-expiration view, interval charters, integrated demonstration cadence, and revised schedule/risks.

Submit controlled source files, data/calculations, generated views, a change log, and a one-page self-review against the rubric. Screenshots alone are not acceptable evidence.

**Common critical failures to avoid (from the guide):**

* Defining intervals only by task completion.
* Calling a demo evidence without measures or decision criteria.
* Ignoring long-lead option expiration.
* Using “MVP” to bypass safety, accessibility, or security obligations.

**Mastery and revision standard:**

At least six intervals must connect a learning question to evidence and a real decision. No critical commitment may occur before its required evidence without explicit risk acceptance.
