EN.645.780 · Week 07 — Architect products and engineering processes for affordable change, options, and continuous integration

Phase 4 Companion Textbook · Agile Systems Engineering
Primary concept: Architecture as the economics of future change
Status: Original companion explanation plus adapted excerpts from the supplied Week 7 learning guide.
Data: Worked numerical values are synthetic teaching illustrations, not field observations.

Why this idea matters

A systems engineer must produce a decision that remains defensible when the conditions change. This chapter focuses on architecture as the economics of future change: how to reason from purpose and evidence to a bounded action. The objective is not to memorize a term, but to state what it permits, when it fails, and what observable evidence would change your conclusion.

Build the mental model

Modularity creates change options when components can vary without forcing coordinated revisions elsewhere. The economic value comes from independent substitutability, stable interface contracts, observability and testability; module count alone is not proof. Map likely change classes to coupling paths. Reserve margins where uncertainty is substantial and identify what would consume them. Technical debt may be rational if intentionally bounded, visible and paid down before it creates system risk.

Useful diagnostic distinction: More microservices or interface layers do not guarantee cheaper change; extra interfaces also cost money.

The underlying mechanisms: lessons to retain

The following instructional notes are adapted from the corresponding original course guide. They form a compact mechanism checklist to use while reading the new explanation and doing the chapter exercise.

  • Modularity is a set of change and interaction properties, not a diagram style. A module should have coherent responsibility and controlled interactions.
  • Interface stability can enable independent change, but freezing an immature interface too early can institutionalize wrong assumptions.
  • Architecture runway is the minimum enabling architecture needed for forecast increments and experiments, created with explicit option and evidence logic.
  • Product-line architecture can speed variation through reusable assets and defined variation points, while increasing configuration and verification burden.
  • Process architecture matters too: reusable test environments, interface contracts, model patterns, and automated evidence reduce transaction cost.
  • Architecture debt is measured by change cost, dependency, integration delay, defect escape, and option loss—not by aesthetic dislike.

Worked reasoning: the Northstar mobility case

Northstar considers a new accessible boarding sensor. In Architecture A, a sensor message is directly consumed by five subsystems; in B, an interface adaptor converts messages into a validated event contract. B adds a small initial cost but may reduce five coordinated retests to adaptor-plus-contract validation. Quantify this under multiple change frequencies.

Compare with the guide's worked case

The original guide uses the following short case discussion. Reproduce the reasoning with a changed assumption rather than copying its conclusion.

NMS finds that changing passenger-assistance behavior requires coordinated edits in the app, dispatch service, vehicle controller, operator console, and test scripts. The team introduces a versioned assistance-service contract, common event schema, simulator adapter, and conformance tests. Change-propagation paths fall from nine manual touchpoints to four controlled interfaces, but the variation point creates a new configuration matrix that must be governed.

How to challenge the example

  1. Boundary: Name the engineered product, process, actors and environment relevant to this claim. List one important exclusion.
  2. Evidence: Identify one source that would let an independent reviewer reproduce or falsify the result.
  3. Failure mode: Test what happens when an input is stale, unavailable, biased, incorrectly configured or in conflict with another artifact.
  4. Decision: State who can authorize the next action, which guardrail could block it, and how the decision will be recorded.

Learning objectives from the original guide

  • Evaluate modularity, cohesion, coupling, interface stability, substitutability, testability, deployability, and commonality as agility properties.
  • Distinguish modular architecture from a large number of components or services.
  • Design architecture runway and technical options tied to forecast learning and variability.
  • Analyze product-line and platform opportunities without hiding variant complexity.
  • Quantify change propagation and identify architecture debt that constrains learning.

Practice questions with guided answers

Attempt the questions before expanding each answer. These five knowledge checks and their answer guidance are adapted from the supplied Week 7 course guide; their inclusion does not replace the larger independent assignment.

  1. What is architecture-enabled agility?

    <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

    The ability of product and process structures to support timely, affordable, controlled change and integration.

    </details>

  2. What is false modularity?

    <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

    A component decomposition that still requires pervasive coordinated changes because responsibilities or interfaces are not truly independent.

    </details>

  3. What is architecture runway?

    <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

    Evidence-justified enabling architecture needed for near-future increments and learning, not unlimited speculative infrastructure.

    </details>

  4. What new burden can product-line variation create?

    <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

    Configuration, interaction, compatibility, test, and evidence-combination complexity.

    </details>

  5. How should architecture debt be evidenced?

    <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

    Through change propagation, delay, defect, integration, testability, option, and lifecycle-cost effects.

    </details>

Extended transfer exercise

Construct a one-page claim–evidence–decision map for this week's topic. Include a claim, current baseline, configuration or version, measurable response, source and timestamp of evidence, alternative explanation, reviewer, unresolved uncertainty and stop/go rule. Then deliberately inject one defect (missing source, wrong version, unexpected consequence or inadequate coverage) and explain how the decision should change.

Stretch: Use a second scenario with a different consequence level or stakeholder group. Explain why the same method may require different assurance controls.

Bridge to the assessed curriculum

The companion explains the concepts; the original guide supplies the complete assignment, sources, rubric and formal mastery gate. This is the guide's weekly deliverable:

Architecture for Agility Package with change scenarios, propagation graphs, modularity assessment, interface/variation decisions, architecture runway, option economics, testability strategy, and a 1,200-word executive essay on architecture-enabled agility.

Submit controlled source files, data/calculations, generated views, a change log, and a one-page self-review against the rubric. Screenshots alone are not acceptable evidence.

Common critical failures to avoid (from the guide):

  • Claiming microservices, APIs, or more components automatically create agility.
  • Creating a variation point without configuration and verification controls.
  • Refactoring without a linked learning/value need.
  • Ignoring physical, safety, or data coupling.

Mastery and revision standard:

The package must show at least three quantified change scenarios and one rejected architecture option. No critical interface may be made “flexible” by removing its contract or verification.
