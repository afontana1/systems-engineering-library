## Why this idea matters

A systems engineer must produce a decision that remains defensible when the conditions change. This chapter focuses on **event logs and conformance**: how to reason from purpose and evidence to a bounded action. The objective is not to memorize a term, but to state what it permits, when it fails, and what observable evidence would change your conclusion.

## Build the mental model

Map actual flow from time-stamped case IDs, events, ownership and state transitions. Compare observed paths with prescribed paths. Analyze variants, loops, queues, skipped checks and rework—not merely average durations. Event-log quality matters: duplicate records, missing IDs, shifted clocks and stage labels can create phantom bottlenecks.

**Useful diagnostic distinction:** Process mining describes recorded events; it cannot directly establish why people behaved that way.

## The underlying mechanisms: lessons to retain

The following instructional notes are adapted from the corresponding original course guide. They form a compact mechanism checklist to use while reading the new explanation and doing the chapter exercise.

* Use maps for different purposes: context, value stream, decision flow, responsibility, and executable behavior.
* An event log should have stable case identifiers, activity semantics, timestamps, actors or systems, and provenance.
* Process mining can expose paths and variants, but poor event semantics produce false precision.
* Conformance gaps may represent noncompliance, justified tailoring, tool behavior, emergency procedure, or bad data.
* Validate with data, artifacts, observation/interviews, and exception cases rather than one workshop.

### How to challenge the example

1. **Boundary:** Name the engineered product, process, actors and environment relevant to this claim. List one important exclusion.
2. **Evidence:** Identify one source that would let an independent reviewer reproduce or falsify the result.
3. **Failure mode:** Test what happens when an input is stale, unavailable, biased, incorrectly configured or in conflict with another artifact.
4. **Decision:** State who can authorize the next action, which guardrail could block it, and how the decision will be recorded.

   </details>

2. **Why distinguish event time from record-entry time?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Record-entry delay can distort sequence, duration, and causality.

   </details>

3. **What is conformance analysis?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Comparison of observed execution with a reference model or rules to locate and interpret differences.

   </details>

4. **Why retain variants?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Different risk classes or contexts may require legitimate tailored paths.

   </details>

5. **What validates an as-is map?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Agreement among controlled data, artifacts, performers, authorities, and observed exception behavior.

   </details>

## Extended transfer exercise

Construct a one-page **claim–evidence–decision map** for this week's topic. Include a claim, current baseline, configuration or version, measurable response, source and timestamp of evidence, alternative explanation, reviewer, unresolved uncertainty and stop/go rule. **Common critical failures to avoid (from the guide):**

* Presenting the documented procedure as the actual process without validation.
* Using unstable case identifiers or unexplained timestamp substitutions.
* Treating every conformance deviation as performer failure.
* Hiding variants or exceptions to make the map appear clean.
