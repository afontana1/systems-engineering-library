# EN.645.783 · Week 07 — Process modeling and simulation for improvement decisions

## Why this idea matters

A systems engineer must produce a decision that remains defensible when the conditions change. This chapter focuses on **process simulation as a decision instrument**: how to reason from purpose and evidence to a bounded action. The objective is not to memorize a term, but to state what it permits, when it fails, and what observable evidence would change your conclusion.

## Build the mental model

A process model represents arrivals, routing, rework, resources, service times and priorities. Use it to explore interventions that are too costly to test directly. First reproduce baseline distributions and bottlenecks, then challenge the model under credible extremes. Add variability and resource calendars rather than assuming deterministic average task times.

**Useful diagnostic distinction:** A simulation optimization is not credible without baseline validation and uncertainty analysis.

## The underlying mechanisms: lessons to retain

The following instructional notes are adapted from the corresponding original course guide. They form a compact mechanism checklist to use while reading the new explanation and doing the chapter exercise.

* The model should be no more complex than required for the stated improvement decision.
* Queues, priorities, calendars, batching, resource skills, rework, blocked states, and evidence dependencies often dominate engineering-process behavior.
* Historical fit is not proof that an intervention forecast is valid.
* Verification includes code review, event traces, conservation checks, extreme conditions, and deterministic test cases.
* Validation includes stakeholder face validity, distribution/behavior comparison, holdout cases, and limitation statements.

## Worked reasoning: the Northstar mobility case

A simple queue model shows adding one reviewer may lower queue time during peak demand, but gains disappear if submissions are incomplete. Simulate both reviewer capacity and rejection/rework rates; distinguish the interventions by their failure signatures.

### Compare with the guide's worked case

The original guide uses the following short case discussion. Reproduce the reasoning with a changed assumption rather than copying its conclusion.

A spreadsheet capacity ratio predicts that adding one reviewer will cut lead time by 25%. The DES model shows little benefit because cases wait for incomplete supplier and test evidence, not reviewer capacity. An automated evidence-readiness check plus smaller review batches performs better, but benefits depend on adoption and false-positive rates.

Reproduce the example using controlled course data, change one assumption, and explain how the result or decision changes.

### How to challenge the example

1. **Boundary:** Name the engineered product, process, actors and environment relevant to this claim. List one important exclusion.
2. **Evidence:** Identify one source that would let an independent reviewer reproduce or falsify the result.
3. **Failure mode:** Test what happens when an input is stale, unavailable, biased, incorrectly configured or in conflict with another artifact.
4. **Decision:** State who can authorize the next action, which guardrail could block it, and how the decision will be recorded.

## Learning objectives from the original guide

* Define process-model intended use, boundary, inputs, outputs, assumptions, and acceptance criteria.
* Implement a discrete-event or hybrid process model with queues, resources, rework, prioritization, and variants.
* Verify model logic, units, event sequencing, and reproducibility.
* Validate structure and behavior against current-state evidence.
* Use experiments and sensitivity analysis without overstating predictive accuracy.

## Extended transfer exercise

Construct a one-page **claim–evidence–decision map** for this week's topic. Include a claim, current baseline, configuration or version, measurable response, source and timestamp of evidence, alternative explanation, reviewer, unresolved uncertainty and stop/go rule. Then deliberately inject one defect (missing source, wrong version, unexpected consequence or inadequate coverage) and explain how the decision should change.

**Stretch:** Use a second scenario with a different consequence level or stakeholder group. Explain why the same method may require different assurance controls.

## Bridge to the assessed curriculum

The companion explains the concepts; the original guide supplies the complete assignment, sources, rubric and formal mastery gate. This is the guide's weekly deliverable:

**Executable Current-State Process Model Review Package** containing intended-use statement, conceptual model, source, inputs, assumptions, V&V tests, calibration, sensitivity, scenario results, limitations, and clean-run instructions.

Submit native or textual source, controlled data and transformations, generated views, a change log, and a one-page self-review. Screenshots alone are not acceptable evidence.

**Common critical failures to avoid (from the guide):**

* Using the model outside its stated intended use.
* Calibrating to one statistic while missing important paths or tails.
* Reporting stochastic results without replications or uncertainty.
* Submitting only screenshots or an opaque tool file.

**Mastery and revision standard:**

Pass the model review and clean reproduction; resolve every critical V&V or intended-use finding before selecting a future-state process.
