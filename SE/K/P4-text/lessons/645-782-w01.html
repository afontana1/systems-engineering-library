EN.645.782 · Week 01 — Lifecycle, acquisition, and digital/mission framing

Phase 4 Companion Textbook · Foundations of Digital and Mission Engineering
Primary concept: Decisions first, digital artifacts second
Status: Original companion explanation plus adapted excerpts from the supplied Week 1 learning guide.
Data: Worked numerical values are synthetic teaching illustrations, not field observations.

Why this idea matters

A systems engineer must produce a decision that remains defensible when the conditions change. This chapter focuses on decisions first, digital artifacts second: how to reason from purpose and evidence to a bounded action. The objective is not to memorize a term, but to state what it permits, when it fails, and what observable evidence would change your conclusion.

Build the mental model

Digital engineering is valuable when linked data and models improve a defined lifecycle decision. Start by listing who decides what, when, and with what consequence. Distinguish the product boundary from the mission boundary, which can include infrastructure, people, policies and other systems. An inherited model may be valid for design exploration yet not authoritative for operational release. Document authority at the level of intended use and configuration.

Useful diagnostic distinction: More detailed digital models do not imply better decisions if they model the wrong boundary.

The underlying mechanisms: lessons to retain

The following instructional notes are adapted from the corresponding original course guide. They form a compact mechanism checklist to use while reading the new explanation and doing the chapter exercise.

  • Digital engineering is an integrated way of performing engineering; it does not replace systems engineering or acquisition accountability.
  • Lifecycle stages create different evidence users and authority. A concept model, released design, test record, and operational state estimate can all describe the same system while having different authority.
  • Mission engineering begins with mission outcomes and end-to-end dependencies. It may change products, organizations, processes, doctrine, training, logistics, policy, or information exchanges.
  • Decision framing should state decision owner, question, timing, alternatives, evidence, consequence, and acceptable uncertainty.
  • The receiving review prevents inherited models and dashboards from becoming trusted by default.

Worked reasoning: the Northstar mobility case

Northstar wishes to improve emergency evacuation time. The shuttle architecture is only one contributor; boarding rules, communications, human dispatch and curb access are also inside the mission analysis. A detailed battery model cannot answer whether the whole evacuation mission succeeds.

Compare with the guide's worked case

The original guide uses the following short case discussion. Reproduce the reasoning with a changed assumption rather than copying its conclusion.

The sponsor asks Northstar to “build a digital twin for emergency mobility.” The example reframes this into four decisions: whether to pre-position vehicles, whether to curtail charging, how to route accessibility-dependent riders, and whether to request mutual aid. Each decision has different update rates, evidence, consequence, and uncertainty. The example shows that one monolithic twin is neither necessary nor automatically credible.

Reproduce the example using controlled course data, change one assumption, and explain how the decision or evidence changes.

How to challenge the example

  1. Boundary: Name the engineered product, process, actors and environment relevant to this claim. List one important exclusion.
  2. Evidence: Identify one source that would let an independent reviewer reproduce or falsify the result.
  3. Failure mode: Test what happens when an input is stale, unavailable, biased, incorrectly configured or in conflict with another artifact.
  4. Decision: State who can authorize the next action, which guardrail could block it, and how the decision will be recorded.

Learning objectives from the original guide

  • Reconstruct the principal technical and technical-management activities across the lifecycle and relate them to acquisition decisions.
  • Distinguish system, system of systems, enterprise, capability, mission, mission thread, and operational scenario.
  • Identify decision authorities, evidence users, timing, consequence, and information constraints.
  • Frame the Northstar mission problem without assuming the product is the only intervention.
  • Create a receiving baseline that distinguishes inherited evidence, assumptions, gaps, and unsupported claims.

Practice questions with guided answers

Attempt the questions before expanding each answer. These five knowledge checks and their answer guidance are adapted from the supplied Week 1 course guide; their inclusion does not replace the larger independent assignment.

  1. Why can two artifacts both be accurate but authoritative for different uses?

    <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

    Authority is scoped by decision, lifecycle state, configuration, owner, pedigree, and acceptable use.

    </details>

  2. What distinguishes a mission from a system?

    <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

    A mission is an intended outcome achieved by interacting systems, people, organizations, processes, and environment; a system is one engineered contributor.

    </details>

  3. What is the purpose of a receiving review?

    <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

    To establish what is inherited, controlled, credible, authoritative, missing, or disputed before use.

    </details>

  4. Why does digital engineering not remove technical management?

    <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

    Connected models and data increase the need for planning, authority, configuration, risk, decisions, access, and review.

    </details>

  5. What makes a decision-to-evidence map useful?

    <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

    It exposes whether the right evidence exists, is timely, trusted, accessible, and connected to the accountable decision.

    </details>

Extended transfer exercise

Construct a one-page claim–evidence–decision map for this week's topic. Include a claim, current baseline, configuration or version, measurable response, source and timestamp of evidence, alternative explanation, reviewer, unresolved uncertainty and stop/go rule. Then deliberately inject one defect (missing source, wrong version, unexpected consequence or inadequate coverage) and explain how the decision should change.

Stretch: Use a second scenario with a different consequence level or stakeholder group. Explain why the same method may require different assurance controls.

Bridge to the assessed curriculum

The companion explains the concepts; the original guide supplies the complete assignment, sources, rubric and formal mastery gate. This is the guide's weekly deliverable:

Digital and Mission Engineering Receiving Package containing the lifecycle map, decision register, boundary views, evidence inventory, authority/gap assessment, mission-neutral problem statement, and receiving memo. Audience: sponsor, chief engineer, mission owner, and data/model governance lead. Length: 10–14 pages plus controlled tables and source files.

Submit native or textual source, data and transformations, generated views, query/analysis source, a change log, and a one-page self-review. Screenshots alone are not acceptable evidence.

Common critical failures to avoid (from the guide):

  • Treating the digital-engineering initiative as a tool-selection project.
  • Assuming the Northstar product boundary equals the mission boundary.
  • Declaring inherited data or models authoritative without review.
  • Omitting decision owners, timing, consequence, or information restrictions.

Mastery and revision standard:

Revise until every critical decision has an owner, evidence need, timing, consequence, and uncertainty statement; mission and product boundaries are distinct; and inherited authority claims are dispositioned. Score at least 80% and answer four of five questions correctly.
