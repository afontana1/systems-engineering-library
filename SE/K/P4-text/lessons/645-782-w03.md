# EN.645.782 · Week 03 — M&S foundations, lifecycle uses, and credibility

**Status:** Original companion explanation plus adapted excerpts from the supplied Week 3 learning guide.

## Why this idea matters

A systems engineer must produce a decision that remains defensible when the conditions change. This chapter focuses on **modeling and simulation credibility**: how to reason from purpose and evidence to a bounded action. The objective is not to memorize a term, but to state what it permits, when it fails, and what observable evidence would change your conclusion.

## Build the mental model

A model is a purposeful abstraction. Describe intended use, conceptual assumptions, equations or rules, input pedigree, solver limitations, validation comparisons and the resulting decision envelope. Verification asks whether implementation conforms to the model specification; validation asks whether the model is sufficiently representative for intended use. The credibility burden rises with consequence and sensitivity of the decision.

**Useful diagnostic distinction:** An executable model can be internally correct and operationally unsuitable.

## The underlying mechanisms: lessons to retain

The following instructional notes are adapted from the corresponding original course guide. They form a compact mechanism checklist to use while reading the new explanation and doing the chapter exercise.

* A model’s credibility is not an intrinsic badge. It is a judgment relative to intended use, context, evidence, uncertainty, and consequence.
* Model fidelity should be sufficient and purposeful. Excess fidelity can increase cost, obscure uncertainty, and delay learning.
* Reuse requires context comparison: purpose, assumptions, input domain, environment, time, version, data, and output interpretation.
* Composing individually credible models does not guarantee a credible federation or co-simulation. Interfaces, timing, semantics, and coupled uncertainty require evaluation.
* Accreditation or acceptance is a decision by an accountable authority that evidence is sufficient for a use; it is not another word for validation.

## Worked reasoning: the Northstar mobility case

An evacuation simulator predicts a seven-minute clearance but omits wheelchair boarding delays. Its code may be verified, yet its validation evidence cannot support inclusive performance claims. Challenge the omitted mechanism with data, bounding analyses and scenarios.

### Compare with the guide's worked case

The original guide uses the following short case discussion. Reproduce the reasoning with a changed assumption rather than copying its conclusion.

A fleet-charging simulation validated for normal weekday planning is proposed for emergency outage routing. The example compares intended uses, input ranges, demand behavior, grid assumptions, operator actions, and consequence. It concludes that parts of the model can be reused, but emergency demand, communication loss, and manual-override behavior require new validation and uncertainty treatment.

Reproduce the example using controlled course data, change one assumption, and explain how the decision or evidence changes.

### How to challenge the example

1. **Boundary:** Name the engineered product, process, actors and environment relevant to this claim. List one important exclusion.
2. **Evidence:** Identify one source that would let an independent reviewer reproduce or falsify the result.
3. **Failure mode:** Test what happens when an input is stale, unavailable, biased, incorrectly configured or in conflict with another artifact.
4. **Decision:** State who can authorize the next action, which guardrail could block it, and how the decision will be recorded.

## Learning objectives from the original guide

* Distinguish conceptual, descriptive, analytic, physics-based, statistical, discrete-event, agent-based, human-in-the-loop, and live/virtual/constructive representations.
* Define intended use, decision consequence, required fidelity, input data, outputs, and uncertainty for each model.
* Relate M&S uses to concept, requirements, design, integration, test, operations, sustainment, and mission analysis.
* Construct a proportionate verification, validation, and accreditation or acceptance plan.
* Identify composition, reuse, transformation, and extrapolation risks in a model portfolio.

## Practice questions with guided answers

Attempt the questions before expanding each answer. These five knowledge checks and their answer guidance are **adapted from the supplied Week 3 course guide**; their inclusion does not replace the larger independent assignment.

1. **What is model verification?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Evidence that the implementation correctly represents the conceptual model and specified mathematics/logic.

   </details>

2. **What is model validation?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Evidence that the model adequately represents the real-world referent for an intended use.

   </details>

3. **What is accreditation or acceptance?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   An accountable decision that the model is acceptable for a specified use, based on evidence and risk.

   </details>

4. **Why is higher fidelity not always better?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   It can add cost, parameters, opacity, and delay without changing the decision or reducing dominant uncertainty.

   </details>

5. **What makes reuse risky?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Differences in purpose, context, domain, assumptions, data, version, interfaces, or consequence.

   </details>

## Extended transfer exercise

Construct a one-page **claim–evidence–decision map** for this week's topic. Include a claim, current baseline, configuration or version, measurable response, source and timestamp of evidence, alternative explanation, reviewer, unresolved uncertainty and stop/go rule. Then deliberately inject one defect (missing source, wrong version, unexpected consequence or inadequate coverage) and explain how the decision should change.

**Stretch:** Use a second scenario with a different consequence level or stakeholder group. Explain why the same method may require different assurance controls.

## Bridge to the assessed curriculum

The companion explains the concepts; the original guide supplies the complete assignment, sources, rubric and formal mastery gate. This is the guide's weekly deliverable:

**Model and Simulation Portfolio, Intended-Use, and Credibility Package** containing taxonomy, lifecycle matrix, model register, reuse assessment, credibility plans, accreditation/acceptance authorities, risks, and limits. Length: 12–16 pages plus controlled registers.

Submit native or textual source, data and transformations, generated views, query/analysis source, a change log, and a one-page self-review. Screenshots alone are not acceptable evidence.

**Common critical failures to avoid (from the guide):**

* Calling a model validated without naming the intended use.
* Using fidelity as a synonym for accuracy or credibility.
* Reusing a model outside its validated domain without analysis.
* Omitting uncertainty, decision consequence, or accountable acceptance authority.

**Mastery and revision standard:**

No model may enter the selected use-case portfolio without an intended-use statement, owner, configuration, input/output definition, credibility status, uncertainty/limitations, and acceptance authority. Score at least 80%.
