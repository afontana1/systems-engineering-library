# EN.645.780 · Week 02 — Assess project characteristics and tailor the lifecycle, cadence, and controls

**Phase 4 Companion Textbook · Agile Systems Engineering**

**Status:** Original companion explanation plus adapted excerpts from the supplied Week 2 learning guide.

## Why this idea matters

A systems engineer must produce a decision that remains defensible when the conditions change. This chapter focuses on **tailoring lifecycles to consequence and uncertainty**: how to reason from purpose and evidence to a bounded action. The objective is not to memorize a term, but to state what it permits, when it fails, and what observable evidence would change your conclusion.

## Build the mental model

Methods fit decisions, not organizational fashion. Use **uncertainty** (what is unknown), **consequence** (cost of being wrong), **coupling** (what else changes), and **reversibility** (how costly reversal would be) to choose feedback cadence. A high-uncertainty, reversible user-interface decision can be prototyped quickly. A high-consequence braking controller demands early architecture and independent verification even when developed in iterations. A hybrid lifecycle retains controlled interfaces and assurance gates while varying the frequency of learning.

**Useful diagnostic distinction:** “Agile or waterfall” is a false binary; tailor individual commitments and controls.

## The underlying mechanisms: lessons to retain

The following instructional notes are adapted from the corresponding original course guide. They form a compact mechanism checklist to use while reading the new explanation and doing the chapter exercise.

* Project characteristics govern the viable operating model. Important variables include consequence of error, rate of external change, ability to modularize, feedback latency, asset cost, and regulatory evidence.
* Tailoring is a documented engineering decision. It identifies what is retained, modified, automated, combined, moved earlier, made event-driven, or removed, and why.
* High-consequence work benefits from short evidence loops but may require stronger independence, configuration control, and authorization—not weaker rigor.
* Hardware and suppliers do not prevent agility. They change the feasible increment, simulation strategy, interface discipline, option strategy, and commitment horizon.
* A single program may use flow for defects, timeboxed learning for uncertain design, sequential qualification for a physical item, and staged operational deployment.
* The result should be a portfolio of lifecycle patterns tied to work characteristics, not one framework imposed everywhere.

## Worked reasoning: the Northstar mobility case

Evaluate two Northstar changes: dispatch text labels and emergency-stop wiring. For labels, build two prototypes and collect accessibility feedback before freezing. For wiring, freeze critical interface parameters, test on a rig, and authorize change only after hazard disposition. Both can receive iterative feedback, but their release rules differ.

### Compare with the guide's worked case

The original guide uses the following short case discussion. Reproduce the reasoning with a changed assumption rather than copying its conclusion.

The autonomy perception algorithm can be updated in two-week learning loops using recorded data and simulation, while a battery enclosure tooling decision requires staged commitment because tooling is expensive and the supplier lead time is 24 weeks. An interface emulator and mass/thermal surrogate allow early integration evidence before hardware arrival. The lifecycle therefore mixes frequent software increments, monthly integrated demonstrations, and controlled hardware decision gates.

### How to challenge the example

1. **Boundary:** Name the engineered product, process, actors and environment relevant to this claim. List one important exclusion.
2. **Evidence:** Identify one source that would let an independent reviewer reproduce or falsify the result.
3. **Failure mode:** Test what happens when an input is stale, unavailable, biased, incorrectly configured or in conflict with another artifact.
4. **Decision:** State who can authorize the next action, which guardrail could block it, and how the decision will be recorded.

## Learning objectives from the original guide

* Assess uncertainty, novelty, modularity, integration frequency, criticality, compliance, supplier structure, and hardware lead time.
* Differentiate reversible from hard-to-reverse decisions and align commitment timing accordingly.
* Compare sequential, iterative, incremental, spiral, flow-based, and hybrid patterns for portions of one program.
* Create a tailoring matrix that preserves required controls while shortening feedback.
* Defend where agile methods are unsuitable, premature, or limited.

## Practice questions with guided answers

Attempt the questions before expanding each answer. These five knowledge checks and their answer guidance are **adapted from the supplied Week 2 course guide**; their inclusion does not replace the larger independent assignment.

1. **What does project-characteristics-govern mean?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   The context determines which agile/Lean strategies and controls are appropriate; method selection follows the engineering characteristics.

   </details>

2. **Why separate release cadence from iteration cadence?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Learning and build cycles can occur more frequently than authorized release or operational deployment.

   </details>

3. **What is tailoring?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   A documented, risk-aware adaptation of lifecycle processes and controls to context, with rationale and evidence responsibilities.

   </details>

4. **Why is reversibility important?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   It informs how long options can remain open and how much evidence is needed before commitment.

   </details>

5. **Does long-lead hardware make agile SE impossible?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   No; it requires architecture, surrogates, early interfaces, staged commitments, and cadences appropriate to the dependency.

   </details>

The companion explains the concepts; the original guide supplies the complete assignment, sources, rubric and formal mastery gate. This is the guide's weekly deliverable:

**Agile Suitability and Tailoring Review Package** with characteristics matrix, reversibility analysis, lifecycle pattern map, tailored controls, rejected alternatives, and a 12-slide review briefing.

**Common critical failures to avoid (from the guide):**

* Applying one cadence or framework to all work.
* Removing a required assurance or configuration control without replacement evidence.
* Ignoring long-lead hardware, suppliers, or integration dependencies.
* Claiming a practice is agile solely because it is iterative.

**Mastery and revision standard:**

Every workstream must have a defensible cadence and control pattern, with no critical evidence obligation deleted. Resolve all review findings rated major before proceeding.
