# EN.645.780 · Week 04 — Design common-mission, self-managing teams with explicit technical leadership and decision rights

## Why this idea matters

A systems engineer must produce a decision that remains defensible when the conditions change. This chapter focuses on **teams, decision rights, and technical independence**: how to reason from purpose and evidence to a bounded action. The objective is not to memorize a term, but to state what it permits, when it fails, and what observable evidence would change your conclusion.

## Build the mental model

A cross-functional team needs access to design, test, operations and supplier evidence, but **autonomy is bounded by decision rights**. Define who may explore, recommend, approve baselines, accept residual risk, and authorize operation. RACI-style role charts are helpful only if they resolve actual ambiguity. Independent assurance may belong outside the sprint team even when engaged continuously. A fast local decision that breaks a shared interface is system-level rework.

**Useful diagnostic distinction:** A self-managing team is not an unaccountable team.

## The underlying mechanisms: lessons to retain

The following instructional notes are adapted from the corresponding original course guide. They form a compact mechanism checklist to use while reading the new explanation and doing the chapter exercise.

* Self-managing means the team decides how to accomplish bounded work. It does not mean the team defines its own mission, ignores interfaces, or self-approves independent evidence.
* Team topology and system architecture influence one another. Persistent cross-team dependencies are architecture and ownership signals.
* Technical leadership maintains intent, interfaces, integrity, capability strategy, evidence expectations, and escalation—not task assignment for every item.
* Decision rights should state decision type, bounded authority, required evidence, required consultation, notification, appeal, and time limit.
* Specialty engineers should be integrated early enough to shape work, not used as late compliance gates.
* Psychological safety supports surfacing uncertainty and defects, but it does not remove accountability for evidence and decisions.

## Worked reasoning: the Northstar mobility case

An agile dispatch team wants to change the vehicle telemetry schema. Its lead can approve an experimental branch, but the shared-interface owner must authorize the production schema change after downstream replay tests. Write both decisions as explicit rules, with response-time commitments.

### Compare with the guide's worked case

The original guide uses the following short case discussion. Reproduce the reasoning with a changed assumption rather than copying its conclusion.

NMS has separate software, vehicle hardware, safety, cybersecurity, and test departments. Every interface change requires five sequential approvals. The redesigned operating model creates a mobility-capability team with embedded software, hardware, test, operations, and safety participation; a platform team owns shared simulation and CI assets; a technical council handles only cross-capability or threshold decisions. A decision-rights table allows the capability team to approve changes inside an interface and risk envelope, while changes to safety assumptions require independent review.

### How to challenge the example

1. **Boundary:** Name the engineered product, process, actors and environment relevant to this claim. List one important exclusion.
2. **Evidence:** Identify one source that would let an independent reviewer reproduce or falsify the result.
3. **Failure mode:** Test what happens when an input is stale, unavailable, biased, incorrectly configured or in conflict with another artifact.
4. **Decision:** State who can authorize the next action, which guardrail could block it, and how the decision will be recorded.

## Learning objectives from the original guide

* Distinguish self-organizing, self-managing, cross-functional, component, platform, enabling, and integration teams.
* Design team boundaries that align with architecture, value streams, and cognitive load.
* Define decision rights, consultation, escalation, and independent assurance responsibilities.
* Create technical-leadership cadences that guide without becoming approval queues.
* Analyze supplier, regulator, operator, and specialty-engineering participation in common-mission teaming.

## Practice questions with guided answers

Attempt the questions before expanding each answer. These five knowledge checks and their answer guidance are **adapted from the supplied Week 4 course guide**; their inclusion does not replace the larger independent assignment.

1. **What is bounded authority?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Explicit authority within defined mission, architecture, risk, cost, and evidence limits.

   </details>

2. **Why align teams and architecture?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Frequent dependencies and handoffs increase coordination cost; clearer modular ownership can improve flow and integrity.

   </details>

3. **What remains a technical-leadership responsibility?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   System intent, architecture integrity, cross-team interfaces, evidence strategy, threshold decisions, and escalation.

   </details>

4. **Can assurance be embedded and independent?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Yes; specialists can participate continuously while specified approval or assessment decisions retain independence.

   </details>

5. **What is common-mission teaming?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Coherent collaboration and cooperation among relevant stakeholders toward a shared outcome, not merely colocated work.

   </details>

## Bridge to the assessed curriculum

The companion explains the concepts; the original guide supplies the complete assignment, sources, rubric and formal mastery gate. This is the guide's weekly deliverable:

**Common-Mission Team Operating Model** with team topology, architecture/value-stream alignment, charters, decision rights, assurance independence, supplier/stakeholder participation, cadences, escalation paths, and recorded 12-minute team briefing.

Submit controlled source files, data/calculations, generated views, a change log, and a one-page self-review against the rubric. Screenshots alone are not acceptable evidence.

**Common critical failures to avoid (from the guide):**

* Treating self-management as absence of accountability.
* Giving a delivery team authority to waive independent safety/security evidence.
* Creating team boundaries that ignore dominant technical dependencies.
* Leaving cross-team interface ownership undefined.

**Mastery and revision standard:**

Every critical interface and decision class must have an owner and escalation path. Independent assurance responsibilities must remain explicit. Resolve role conflicts and missing competencies before Week 5.
