# EN.645.780 · Week 09 — Build quality, safety, security, and verification into every increment

**Phase 4 Companion Textbook · Agile Systems Engineering**  
**Primary concept:** Continuous built-in assurance  
**Status:** Original companion explanation plus adapted excerpts from the supplied Week 9 learning guide.  
**Data:** Worked numerical values are synthetic teaching illustrations, not field observations.

## Why this idea matters

A systems engineer must produce a decision that remains defensible when the conditions change. This chapter focuses on **continuous built-in assurance**: how to reason from purpose and evidence to a bounded action. The objective is not to memorize a term, but to state what it permits, when it fails, and what observable evidence would change your conclusion.

## Build the mental model

Quality and safety must be designed into increments: acceptance criteria, hazard-control claims, configuration-specific tests, and independent disposition of critical findings. Automated tests can detect specified failures early, but passing them proves only what those tests cover under their assumptions. Maintain a claim–argument–evidence chain, including negative and missing evidence. Define stop-the-line conditions in advance.

**Useful diagnostic distinction:** A green continuous-integration dashboard is not a safety case.

## The underlying mechanisms: lessons to retain

The following instructional notes are adapted from the corresponding original course guide. They form a compact mechanism checklist to use while reading the new explanation and doing the chapter exercise.

* Built-in quality means quality and assurance criteria shape the work and evidence from the beginning; it does not mean every check is automated.
* Definition of done is use-context dependent. “Ready for simulation,” “ready for integrated lab,” “ready for pilot,” and “ready for unrestricted operation” require different evidence.
* An evidence backlog makes missing analyses, tests, reviews, and data explicit and prioritized with the associated capability.
* Shift-left reduces expensive late discovery; shift-right operational observation detects behavior that predeployment evidence cannot fully establish.
* Independent assessment can be continuous and risk based. Independence should be preserved where required without creating a late surprise gate.
* Metrics should expose defect age, evidence latency, coverage gaps, rework, escapes, and residual risk—not reward superficial test count.

## Worked reasoning: the Northstar mobility case

A new dispatch rule passes 500 simulated rides but has no evidence for emergency-stop arbitration in degraded communications. The safety claim remains unsupported. Add a fault-injection test, review its assumptions, and require authorization before field release.

### Compare with the guide's worked case

The original guide uses the following short case discussion. Reproduce the reasoning with a changed assumption rather than copying its conclusion.

A software change passes unit and service tests but alters braking-command timing under degraded network conditions. The increment’s integrated definition of done therefore requires interface conformance, timing analysis, simulation scenarios, HIL evidence, safety-impact disposition, cybersecurity scan, trace update, and configuration capture before pilot authorization. Lower-tier evidence can still support earlier learning without mislabeling it operationally ready.

Reproduce the example with the supplied case data, then change one assumption and explain the effect on the conclusion.

### How to challenge the example

1. **Boundary:** Name the engineered product, process, actors and environment relevant to this claim. List one important exclusion.
2. **Evidence:** Identify one source that would let an independent reviewer reproduce or falsify the result.
3. **Failure mode:** Test what happens when an input is stale, unavailable, biased, incorrectly configured or in conflict with another artifact.
4. **Decision:** State who can authorize the next action, which guardrail could block it, and how the decision will be recorded.

## Learning objectives from the original guide

* Define built-in quality for cyber-physical systems beyond automated unit testing.
* Create an evidence backlog and definition-of-done tiers for model, software, hardware, integrated, and operational increments.
* Integrate safety, security, reliability, human factors, privacy, and regulatory evidence into learning intervals.
* Design risk-based independent review and release/authorization policies.
* Use defect, evidence-age, coverage, and escape metrics without creating perverse incentives.

## Practice questions with guided answers

Attempt the questions before expanding each answer. These five knowledge checks and their answer guidance are **adapted from the supplied Week 9 course guide**; their inclusion does not replace the larger independent assignment.

1. **What is built-in quality?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Designing work, architecture, criteria, and evidence so quality and assurance are created continuously rather than inspected in at the end.

   </details>

2. **Why use readiness tiers?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Evidence sufficiency depends on the next intended use; learning, lab integration, pilot, and unrestricted operation differ.

   </details>

3. **What is an evidence backlog?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Controlled missing or planned evidence linked to decisions, work, requirements, risk, and intended use.

   </details>

4. **What is shift-right?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Use of operational or production observation to detect and learn from behavior after controlled deployment, with safeguards.

   </details>

5. **Why can independent assurance coexist with agile delivery?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Independence concerns judgment and authority, not necessarily late timing; reviewers can engage continuously.

   </details>

## Extended transfer exercise

Construct a one-page **claim–evidence–decision map** for this week's topic. Include a claim, current baseline, configuration or version, measurable response, source and timestamp of evidence, alternative explanation, reviewer, unresolved uncertainty and stop/go rule. Then deliberately inject one defect (missing source, wrong version, unexpected consequence or inadequate coverage) and explain how the decision should change.

**Stretch:** Use a second scenario with a different consequence level or stakeholder group. Explain why the same method may require different assurance controls.

## Bridge to the assessed curriculum

The companion explains the concepts; the original guide supplies the complete assignment, sources, rubric and formal mastery gate. This is the guide's weekly deliverable:

**Continuous Quality and Assurance Package** with readiness tiers, definitions of done, evidence backlog, SSDF mapping, safety/security integration, assurance responsibilities, metrics, gap analysis, and a 1,200-word executive essay on speed versus rigor as a false trade.

Submit controlled source files, data/calculations, generated views, a change log, and a one-page self-review against the rubric. Screenshots alone are not acceptable evidence.

**Common critical failures to avoid (from the guide):**

* Calling an increment done without naming its intended use.
* Deferring safety or security to a final hardening phase.
* Counting tests without evaluating coverage and evidence quality.
* Allowing schedule pressure to silently waive critical evidence.

**Mastery and revision standard:**

Every critical work item must have a defined evidence path and readiness tier. No safety- or security-critical evidence gap may be hidden in a generic technical-debt category.
