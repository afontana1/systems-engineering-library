# EN.645.782 · Week 11 — Mission analysis, alternatives, gaps, and interventions

**Phase 4 Companion Textbook · Foundations of Digital and Mission Engineering**  
**Primary concept:** Mission trade space and intervention choice  
**Status:** Original companion explanation plus adapted excerpts from the supplied Week 11 learning guide.  
**Data:** Worked numerical values are synthetic teaching illustrations, not field observations.

## Why this idea matters

A systems engineer must produce a decision that remains defensible when the conditions change. This chapter focuses on **mission trade space and intervention choice**: how to reason from purpose and evidence to a bounded action. The objective is not to memorize a term, but to state what it permits, when it fails, and what observable evidence would change your conclusion.

## Build the mental model

Analyze mission alternatives that include technical, procedural and organizational interventions. Define baseline scenarios, exogenous threats, measures of effectiveness, uncertainty distributions, and constraints. Evaluate robustness across scenarios and distinguish gaps from root causes. A large numerical gain in one mission scenario may be outweighed by degradation in another.

**Useful diagnostic distinction:** More equipment is not always the dominant mission intervention.

## The underlying mechanisms: lessons to retain

The following instructional notes are adapted from the corresponding original course guide. They form a compact mechanism checklist to use while reading the new explanation and doing the chapter exercise.

* Mission analysis should compare alternatives under multiple plausible scenarios and uncertainties, not optimize one nominal case.
* Interventions may be materiel or non-materiel. Training, policy, staffing, organization, data-sharing, and procedures can dominate a technology upgrade.
* Architecture should be updated with analysis results and evidence rather than left as a static pre-analysis picture.
* Transparency requires recording assumptions, constraints, source data, models, versions, scenarios, uncertainty, and decision logic.
* Robust recommendations acknowledge tradeoffs, implementation dependencies, residual risk, and conditions that change the decision.

## Worked reasoning: the Northstar mobility case

Compare three options: purchase two shuttles, schedule accessible boarding staff, or change evacuation staging. Synthetic scenario runs show staffing helps high-accessibility-demand conditions more than fleet expansion. Preserve data and failure cases before selecting an intervention.

### Compare with the guide's worked case

The original guide uses the following short case discussion. Reproduce the reasoning with a changed assumption rather than copying its conclusion.

Four alternatives are compared: add autonomous vehicles, establish mutual-aid dispatch, install mobile charging, and pre-register mobility-dependent riders with privacy controls. The example shows that the highest-performing technology option under nominal conditions is not the most robust under grid and communications degradation; a combined policy/data-sharing and mutual-aid intervention performs better across scenarios.

Reproduce the example using controlled course data, change one assumption, and explain how the decision or evidence changes.

### How to challenge the example

1. **Boundary:** Name the engineered product, process, actors and environment relevant to this claim. List one important exclusion.
2. **Evidence:** Identify one source that would let an independent reviewer reproduce or falsify the result.
3. **Failure mode:** Test what happens when an input is stale, unavailable, biased, incorrectly configured or in conflict with another artifact.
4. **Decision:** State who can authorize the next action, which guardrail could block it, and how the decision will be recorded.

## Learning objectives from the original guide

* Derive mission-analysis questions, alternatives, factors, scenarios, experiments, and decision criteria.
* Connect mission architecture to simulations, data, analytic models, and uncertainty.
* Identify capability gaps, redundancies, bottlenecks, fragilities, and opportunities.
* Compare technology, system, process, organization, staffing, training, policy, and information interventions.
* Assess robustness, sensitivity, feasibility, implementation, and residual mission risk.

## Practice questions with guided answers

Attempt the questions before expanding each answer. These five knowledge checks and their answer guidance are **adapted from the supplied Week 11 course guide**; their inclusion does not replace the larger independent assignment.

1. **Why include non-materiel interventions?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Mission outcomes depend on doctrine/policy, organization, training, leadership, personnel, facilities, information, and processes as well as technology.

   </details>

2. **What is a robust recommendation?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   One that performs acceptably across plausible scenarios/uncertainties and has understood tradeoffs and reversal conditions.

   </details>

3. **Why update the architecture after analysis?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Analysis changes understanding of dependencies, performance, gaps, assumptions, and recommended structure/behavior.

   </details>

4. **What is decision reversal analysis?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Identification of changes in weights, assumptions, scenarios, or data that would change the preferred alternative.

   </details>

5. **What is the role of experimentation?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   To reduce important uncertainty, test concepts and interactions, validate models, and inform implementation.

   </details>

## Extended transfer exercise

Construct a one-page **claim–evidence–decision map** for this week's topic. Include a claim, current baseline, configuration or version, measurable response, source and timestamp of evidence, alternative explanation, reviewer, unresolved uncertainty and stop/go rule. Then deliberately inject one defect (missing source, wrong version, unexpected consequence or inadequate coverage) and explain how the decision should change.

**Stretch:** Use a second scenario with a different consequence level or stakeholder group. Explain why the same method may require different assurance controls.

## Bridge to the assessed curriculum

The companion explains the concepts; the original guide supplies the complete assignment, sources, rubric and formal mastery gate. This is the guide's weekly deliverable:

**Mission Analysis, Alternatives, and Recommendation Package** containing questions, objectives, measures, alternatives, intervention portfolios, scenarios, model/data links, experiments, results, uncertainty/sensitivity, robustness, feasibility, implementation dependencies, architecture updates, recommendation, residual risk, and review findings.

Submit native or textual source, data and transformations, generated views, query/analysis source, a change log, and a one-page self-review. Screenshots alone are not acceptable evidence.

**Common critical failures to avoid (from the guide):**

* Comparing only product or technology alternatives.
* Optimizing one nominal scenario without degraded conditions or uncertainty.
* Using analytic inputs that lack source, version, or architecture linkage.
* Recommending an option without implementation dependencies and residual risk.

**Mastery and revision standard:**

The recommendation must compare materiel and non-materiel portfolios, remain defensible across degraded cases, expose decision-reversal conditions, link results to architecture and authoritative sources, and resolve the controlled communications-degradation event.
