## Why this idea matters

A systems engineer must produce a decision that remains defensible when the conditions change. This chapter focuses on **capability evidence and causal analysis**: how to reason from purpose and evidence to a bounded action. The objective is not to memorize a term, but to state what it permits, when it fails, and what observable evidence would change your conclusion.

## Build the mental model

Capability assessment asks whether a process can reliably deliver expected outcomes under real conditions. Root-cause analysis must distinguish correlated symptoms from causal mechanisms. Triangulate interviews, traces, data and controlled intervention. Avoid scoring maturity without recording what the score means, the evidence sampled, and whether performance results agree.

**Useful diagnostic distinction:** A plausible cause is only a hypothesis until the evidence discriminates it from alternatives.

## The underlying mechanisms: lessons to retain

The following instructional notes are adapted from the corresponding original course guide. They form a compact mechanism checklist to use while reading the new explanation and doing the chapter exercise.

* Assessment should answer a decision question and use defined criteria, sampling, evidence, and limitations.
* Capability ratings summarize process attributes; they do not explain every performance result.
* Root-cause analysis is hypothesis generation and testing, not a ritual that guarantees one “root.”
* Causal loops reveal reinforcing behavior such as backlog → schedule pressure → bypass → rework → backlog.
* Findings should state condition, criterion, evidence, consequence, confidence, and recommended next analysis.

## Worked reasoning: the Northstar mobility case

A Pareto chart shows most late cases await board review. Interviews reveal missing supplier evidence creates repeated resubmissions. Simply adding meetings will not solve the intake-quality mechanism. Test completeness criteria before changing board frequency.

### Compare with the guide's worked case

The original guide uses the following short case discussion. Reproduce the reasoning with a changed assumption rather than copying its conclusion.

The initial cause statement blames slow reviewers. Data shows reviewer touch time is modest; the dominant delay is waiting for complete impact evidence. A causal analysis finds unclear evidence requirements, fragmented repositories, late supplier input, batch board cadence, and incentives to submit early. The improvement target shifts from reviewer utilization to evidence readiness and flow.

Reproduce the example using controlled course data, change one assumption, and explain how the result or decision changes.

### How to challenge the example

1. **Boundary:** Name the engineered product, process, actors and environment relevant to this claim. List one important exclusion.
2. **Evidence:** Identify one source that would let an independent reviewer reproduce or falsify the result.
3. **Failure mode:** Test what happens when an input is stale, unavailable, biased, incorrectly configured or in conflict with another artifact.
4. **Decision:** State who can authorize the next action, which guardrail could block it, and how the decision will be recorded.

## Learning objectives from the original guide

* Construct a bounded capability and maturity assessment without claiming formal appraisal status.
* Rate evidence quality and confidence for process findings.
* Use Pareto, cause-and-effect, 5-why, fault-tree, and causal-loop reasoning appropriately.
* Distinguish proximal cause, systemic cause, constraint, and correlation.
* Integrate standards, process, data, stakeholder, and risk evidence in a Current-State Process Assessment.

## Practice questions with guided answers

Attempt the questions before expanding each answer. These five knowledge checks and their answer guidance are **adapted from the supplied Week 6 course guide**; their inclusion does not replace the larger independent assignment.

1. **What is a process finding?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   A traceable statement of condition versus criterion, supported by evidence and consequence.

   </details>

2. **Why rate evidence confidence?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   To prevent weak evidence from carrying the same decision weight as reproducible, triangulated evidence.

   </details>

3. **What is a systemic cause?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   A structure, policy, incentive, dependency, or feedback condition that repeatedly produces the outcome.

   </details>

4. **Why use multiple cause tools?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Different tools reveal frequency, hierarchy, mechanism, feedback, or failure paths.

   </details>

5. **What is the current-state review decision?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Whether evidence is sufficient to authorize modeling and future-state design, and what limitations must remain.

   </details>

## Extended transfer exercise

Construct a one-page **claim–evidence–decision map** for this week's topic. Include a claim, current baseline, configuration or version, measurable response, source and timestamp of evidence, alternative explanation, reviewer, unresolved uncertainty and stop/go rule. Then deliberately inject one defect (missing source, wrong version, unexpected consequence or inadequate coverage) and explain how the decision should change.

**Stretch:** Use a second scenario with a different consequence level or stakeholder group. Explain why the same method may require different assurance controls.

## Bridge to the assessed curriculum

The companion explains the concepts; the original guide supplies the complete assignment, sources, rubric and formal mastery gate. This is the guide's weekly deliverable:

**Current-State Process Assessment** containing assessment scope/method, criteria, evidence profile, capability and performance findings, causal analysis, constraints, risks, improvement opportunity backlog, limitations, review findings, and dispositions. Complete the closed-book/open-artifact midcourse exam.

Submit native or textual source, controlled data and transformations, generated views, a change log, and a one-page self-review. Screenshots alone are not acceptable evidence.

**Common critical failures to avoid (from the guide):**

* Claiming formal certification, appraisal, or maturity level.
* Using one anecdote or one metric as the sole cause evidence.
* Blaming individuals without examining system conditions.
* Proceeding to redesign with unresolved critical data or model credibility gaps.

**Mastery and revision standard:**

Pass the midcourse exam and Current-State Process Assessment with no critical open finding on evidence integrity, cause reasoning, or stakeholder risk.
