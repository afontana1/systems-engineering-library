## Why this idea matters

A systems engineer must produce a decision that remains defensible when the conditions change. This chapter focuses on **sysml and oosem with lightweight purpose**: how to reason from purpose and evidence to a bounded action. The objective is not to memorize a term, but to state what it permits, when it fails, and what observable evidence would change your conclusion.

## Build the mental model

SysML offers ways to represent structure, behavior, requirements and parametric constraints, while OOSEM is a systems-analysis method rather than a file format. Begin with black-box scenarios and capabilities; then define functions and logical responsibilities before premature physical allocation. Keep relationships queryable and distinguish a logical interface from a hardware pin or software endpoint.

**Useful diagnostic distinction:** Diagram types are views of a reasoning process, not a checklist to complete.

## The underlying mechanisms: lessons to retain

The following instructional notes are adapted from the corresponding original course guide. They form a compact mechanism checklist to use while reading the new explanation and doing the chapter exercise.

* OOSEM is used here as a reasoning flow, not as a rigid universal process.
* Mission tasks describe what must occur in the mission; capabilities describe the ability needed; system functions describe what a system does; components implement behavior.
* Logical architecture should remain solution-neutral long enough to preserve alternatives and expose dependencies.
* State and degraded-mode models are essential when mission outcomes depend on synchronization, communications, power, operator authority, or recovery.
* Model validation includes semantic and cross-view consistency, not merely syntax.

## Worked reasoning: the Northstar mobility case

For the boarding mission thread, a black-box activity “authorize safe departure” is allocated to sensors, vehicle controller and operator intervention rules. This reveals a missing degraded-state decision before selecting device models.

The mission task “Maintain accessible evacuation mobility” is decomposed into detect demand, prioritize riders, allocate capacity, communicate assignment, transport, and confirm completion. The example maps capabilities and participating systems without prematurely assigning all behavior to Northstar vehicles. ### How to challenge the example

1. **Boundary:** Name the engineered product, process, actors and environment relevant to this claim. List one important exclusion.
2. **Evidence:** Identify one source that would let an independent reviewer reproduce or falsify the result.
3. **Failure mode:** Test what happens when an input is stale, unavailable, biased, incorrectly configured or in conflict with another artifact.
4. **Decision:** State who can authorize the next action, which guardrail could block it, and how the decision will be recorded.

## Learning objectives from the original guide

* Apply a lightweight OOSEM flow from stakeholder and mission analysis through logical and physical architecture.
* Construct SysML representations of requirements, behavior, structure, interfaces, states, measures, analysis, and verification.
* Separate mission tasks and capability needs from system functions and design elements.
* Allocate behavior and requirements while preserving rationale and alternatives.
* Validate model consistency across scenarios, requirements, behavior, structure, interfaces, and measures.

## Practice questions with guided answers

Attempt the questions before expanding each answer. These five knowledge checks and their answer guidance are **adapted from the supplied Week 5 course guide**; their inclusion does not replace the larger independent assignment.

1. **Why separate mission tasks from system functions?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   It preserves mission-level reasoning, alternatives, and multi-system contributions.

   </details>

2. **What does a logical architecture provide?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   A solution-neutral organization of behavior, information, and responsibilities before physical implementation.

   </details>

3. **Why model states?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   State-dependent behavior, authority, timing, degradation, and recovery cannot be inferred reliably from static structure.

   </details>

4. **What is an allocation rationale?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Evidence and reasoning for assigning behavior, requirements, or responsibilities to architecture elements.

   </details>

5. **What is a semantic model defect?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   An element or relationship whose meaning is wrong, ambiguous, inconsistent, or unsupported even if the diagram is syntactically valid.

   </details>

## Extended transfer exercise

Construct a one-page **claim–evidence–decision map** for this week's topic. Include a claim, current baseline, configuration or version, measurable response, source and timestamp of evidence, alternative explanation, reviewer, unresolved uncertainty and stop/go rule. Then deliberately inject one defect (missing source, wrong version, unexpected consequence or inadequate coverage) and explain how the decision should change.

**Stretch:** Use a second scenario with a different consequence level or stakeholder group. Explain why the same method may require different assurance controls.

**Common critical failures to avoid (from the guide):**

* Assigning every mission task directly to the Northstar product.
* Using one diagram type to represent all semantics.
* Omitting degraded/off-nominal behavior or interface information.
* Closing validation findings without correcting model content.
