# EN.645.783 · Week 05 — Measurement system, variation, and baseline performance

## Why this idea matters

A systems engineer must produce a decision that remains defensible when the conditions change. This chapter focuses on **measurement validity, distribution and drift**: how to reason from purpose and evidence to a bounded action. The objective is not to memorize a term, but to state what it permits, when it fails, and what observable evidence would change your conclusion.

## Build the mental model

Operational process data commonly have skew, censoring and subgroup effects. Report medians and upper-tail lead times, defect escape, evidence completeness and stakeholder impact, with explicit definitions and denominators. Validate measurement consistency across teams and periods. Distinguish common-cause process variation from a special event; do not recalculate baselines opportunistically after every improvement.

**Useful diagnostic distinction:** A lower average is not always a better process.

## The underlying mechanisms: lessons to retain

The following instructional notes are adapted from the corresponding original course guide. They form a compact mechanism checklist to use while reading the new explanation and doing the chapter exercise.

* Measures should support questions and decisions; collecting data without a use case creates burden and noise.
* Median alone hides tails, multimodality, blocked cases, and changes in work mix.
* A control limit is estimated from process behavior; a specification or target comes from need or policy.
* Process performance should be stratified by risk class, source, subsystem, supplier, and path when justified.
* Balance speed with first-pass quality, escaped defects, residual risk, evidence completeness, workload, and stakeholder experience.

## Worked reasoning: the Northstar mobility case

A mean lead time falls from 30 to 22 days, but the 90th percentile grows from 70 to 86 days for high-risk changes. A broad average hides a worsening tail. Segment by change risk and report both service and assurance guardrails.

### How to challenge the example

1. **Boundary:** Name the engineered product, process, actors and environment relevant to this claim. List one important exclusion.
2. **Evidence:** Identify one source that would let an independent reviewer reproduce or falsify the result.
3. **Failure mode:** Test what happens when an input is stale, unavailable, biased, incorrectly configured or in conflict with another artifact.
4. **Decision:** State who can authorize the next action, which guardrail could block it, and how the decision will be recorded.

## Learning objectives from the original guide

* Develop an operational measurement dictionary and Goal–Question–Measure chain.
* Assess data completeness, validity, timeliness, repeatability, and measurement burden.
* Decompose lead time into touch, wait, queue, rework, and blocked time.
* Analyze distributions, percentiles, variation, stability, and subgroup differences.
* Design balanced flow, quality, value, risk, and human-system measures.

1. **Why use percentiles?**

   They show tail behavior and service levels hidden by averages.

   </details>

2. **What is common-cause variation?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Variation inherent in the current process system rather than attributable to a specific unusual event.

   </details>

3. **Why stratify data?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   Different work classes or contexts can have different mechanisms and performance.

   </details>

4. **What is measurement burden?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   The time, cognitive load, tooling, and behavioral cost of collecting and maintaining measures.

   </details>

5. **What is a balanced measure set?**

   <details><summary>Show explanation</summary>

   A set covering flow, quality, value, risk, and human outcomes with guardrails.

   </details>

**Common critical failures to avoid (from the guide):**

* Using averages alone for skewed or censored process data.
* Confusing targets/specifications with control limits.
* Changing closure definitions without restating the baseline.
* Publishing person-level rankings from weak or context-free process data.
