Chapter 02 — Digital Engineering strategy, policy, competencies, and adoption

Book 16: Foundations of Digital and Mission Engineering · Course EN.645.782 · Week 2
Companion to: Original weekly course guide · Hypothetical laboratory · Book contents

Teaching purpose. The guide defines the assessed engineering deliverable. This chapter builds the underlying mental model and practices reasoning before the lab. All Northstar program details and calculations below are fictional teaching cases, not operational claims.

The engineering situation

Strategy and policy are useful only when translated into program behavior, competencies, controls, and measurable decisions. The essential question is: What must Northstar change in models, data, environment, workforce, governance, and contracts to realize digital-engineering value?

The central idea of this chapter is strategy is governance plus capability. Before a method is selected, the engineer must distinguish the decision being made, what evidence could falsify the leading approach, and which obligations cannot be relaxed. In the Northstar case, engineers, operators, safety leads and suppliers are accountable for different portions of one mission result. A locally successful artifact is not sufficient if the complete outcome is unsupported.

Explanation: why the method works

Adopting tools does not itself establish a trustworthy digital environment. A strategy must allocate authority for model ownership, access, interfaces, version control, competence, and validation. Assess organizational capability with a concrete workflow and a measured failure—not only staff certifications or licenses.

A useful way to understand the mechanism is to follow the causal chain explicitly. Start with a stimulus or observed condition; ask which model connects it to the engineering outcome; identify the assumptions that let the model predict anything; and define the next decision. Avoid confusing a convenient dashboard measure with the actual mission variable. Where constraints are hard—such as safety authorization, configuration compatibility or accessibility—treat them as eligibility conditions before computing preferences.

Deepening the reasoning — Competency and governance. Map a specific failure to capability: poor versioning needs configuration practice, missing hazard reasoning needs systems expertise, and weak access control needs security architecture. Acquiring a software license is not evidence of any of these.

Connect the theory to the course vocabulary

  • The 2018 strategy defines direction; DoDI 5000.97 establishes policy and responsibility; program plans define the actual implementation.
  • The five goals are interdependent. Models without authority, infrastructure, methods, and workforce do not create a digital engineering capability.
  • DECF is a workforce framework, not a single job description. Roles require different proficiency across data, models, methods, environment, and behaviors.
  • Benefits should be framed as decision quality, cycle time, defect prevention, reuse, confidence, or lifecycle cost—not number of models or tools.
  • Adoption should begin with high-value use cases, controlled pilots, baseline measures, and explicit stop/scale criteria.

The statements above originate in the assigned course guide; use them as technical claims to test against a specific scenario, not as terminology to memorize. For each claim, ask what observation could show that it is false or inapplicable in the current design. The same discipline distinguishes an engineering argument from a procedural assertion.

The difficult part is governance, not file conversion. Different teams may use the same term for different phenomena or different terms for the same variable. An authoritative source must define ownership, semantic rules, versioning, and acceptable synchronization delay. Automation can quickly retrieve relationships but cannot decide whether those relationships are adequate for a mission decision. Successful adoption therefore joins technical infrastructure to training, incentives, review practice, and decision rights. Without that social architecture, a digital thread becomes an expensive collection of links.

Digital engineering is more than replacing documents with models or storing files in a shared platform. Its promise is to make engineering claims, assumptions, configurations, and evidence addressable and related across organizational boundaries. A requirement, simulation result, interface contract, and test report become more useful when their identity and meaning survive tool boundaries and revision changes.

Digital engineering is a change in the structure of engineering knowledge

Graduate depth: digital transformation as an information-systems change

Information continuity is the central engineering mechanism

A digital-engineering strategy is not a procurement plan for modeling software. It changes how technical claims are created, versioned, linked, reviewed, and reused through the lifecycle. In a document-centric arrangement, a requirement may be copied into a design report, manually interpreted in a test procedure, and summarized again in a management slide. Each copy can diverge. A connected digital environment instead attempts to preserve identity and relations among the requirement, its rationale, model representation, interface allocation, verification method, and released evidence. The expected benefit comes from reducing interpretation and reconciliation work, not from converting every PDF into a database record.

This is a graph and governance problem. Let artifacts be vertices V and typed relations such as satisfies, derived from, and verified by be edges E. A graph query can identify candidate downstream objects affected by a changed requirement, but only if link semantics, configuration scope, and coverage are trustworthy. An edge marked verified by should say whether a test verified a requirement, verified a model assumption, or merely exercised a function; these are different evidence claims. The presence of a link is not proof of technical correctness. Graph connectivity is a prerequisite for certain analyses, not a replacement for expert interpretation.

Configuration, identity, and the authoritative source

Many digital-engineering failures arise from unclear object authority. Two teams may both maintain a field called 'maximum payload' with different definitions, units, or configuration applicability. An authoritative source of truth is therefore not necessarily one physical database. It is a governance arrangement specifying which representation controls which meaning at a given baseline, how references resolve across systems, and how conflicts are adjudicated. Persistent identifiers and version-aware links are essential: a test associated with a retired software build cannot automatically support the current one.

Interoperability has multiple layers. File compatibility allows data to move; syntactic interoperability allows software to parse it; semantic interoperability preserves its engineering meaning; and organizational interoperability gives responsible parties a way to resolve disagreements. A platform can achieve the first two while failing the latter two. For example, two model tools can exchange an interface named 'speed' while one measures meters per second and the other interprets kilometers per hour. Formal units and type constraints help, but shared definitions and review processes are still required.

Adoption economics and organizational feedback

Digital transformation creates transitional costs before benefits: training, data migration, model remediation, interface maintenance, and parallel legacy reporting. If adoption is evaluated only by licenses purchased or artifacts migrated, teams may optimize for visible activity while true engineering lead time worsens. A more meaningful benefit hypothesis might be: version-controlled traceability reduces the time to identify affected verification evidence after an interface change from ten days to three without increasing escaped defects. The hypothesis requires baseline measurement, a comparison condition, and clear definitions for quality guardrails.

Adoption follows a feedback loop. Better models can make reuse easier, attracting contributors, which improves coverage. But poor early quality can cause distrust, drive people back to local spreadsheets, and leave the shared model increasingly stale. This is why governance, training, and incentives must be designed together. Technical maturity is not a substitute for a sustainable contribution workflow; compulsory data entry without decision value creates compliance theater.

Northstar's migration decision

Suppose Northstar has a spreadsheet of vehicle interfaces, a SysML architecture, a supplier document repository, and a test database. The sensible first intervention may be to establish controlled identifiers for critical interfaces and build a trace from those interfaces to system tests. A broad mandate to model every engineering artifact could consume resources while leaving this high-risk relationship unresolved. Pilot one consequential change scenario, compare the old and new time to locate impacted evidence, and verify that the linked records are actually correct.

A digital-engineering strategy should name its intended decisions, authoritative objects, semantic contracts, configuration rules, access boundaries, quality checks, and measured adoption outcomes. The result is an engineered knowledge system. Its credibility depends on the behavior of the people and processes maintaining it as much as on its data technology.

Worked Northstar engineering case

A proposed initiative says, “Connect all tools and train everyone in SysML.” The example decomposes it into three use cases: automated requirement-to-test coverage, supplier-interface change impact, and emergency-route mission analysis. It maps each use case to strategy goals, competencies, owners, measures, data rights, risks, and a six-month pilot decision.

A modeling team has mature diagram tools but releases conflicting interface versions. Prioritize configuration reconciliation and signed baselines before procuring a more elaborate graphical editor.

Work the example in two passes. First compute or trace the nominal outcome, naming the unit of analysis, released configuration and decision threshold. Second change one assumption, such as a degraded interface, heavier demand, invalid provenance or altered stakeholder constraint. If the recommendation changes, identify the threshold at which it changes; if it does not, explain the margin of robustness. A conclusion without its assumptions is not a reusable engineering result.

A systematic check before accepting the result

  1. Boundary: What part of the program, process or mission is represented, and what has been excluded?
  2. Evidence: Which observations, versions, or tests support the premises, and what remains hypothetical?
  3. Causality: Could the proposed mechanism be confused with a proxy, confounding condition or common dependency?
  4. Decision: Which alternatives are still feasible, who has authority, and what would force reconsideration?
  5. Assurance: Which safety, security, accessibility or quality guardrail cannot be traded for speed or convenience?

Counterexample and failure boundary

Distinguish a technology roadmap from an evidence and competency strategy.

Prioritize three changes within a constrained six-month improvement budget and state the adoption and proof metrics. Notice that a mathematically consistent answer can still be an inappropriate decision if the intended use or validity assumptions have changed. In professional work, a responsible engineer records that limitation alongside the nominal result.

Practise the explanation, not just the procedure

  1. Concept check: Explain the five Digital Engineering Strategy goals and the procedural expectations of DoDI 5000.97. Explain: Give a boundary, an example of evidence, and a concrete case in which the claim would fail.
  2. Concept check: Apply the DoD Digital Engineering Fundamentals to a program-level adoption plan. Explain: Give a boundary, an example of evidence, and a concrete case in which the claim would fail.
  3. Concept check: Assess workforce capability using DECF concepts and identify role-specific gaps. Explain: Give a boundary, an example of evidence, and a concrete case in which the claim would fail.
  4. Sensitivity exercise: Recalculate or re-evaluate the Northstar case after changing one major assumption. State the original and revised decision.
  5. Transfer exercise: Apply competency and governance to a different engineering system (hospital scheduling, power distribution, or emergency communications). Describe what remains transferable and what does not.

Explained response guide

  • A strong answer separates the mission outcome from the internal activity metric, states which evidence is authoritative, and specifies an escalation or stop rule when evidence becomes stale.
  • For the sensitivity exercise, Prioritize three changes within a constrained six-month improvement budget and state the adoption and proof metrics. The correct answer is conditional on the chosen assumption and its justification; there is no single universally preferred architecture or process.
  • For the transfer exercise, preserve the causal logic but redefine the boundary, user population, constraints, measurements, and physical limitations. Copying Northstar numbers into another domain would be invalid.
