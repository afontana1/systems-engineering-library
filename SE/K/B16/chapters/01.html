Chapter 01 — Lifecycle, acquisition, and digital/mission framing

Book 16: Foundations of Digital and Mission Engineering · Course EN.645.782 · Week 1
Companion to: Original weekly course guide · Hypothetical laboratory · Book contents

Teaching purpose. The guide defines the assessed engineering deliverable. This chapter builds the underlying mental model and practices reasoning before the lab. All Northstar program details and calculations below are fictional teaching cases, not operational claims.

The engineering situation

A digital-engineering initiative fails early when lifecycle decisions, mission questions, and accountable users are not defined. The essential question is: Which decisions require connected digital evidence, and what consequence follows if that evidence is late, inconsistent, inaccessible, or wrong?

The central idea of this chapter is digital engineering begins with decisions. Before a method is selected, the engineer must distinguish the decision being made, what evidence could falsify the leading approach, and which obligations cannot be relaxed. In the Northstar case, engineers, operators, safety leads and suppliers are accountable for different portions of one mission result. A locally successful artifact is not sufficient if the complete outcome is unsupported.

Explanation: why the method works

For each lifecycle decision define the owner, timing, alternatives, evidence objects, and consequence of being wrong. One information asset may be authoritative for design geometry yet unsuitable for the current operational state. An end-to-end mission outcome may depend on nontechnical interventions, not merely a better digital product.

A useful way to understand the mechanism is to follow the causal chain explicitly. Start with a stimulus or observed condition; ask which model connects it to the engineering outcome; identify the assumptions that let the model predict anything; and define the next decision. Avoid confusing a convenient dashboard measure with the actual mission variable. Where constraints are hard—such as safety authorization, configuration compatibility or accessibility—treat them as eligibility conditions before computing preferences.

Deepening the reasoning — Decision-use register. A lifecycle artifact earns authority only for its configured purpose. The same demand model can be acceptable for fleet budgeting and insufficient for real-time dispatch. List evidence consumers and expiration times.

Connect the theory to the course vocabulary

  • Digital engineering is an integrated way of performing engineering; it does not replace systems engineering or acquisition accountability.
  • Lifecycle stages create different evidence users and authority. A concept model, released design, test record, and operational state estimate can all describe the same system while having different authority.
  • Mission engineering begins with mission outcomes and end-to-end dependencies. It may change products, organizations, processes, doctrine, training, logistics, policy, or information exchanges.
  • Decision framing should state decision owner, question, timing, alternatives, evidence, consequence, and acceptable uncertainty.
  • The receiving review prevents inherited models and dashboards from becoming trusted by default.

The statements above originate in the assigned course guide; use them as technical claims to test against a specific scenario, not as terminology to memorize. For each claim, ask what observation could show that it is false or inapplicable in the current design. The same discipline distinguishes an engineering argument from a procedural assertion.

The mechanism beneath the method

Digital and mission engineering connect representations to decisions across organizations and lifecycle stages.

Distinguishing related ideas

Digitizing documents does not create an authoritative engineering thread if relationships, versions and ownership remain ambiguous.

Interpretation and boundary conditions

The value lies in being able to recompute consequences when a mission assumption, requirement or system configuration changes.

Worked Northstar engineering case

The sponsor asks Northstar to “build a digital twin for emergency mobility.” The example reframes this into four decisions: whether to pre-position vehicles, whether to curtail charging, how to route accessibility-dependent riders, and whether to request mutual aid. Each decision has different update rates, evidence, consequence, and uncertainty. The example shows that one monolithic twin is neither necessary nor automatically credible.

Northstar must choose whether to reposition vehicles, curtail charging, or request mutual aid. The first needs near-term demand estimates; the second energy and state-of-charge data; the third cross-agency availability and permission. One generic dashboard cannot validate all three.

Work the example in two passes. First compute or trace the nominal outcome, naming the unit of analysis, released configuration and decision threshold. Second change one assumption, such as a degraded interface, heavier demand, invalid provenance or altered stakeholder constraint. If the recommendation changes, identify the threshold at which it changes; if it does not, explain the margin of robustness. A conclusion without its assumptions is not a reusable engineering result.

Graduate synthesis: digital-engineering strategy begins with decision economics

Intended use defines the authority of digital evidence

A digital model does not possess a single universal quality level. Its adequacy depends on the decision it supports. A fleet-demand forecast may be sufficient for comparing ten-year depot locations but unsafe as the sole basis for real-time vehicle dispatch. Formally, a decision uses a representation M to estimate outcomes Y(a, θ) for alternatives a and uncertain context θ. Credibility requires not merely a consistent model but justified correspondence between its domain, data, configuration, and intended use. Different decisions impose different penalties for false confidence.

Start from a decision-use contract: owner, deadline, alternatives, admissible evidence, consequence of error, version, access rules, and expiry condition. Evidence has a lineage graph connecting assumptions, requirements, architecture elements, data sets, analysis runs, test results, approvals, and field observations. An edge means a justified relation, not a hyperlink chosen for convenience. A supplier's simulator may contain excellent geometry and an outdated communication protocol; the geometry remains useful for mechanical clearance while its timing conclusions have expired. Governance should permit selective authority rather than indiscriminate rejection or reuse.

Mission outcomes have dependencies beyond the product boundary

The mission unit is not the digital model or even the shuttle fleet; it is a service delivered through interacting technology, trained people, emergency procedures, roads, operators, data agreements, and public institutions. Write a top-level outcome conceptually as Y = f(V, D, O, P, E), where the arguments represent vehicles, dispatch, operators, policy and environment. Such a function is deliberately incomplete: the interactions can dominate the isolated contributions. If the dispatch algorithm improves routing by 8% while a hospital access policy blocks drop-off at night, the mission result may not improve at all.

This distinction has an acquisition implication. Contracts that reward delivery of individual digital artifacts can produce technically compliant but operationally disconnected systems. Acquisition should therefore specify exchange semantics, model ownership, acceptance evidence, update responsibilities, and exit or migration rights. A digital-thread capability is not proven by having files in one repository: it must answer a consequential trace question across independent configurations, such as which safety claims become suspect if a supplier updates a localization component.

Compare evidence investment against decisions it changes

Digital engineering involves a portfolio of information investments. A digital twin maintained at high fidelity may be expensive in sensors, calibration, cybersecurity, and staffing. Its economic value arises when it improves a decision, shortens credible verification, or detects consequential faults earlier. For uncertain state θ, a basic expected-value-of-information expression is EVSI = E_z[max_a E[V(a,θ)|z]] - max_a E[V(a,θ)], where z is a prospective observation. In practice the information investment is justified only when this expected benefit exceeds acquisition, operating, delay, and security costs. Its usefulness also depends on whether decision authority can act on the observation before the opportunity expires.

As an illustration, an instrumented pilot might cost $80,000 and resolve uncertainty that could lead to a $600,000 unsuitable depot commitment. The possible avoided loss is not itself an $600,000 expected benefit. Estimate probabilities of competing states, how diagnostic the pilot is, and what choices remain reversible. If the pilot cannot change the site decision because property commitments are already binding, its decision value is much smaller.

Receiving review as an epistemic control

At the beginning of the lifecycle, separate evidence into accepted for specified use, conditionally usable, quarantined pending revalidation, and obsolete. Check provenance, unit definitions, time horizon, population coverage, authorization, and dependence among measurements. If several dashboards use the same defective upstream feed, apparent agreement is not independent corroboration. The receiving review converts inherited information into an explicit, bounded knowledge claim. That is the foundation of an authoritative digital mission strategy.

A systematic check before accepting the result

  1. Boundary: What part of the program, process or mission is represented, and what has been excluded?
  2. Evidence: Which observations, versions, or tests support the premises, and what remains hypothetical?
  3. Causality: Could the proposed mechanism be confused with a proxy, confounding condition or common dependency?
  4. Decision: Which alternatives are still feasible, who has authority, and what would force reconsideration?
  5. Assurance: Which safety, security, accessibility or quality guardrail cannot be traded for speed or convenience?

Counterexample and failure boundary

A digital twin without a decision and intended-use statement is at best a visualization project.

Create four decision rows with evidence freshness, decision consequences and required review owner. Notice that a mathematically consistent answer can still be an inappropriate decision if the intended use or validity assumptions have changed. In professional work, a responsible engineer records that limitation alongside the nominal result.

Practise the explanation, not just the procedure

  1. Concept check: Reconstruct the principal technical and technical-management activities across the lifecycle and relate them to acquisition decisions. Explain: Give a boundary, an example of evidence, and a concrete case in which the claim would fail.
  2. Concept check: Distinguish system, system of systems, enterprise, capability, mission, mission thread, and operational scenario. Explain: Give a boundary, an example of evidence, and a concrete case in which the claim would fail.
  3. Concept check: Identify decision authorities, evidence users, timing, consequence, and information constraints. Explain: Give a boundary, an example of evidence, and a concrete case in which the claim would fail.
  4. Sensitivity exercise: Recalculate or re-evaluate the Northstar case after changing one major assumption. State the original and revised decision.
  5. Transfer exercise: Apply decision-use register to a different engineering system (hospital scheduling, power distribution, or emergency communications). Describe what remains transferable and what does not.

Explained response guide

  • A strong answer separates the mission outcome from the internal activity metric, states which evidence is authoritative, and specifies an escalation or stop rule when evidence becomes stale.
  • For the sensitivity exercise, Create four decision rows with evidence freshness, decision consequences and required review owner. The correct answer is conditional on the chosen assumption and its justification; there is no single universally preferred architecture or process.
  • For the transfer exercise, preserve the causal logic but redefine the boundary, user population, constraints, measurements, and physical limitations. Copying Northstar numbers into another domain would be invalid.

Decision economics before a digital infrastructure purchase

A digital program's initial product is not a model repository: it is a decision contract. Record an authorized decision maker, alternatives, time of use, mistake consequence, evidence demand, and an explicit rejection gate. The Northstar regional resilience board must decide whether to fund vehicles, continuity procedures, or a combination. Its evidence package must distinguish a successful query from an adequate mission result. The controlled case inputs state a course-screening intended use, not a deployment authorization. Traceability, mission simulation, and telemetry are three different evidence claims that cannot be interchanged.

Formalize the potential value of a decision-support asset. For alternatives \(a\in A\), uncertain conditions \(\theta\in\Theta\), and loss \(L(a,\theta)\), the current Bayes risk is \(R_0=\min_a E_\theta[L(a,\theta)]\). After observing an information product \(Y\), the risk becomes \(R_Y=E_Y[\min_a E_{\theta\mid Y}L(a,\theta)]\). The expected value of sample information is \(EVSI=R_0-R_Y\), before subtracting the cost and delay of creating and governing the information. This expression requires defensible priors, likelihoods, decision rights, and a stable loss definition; it is not an automatic justification for buying a digital twin.

Worked decision-investment screen. Suppose a hypothetical mission-data integration effort costs $120,000. Without it, an avoidable decision failure has probability 0.040 and consequence $5 million, implying an expected loss of $200,000. If the intervention changes the probability to 0.020, avoided expected loss is \((0.040-0.020)\times 5{,}000{,}000=\$100{,}000\); the simplified net benefit is −$20,000. Under a stronger, independently demonstrated change to 0.005, benefit is $175,000 and net becomes +$55,000. Neither invented probability is a field estimate. This calculation forces the sponsor to specify which failure the information mitigates and how its value would be measured.

Lifecycle and receiving review. In concept exploration, a low-fidelity demand model may screen candidate fleets. Before physical release, the same model's authority expires unless it is attached to validated configurations, software versions, operating envelopes, accessible-service tests, safety evidence, and independent acceptance. On receiving a model, verify owner, source, revision, units, operating conditions, model-use class, security marking, provenance and review date. An executable model does not inherit authorization from a previous lifecycle stage.

Explained exercise. A manager claims a $60,000 automated reporting tool is justified because it produces 500 trace links weekly. If those links do not change a decision, no expected benefit follows from the count. Require a baseline of decision error or cycle time, a plausible causal reduction, and a controlled comparison. If it saves 300 engineering hours at $140/hour, the nominal labor benefit is $42,000, still $18,000 short; that is not necessarily a rejection, but any safety and delay value must be separately defended. Counterexample: a highly linked report may still cite a retired supplier revision. The Book 16 case guide shows how this happens.

Boundaries. Expected-value calculations omit catastrophic tail constraints unless those constraints are represented. Legal, accessibility, safety and data-sharing obligations should appear as feasibility conditions, not be monetized away by an average value score.
