# Hypothetical Lab 10 — The Month Five Recovery Cell

*EN.645.667 · Week 10 · Fictional rehearsal, not a substitute for the course assignment.*

**Read first:** [Book Chapter 10](../chapters/10-integrated-recovery-control.md). **Then consult:** [Course Week 10](../../../C/P0/EN645667.md).

## Scenario packet

All Month 5 warning signals occur at once; the board can choose only one initial investigation and one cost-limited intervention. All people, figures and institutional decisions are synthetic. Use only the role authority and requirements stated here; if information is missing, list assumptions and propose the evidence needed.

## Student tasks

1. Construct a causal diagram connecting supplier, interface, software tests, TPMs, costs and schedule.
2. Compare three recovery paths using a four-criterion decision table.
3. Write a two-page executive recovery memo with a baseline/forecast distinction.

## Suggested deliverables

Save `LAB10_DecisionMemo.md` and `LAB10_EvidenceRegister.csv`. The memo should define the decision, alternatives, authority, assumptions, analysis, remaining risks and exit condition. The register should contain claim, source, evidence date, configuration/version and reviewer status. Keep calculations and fact claims separable from judgments.

## Self-review rubric (10 points)

- 2 — Problem and authority framed correctly; 2 — All material scope, schedule, cost and technical effects surfaced; 2 — Reasoning and quantitative work reproducible where applicable; 2 — Counterargument and uncertainty addressed; 2 — Final decision bounded by evidence and approval rights.

## Reference response — read only after attempting

The response should prioritize validation of the suspected interface/testing bottleneck rather than rewarding unverified “progress.” Every option must state evidence, authority, incremental cost and an explicit failure trigger. This response is a **minimum argument**, not a unique solution. Full credit requires a traceable rationale and acknowledgement of credible objections. In particular, a model or administrative approval must not be mistaken for a real-world safety authorization.
