# Candidate physical architectures, model-based trades, and alternative evaluation

**Companion chapter:** EN.645.631, Week 09 · Phase 1 edition 1.0 (2026-10-30)  
**Original course:** [Introduction to Model Based Systems Engineering](../../../C/P1/EN645631.md) — find **Week 9** for assignment, assessment criteria and complete reading plan.  
**Concepts:** trade study, candidate baseline, utility, sensitivity  
**Use of case:** Synthetic Autonomous Campus Shuttle case; all values and designs are educational, not field safety guidance.

## The question to answer
> How do you compare architectures without disguising preferences as objectivity?

## Conceptual tutorial

### Make choices observable
A defensible trade begins with decision context, alternatives, evaluation criteria and mandatory constraints. Eliminate infeasible options before assigning soft preferences. A weighted score is a preference model, not a physical fact: criteria definitions, units, scales, weights and uncertainty must be visible. Summing scores without normalized scales can accidentally give one measure disproportionate influence.

Use at least two forms of evidence: models or calculations for quantitative constraints, and reasoned judgments with rationale for qualitative criteria. For uncertainty, run one-at-a-time sensitivity or scenarios. An alternative that wins only when an arbitrary weight is set at 0.31 rather than 0.30 is fragile. Record the break-even point and conditions under which the recommendation would change.

### What the architecture review decides
The review should distinguish **selected**, **rejected**, and **deferred** alternatives, with consequences and assumptions. Selection is conditional on evidence: prototype results, interface contracts and verification provisions may still be outstanding. Keep the losing options accessible for future design changes; otherwise the team loses its reasoning history.

## Worked application: follow the reasoning

**Trade example.** Candidate A: centralized dispatch scores [9 cost, 5 resilience, 8 manageability]; candidate B: local-first scores [6, 9, 6]. Weights [0.3, 0.5, 0.2] give A = 2.7 + 2.5 + 1.6 = 6.8; B = 1.8 + 4.5 + 1.2 = 7.5. B wins under these judgments, but a mandatory data-governance constraint could eliminate B regardless of score.

## The misconception to avoid

**Watch for:** Missing hard constraints and double-counted criteria turn an apparent optimization into a preference laundering exercise.
