EN.645.742 · Week 01 — Complex problem situations, systems theory, and receiving review

Phase 5 companion · Management of Complex Systems
Central idea: Problem situations before solution selection
Original guide: EN.645.742, Week 1

Learning outcome: Be able to explain the week's mechanism, apply it to a defensible case, identify the boundary of a method and present evidence for the associated engineering decision. This lesson is original companion instruction; source-guide notes are explicitly labeled below.

Explain the concept

Complex situations are not merely complicated technical designs. They can include contested purposes, unequal authority, adaptive actors and side effects that change the problem while it is being addressed. Start by mapping perspectives and behavior over time, not by declaring one root cause.

A boundary is a purposeful analytical decision. Every exclusion may conceal an important affected party, mechanism or risk. State what is known, unknown and disputed, and distinguish observed evidence from interpretations. Provisional models should remain open to revision.

A boundary worth remembering: A complex problem situation need not have one agreed objective or owner.

Work through an independent case

A regional operator says the issue is a dispatch optimization problem; riders say the problem is unreliable access; workers say roster policies cause missed service. All could describe pieces of the situation. Begin with multiple problem statements and an affected-party map rather than a prematurely optimized routing algorithm.

How to generalize the reasoning

  1. Purpose: Write the actual mission, enterprise or people-centered outcome. Avoid replacing an outcome with a proposed tool.
  2. Actors and dependencies: Identify who owns the relevant systems, information, resources, decisions and consequences. Separate authority from influence.
  3. Mechanism: Name the claimed relationship: interface, dependency, feedback, incentive, perception, constraint or testable causal mechanism.
  4. Evidence: Define an observable measure, data provenance, time window, alternative explanation and relevant configuration.
  5. Action: Compare at least two interventions and state the preconditions, uncertainty, legitimate decision owner, stop rule and learning plan.

Instructor notes from the guide

  • Complexity is not a compliment, excuse, or score; it is a claim about relationships, behavior, knowledge, and intervention.
  • A system boundary is a judgment made for a purpose, not a discovered natural line.
  • Open systems exchange information, energy, resources, people, legitimacy, and risk with an environment.
  • Emergence describes whole-system properties arising through interaction; it does not mean “mysterious.”
  • Problem formulation is already an intervention because it privileges some actors, outcomes, and evidence.
  • The receiving review should preserve earlier artifacts but suspend commitment to their preferred solutions.

The guide's worked illustration

A regional authority frames the issue as “optimize autonomous shuttle deployment.” Reframing it as “maintain equitable emergency access during compound disruption” changes the relevant actors, boundaries, options, measures, and authority. The original optimization problem remains useful only as a bounded subproblem.

Reconstruct the example in a diagram, table, memo, or model. Then state where the example is deliberately simplified and why the same conclusion might not transfer to the regional case.

Active reading task: Change one major assumption in the original example. Identify exactly which claim, conclusion or decision becomes unsupported and what evidence could restore it.

Learning outcomes from the original guide

By the end of the week, you should be able to:

  • distinguish simple, complicated, dynamically complex, organizationally complex, and complex-adaptive conditions
  • define a problem situation without presuming a single purpose, owner, boundary, or solution
  • identify open-system relationships, hierarchy, emergence, feedback, and multiple perspectives
  • construct initial behavior-over-time graphs and a complexity-characteristics profile
  • state provisional boundaries, exclusions, uncertainties, and non-claims

Deliberate practice: build and critique an artifact

The original guide recommends the following weekly exercises:

  1. Inventory prior Phase 5 claims and classify each as fact, assumption, interpretation, value judgment, or recommendation.
  2. Create six behavior-over-time graphs: access reliability, emergency travel time, workforce load, public trust, service inequity, and recovery time.
  3. Build a complexity profile covering diversity, connectivity, feedback, delay, adaptation, scale, authority, uncertainty, and conflict.
  4. Draft three alternative system boundaries and compare what each includes and excludes.
  5. Conduct a receiving review and record findings that must be resolved before method selection.

Pause after each step. Compare the result with the decision purpose and record disagreement, ambiguity, and negative evidence instead of forcing convergence.

Companion extension: Produce a one-page claim–evidence–authority ledger for the worked case. Include one supported claim, one disputed or untested claim, a dated configuration, an owner, a measure with units, a plausible failure path, an affected stakeholder who may disagree, and an explicit authorization or learning decision. Perform a red-team review: remove one critical source or change one actor's incentive and update the decision.

Mastery evidence from the original course

Weekly assessed deliverable:

Complexity Framing and Receiving Package

Required contents:

  • problem-situation charter and decision purpose
  • three candidate boundaries and boundary rationale
  • behavior-over-time graphs with data/provenance notes
  • complexity-characteristics profile
  • stakeholder and affected-party preliminary registry
  • assumption, uncertainty, exclusion, and non-claim log
  • receiving-review findings and dispositions

Include a one-page review note stating the decision supported, approach used, assumptions, boundary judgments, evidence, affected groups, limitations, and changes made to prior baselines.

Critical failure modes to avoid:

  • Treats the regional issue as a predefined autonomous-shuttle optimization problem.
  • Presents a single boundary as objectively correct.
  • Uses “emergent” or “irreducible” without evidence or operational meaning.

Any critical failure requires revision regardless of the numerical score.

Revision and mastery requirement:

Pass the receiving review only when the situation is framed as revisable, multiple boundaries are preserved, and no preferred solution has been smuggled into the problem definition.

Minimum weekly score: 80%, with no critical failure and at least Proficient performance on the first two rubric criteria.
