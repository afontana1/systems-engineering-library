## Explain the concept

Type F coercive complexity addresses cases where power shapes whose claims count, who can participate and who pays for outcomes. Critical Systems Heuristics makes boundary judgments and sources of legitimacy visible: whose purposes, resources, expertise and worldview are privileged, and who is affected without control. Structured dialogue methods such as Team Syntegrity can support broad exploration but cannot erase coercion.

Distinguish descriptive claims about existing authority from normative questions about who *ought* to have a voice. Document exclusion, retaliation risk, consent and channels for redress. An intervention may need to change decision rights, not merely improve models.

**A boundary worth remembering:** Calling a process inclusive does not establish that affected people had meaningful influence.

## Work through an independent case

A funding agency sets all performance metrics while riders with long transfers cannot attend public meetings. Create an 'is/ought' boundary comparison and offer paid accessible participation plus independent review. Disclose that engagement still may not grant formal veto authority.

### How to generalize the reasoning

1. **Purpose:** Write the actual mission, enterprise or people-centered outcome. Avoid replacing an outcome with a proposed tool.
2. **Actors and dependencies:** Identify who owns the relevant systems, information, resources, decisions and consequences. Separate authority from influence.
3. **Mechanism:** Name the claimed relationship: interface, dependency, feedback, incentive, perception, constraint or testable causal mechanism.
4. **Evidence:** Define an observable measure, data provenance, time window, alternative explanation and relevant configuration.
5. **Action:** Compare at least two interventions and state the preconditions, uncertainty, legitimate decision owner, stop rule and learning plan.

### Instructor notes from the guide

* Coercion exists when parties cannot freely participate or protect their interests, not only when force is explicit.
* CSH asks who ought to benefit, decide, supply expertise, and represent affected people—and compares that ideal with current conditions.
* Boundary judgments determine facts, values, and affected groups that count as relevant.
* Team Syntegrity seeks nonhierarchical dialogue across a structured network, but access and safety still require design.
* Participation can be extractive or performative if decisions are already fixed.
* A systems practitioner cannot grant emancipation; they can expose boundary judgments, redistribute process power, and refuse misleading legitimacy claims.

### The guide's worked illustration

A public consultation invites disabled travelers to comment on a preselected autonomous-service plan. Procurement deadlines, inaccessible materials, and vendor confidentiality prevent meaningful change. A CSH critique shows that affected users provide “witness” but lack control, expertise resources, and decision influence. The intervention must reopen requirements and funding decisions, not merely add another listening session.

Reconstruct the example in a diagram, table, memo, or model. Then state where the example is deliberately simplified and why the same conclusion might not transfer to the regional case.

Actors lack equal ability to participate, protect interests, challenge boundaries, or influence decisions.

</details>

<details><summary>What is boundary critique?</summary>

Systematic examination of who and what is included, excluded, valued, controlled, known, and represented.

</details>

<details><summary>What are is/ought questions?</summary>

Comparison of current arrangements with normatively defensible arrangements.

</details>

<details><summary>Can a method remove power asymmetry?</summary>

No; it can expose and sometimes reshape process conditions, but material power requires substantive action.

</details>

<details><summary>Why state practitioner positionality?</summary>

The practitioner’s role, interests, access, expertise, and authority shape the intervention.

</details>

## Deliberate practice: build and critique an artifact

The original guide recommends the following weekly exercises:

1. Complete all CSH is/ought boundary questions for the preferred intervention.
2. Map formal, informal, economic, legal, technical, discursive, and data power.
3. Identify participation risks and design safeguards, compensation, accessibility, confidentiality, and escalation.
4. Design a structured dialogue or Syntegrity-inspired process with transparent influence on decisions.
5. Write practitioner positionality and authority statements.

Pause after each step. Compare the result with the decision purpose and record disagreement, ambiguity, and negative evidence instead of forcing convergence.

**Companion extension:** Produce a one-page *claim–evidence–authority ledger* for the worked case. Include one supported claim, one disputed or untested claim, a dated configuration, an owner, a measure with units, a plausible failure path, an affected stakeholder who may disagree, and an explicit authorization or learning decision. Perform a red-team review: remove one critical source or change one actor's incentive and update the decision.
