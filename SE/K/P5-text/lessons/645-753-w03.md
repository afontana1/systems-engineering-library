## Explain the concept

Value is multidimensional and distributed. A change can improve an aggregate measure while making outcomes worse for a smaller group. Distinguish outputs (trips served), outcomes (reliable access to appointments), distribution (which riders benefit), and burden transfers (who absorbs new waiting, cost or safety risk).

Incentives alter behavior. Payment per trip may reward volume but not accessibility or transfers. Build a metric hierarchy that includes experience, reliability, affordability, equity, fiscal health and safety guardrails. Use disaggregated data before calculating overall averages.

**A boundary worth remembering:** A single composite KPI can obscure who benefits and who is harmed.

## Work through an independent case

A redesign lifts average on-time arrival from 86% to 91%, but paratransit riders' timely-arrival rate drops from 80% to 63%. Reporting the overall improvement alone hides a burden transfer. Require subgroup effects and an explicit enterprise decision about acceptable tradeoffs.

### How to generalize the reasoning

1. **Purpose:** Write the actual mission, enterprise or people-centered outcome. Avoid replacing an outcome with a proposed tool.
2. **Actors and dependencies:** Identify who owns the relevant systems, information, resources, decisions and consequences. Separate authority from influence.
3. **Mechanism:** Name the claimed relationship: interface, dependency, feedback, incentive, perception, constraint or testable causal mechanism.
4. **Evidence:** Define an observable measure, data provenance, time window, alternative explanation and relevant configuration.
5. **Action:** Compare at least two interventions and state the preconditions, uncertainty, legitimate decision owner, stop rule and learning plan.

### Instructor notes from the guide

* Enterprise value is plural and distributed. A regional average can improve while accessibility, neighborhood, workforce, or emergency outcomes worsen.
* Measures require operational definitions, provenance, context, uncertainty, owners, thresholds, and prescribed responses.
* Incentives can make an apparently irrational enterprise outcome locally rational for every actor.
* Leading indicators support intervention; lagging outcomes show whether value was actually realized.
* Measure portfolios need anti-gaming and burden-transfer checks.

### The guide's worked illustration

A proposed on-time-performance target improves regional averages by allowing operators to skip time-consuming accessibility boarding support. The measure is technically accurate but creates burden transfer. The corrected value system adds accessible-trip completion, denied-service rate, operator workload, passenger harm, and subgroup reliability; funding incentives are redesigned so local schedule performance cannot be improved by shifting cost to vulnerable riders.

## Learning outcomes from the original guide

1. Construct an enterprise value/outcome hierarchy with stakeholder-specific value propositions and conflicts.
2. Define balanced enterprise measures with owners, formulas, provenance, cadence, thresholds, and interpretation rules.
3. Identify burden transfer, externalities, metric gaming, Goodhart effects, and inequitable subgroup outcomes.
4. Connect incentives and funding rules to expected actor behavior.
5. Design a measurement system suitable for enterprise evaluation rather than project compliance alone.

<details><summary>Why distinguish efficiency from effectiveness?</summary>

Efficiency concerns resource use; effectiveness concerns whether valuable enterprise outcomes are achieved.

</details>

<details><summary>What is burden transfer?</summary>

An apparent improvement achieved by shifting cost, delay, workload, risk, or harm to another actor or population.

</details>

<details><summary>Why need measure action rules?</summary>

A measure has little governance value unless actors know how evidence changes decisions or triggers investigation.

</details>

<details><summary>What is Goodhart&#x27;s law in practice?</summary>

When a measure becomes a target, actors adapt to improve the number, potentially degrading the underlying outcome.

</details>

<details><summary>Why include societal and workforce measures?</summary>

They affect legitimacy, adoption, resilience, and the sustainability of enterprise performance.

</details>

## Deliberate practice: build and critique an artifact

The original guide recommends the following weekly exercises:

1. List value recipients, contributors, funders, regulators, and burden bearers.
2. Create an outcome hierarchy separating ends, means, constraints, and diagnostic measures.
3. Define ten measures with formulas, data sources, owners, cadence, uncertainty, and action rules.
4. For each measure, write one gaming strategy and one countermeasure.
5. Run a subgroup and burden-transfer review using the synthetic case data.

Pause after each step and compare the result with the stated decision purpose. Record discrepancies rather than forcing the artifact to match the expected story.

**Companion extension:** Produce a one-page *claim–evidence–authority ledger* for the worked case. Include one supported claim, one disputed or untested claim, a dated configuration, an owner, a measure with units, a plausible failure path, an affected stakeholder who may disagree, and an explicit authorization or learning decision. Perform a red-team review: remove one critical source or change one actor's incentive and update the decision.

**Weekly assessed deliverable:**

**Enterprise Value and Measurement System**

Required contents:

* Value/outcome hierarchy with stakeholder conflicts.
* Measure dictionary containing at least 15 measures and five anti-gaming controls.
* Incentive-to-behavior causal map.
* Burden-transfer and subgroup analysis.
* One-page measurement governance and response protocol.

**Critical failure modes to avoid:**

* Uses only aggregate averages for claims involving access or public value.
* Defines measures without owners, sources, or action rules.
* Recommends incentives without analyzing gaming or shifted burden.

Any critical failure requires revision regardless of the numerical score.
