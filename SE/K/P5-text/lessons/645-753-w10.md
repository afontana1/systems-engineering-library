## Explain the concept

A target operating model specifies how an enterprise will actually work: decision rights, organization relationships, operating processes, information responsibilities, funding, service levels, assurance and review mechanisms. It bridges high-level strategy and implementation.

Compare transformation alternatives by mechanism, not slogan. Centralized dispatch may strengthen coordination but create single points of failure and governance friction. Federated coordination can preserve autonomy but needs stronger shared contracts and incentives.

**A boundary worth remembering:** A target operating model is not an IT system diagram or reorganization chart.

## Work through an independent case

Option A consolidates dispatch under one authority. Option B retains independent carriers with a common data contract and emergency escalation protocol. Evaluate both against hospital-transfer time, carrier consent, cyber resilience, accessibility and operating cost—not just architectural neatness.

### How to generalize the reasoning

1. **Purpose:** Write the actual mission, enterprise or people-centered outcome. Avoid replacing an outcome with a proposed tool.
2. **Actors and dependencies:** Identify who owns the relevant systems, information, resources, decisions and consequences. Separate authority from influence.
3. **Mechanism:** Name the claimed relationship: interface, dependency, feedback, incentive, perception, constraint or testable causal mechanism.
4. **Evidence:** Define an observable measure, data provenance, time window, alternative explanation and relevant configuration.
5. **Action:** Compare at least two interventions and state the preconditions, uncertainty, legitimate decision owner, stop rule and learning plan.

### Instructor notes from the guide

* A target operating model describes how value is created and governed, not only a target IT architecture.
* Alternatives should vary governance and operating logic: centralized services, federated standards, shared utility, contractual network, mutual-aid compact, or governance-first paths.
* Architecture correspondences expose contradictions such as decentralized accountability with centralized data control.
* Transformation feasibility includes political, legal, workforce, procurement, data, supplier, and change-capacity constraints.
* A low-automation or governance-first alternative is often necessary to reveal whether technology is actually the limiting factor.

### The guide's worked illustration

Alternative A creates a centralized regional mobility authority; Alternative B keeps independent operators but creates shared standards, a data trust, mutual-aid agreements, and pooled emergency funding; Alternative C uses a commercial platform and performance contracts. Authority analysis makes A legally infeasible in the horizon; C creates lock-in and weak public accountability. B provides less central control but better feasibility, resilience, and legitimacy when paired with staged technical services.

</details>

<details><summary>Why require distinct alternatives?</summary>

To expose different assumptions, tradeoffs, and feasible mechanisms rather than confirm a preferred solution.

</details>

<details><summary>What is a transition architecture?</summary>

An intermediate enterprise configuration needed to move safely and feasibly from current to target state.

</details>

<details><summary>Why check correspondences?</summary>

An attractive view can contradict another, such as assigning accountability without authority or resources.

</details>

<details><summary>Why include a governance-first option?</summary>

It tests whether coordination, incentives, policy, or authority—not technology—is the binding constraint.

</details>

## Deliberate practice: build and critique an artifact

The original guide recommends the following weekly exercises:

1. Define target operating-model dimensions and required viewpoints.
2. Generate three alternatives with distinct governance and value-delivery logic.
3. Model each alternative across capabilities, actors, processes, information, systems, resources, incentives, and roadmaps.
4. Check cross-view correspondences and feasibility constraints.
5. Evaluate under scenarios and stakeholder perspectives; identify decision reversals.

Pause after each step and compare the result with the stated decision purpose. Record discrepancies rather than forcing the artifact to match the expected story.

**Companion extension:** Produce a one-page *claim–evidence–authority ledger* for the worked case. Include one supported claim, one disputed or untested claim, a dated configuration, an owner, a measure with units, a plausible failure path, an affected stakeholder who may disagree, and an explicit authorization or learning decision. Perform a red-team review: remove one critical source or change one actor's incentive and update the decision.

## Mastery evidence from the original course

**Weekly assessed deliverable:**

**Target Operating Model Alternatives and Trade Study**

Required contents:

* Alternative definitions and decision logic.
* Target architecture views and correspondence checks.
* Stakeholder, authority, incentive, adoption, affordability, and resilience analysis.
* Scenario robustness and decision-reversal results.
* Review package, recommendation, findings, and dispositions.

Include a one-page review note stating the decision supported, evidence used, assumptions, limitations, unresolved conflicts, and changes made to prior baselines.

**Critical failure modes to avoid:**

* Presents one preferred design plus two straw alternatives.
* Recommends centralization without legal, political, or resource authority.
* Uses aggregate value to hide a material stakeholder loss or legitimacy problem.

Any critical failure requires revision regardless of the numerical score.

**Revision and mastery requirement:**

Pass the review only when alternatives are feasible under actual authority, the preferred path remains defensible across scenarios, and rejected alternatives are rejected for evidence-based reasons.

Minimum weekly score: 80%, with no critical failure and at least Proficient performance on the first two rubric criteria.
