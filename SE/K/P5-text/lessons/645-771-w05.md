## Explain the concept

Interoperability has multiple dimensions: physical connectivity, protocol agreement, shared meanings, organizational workflows, policy permissions and mission suitability. The interoperability matrix should identify actors, exchanged objects, time requirements and conditions of authorized use. Shared vocabulary is important; shared decision responsibility is equally important.

Differentiate ability to send data from ability to use it safely. Information that arrives too late, refers to the wrong geographic boundary, or is barred by access policy may be unusable even if every API test passes. Model the degraded mode explicitly.

**A boundary worth remembering:** Interoperability is not a binary certification of the entire coalition; it is use-case-specific.

## Work through an independent case

Two partners exchange vehicle coordinates. One publishes latitude/longitude in decimal degrees; another encodes a local projection. An API-level connectivity test passes, but dispatch sends the wrong rendezvous point. Add coordinate reference system, timestamp and validation bounds to the contract, and test with a location near the projection boundary.

### How to generalize the reasoning

1. **Purpose:** Write the actual mission, enterprise or people-centered outcome. Avoid replacing an outcome with a proposed tool.
2. **Actors and dependencies:** Identify who owns the relevant systems, information, resources, decisions and consequences. Separate authority from influence.
3. **Mechanism:** Name the claimed relationship: interface, dependency, feedback, incentive, perception, constraint or testable causal mechanism.
4. **Evidence:** Define an observable measure, data provenance, time window, alternative explanation and relevant configuration.
5. **Action:** Compare at least two interventions and state the preconditions, uncertainty, legitimate decision owner, stop rule and learning plan.

## The source guide's instructional anchors

### Instructor notes from the guide

* Information architecture begins with decisions and mission consequences, not a data catalog.
* An exchange contract should define producer, consumer, concept, schema, units, identity, timestamp, latency, freshness, confidence, provenance, security, policy, error behavior, and versioning.
* Authoritative does not mean perfect; it means the recognized source for a stated element and context.
* Reconciliation must preserve disagreement and provenance when sources cannot be safely collapsed.
* Interoperability monitoring is operational engineering: version drift, latency, missingness, and semantic exceptions require detection and ownership.

### The guide's worked illustration

A rail system reports “available capacity” as standing plus seated riders; a paratransit provider reports only safely serviceable wheelchair positions; a hospital interprets capacity as medically suitable transport. All use an integer field named `capacity`. Syntactic interoperability exists, but semantic mismatch could create unsafe evacuation assignments.

Reproduce the reasoning with controlled case data, change one material assumption, and explain how the architecture, evidence, or decision changes.

**Active reading task:** Change one major assumption in the original example. Identify exactly which claim, conclusion or decision becomes unsupported and what evidence could restore it.

<details><summary>Why is freshness part of meaning?</summary>

A correct but stale value can lead to an incorrect or unsafe decision.

</details>

<details><summary>Can two sources both be authoritative?</summary>

Yes, for different elements, contexts, jurisdictions, or times; reconciliation rules must be explicit.

</details>

<details><summary>What is degraded-information behavior?</summary>

Version drift, missingness, latency, semantic exceptions, unauthorized use, confidence degradation, and contract breaches.

## Deliberate practice: build and critique an artifact

The original guide recommends the following weekly exercises:

* Select two mission decisions and enumerate required information.
* Create exchange contracts for location, availability, route status, and acceptance capacity.
* Build a semantic mapping and identify lossy transformations.
* Define authoritative-source and reconciliation rules for conflicting reports.
* Write interoperability tests for normal, stale, missing, conflicting, and unauthorized data.

**Checkpoint:** compare the result with the reference rationale, record discrepancies, correct the source artifact or model, and rerun affected analysis.

**Companion extension:** Produce a one-page *claim–evidence–authority ledger* for the worked case. Include one supported claim, one disputed or untested claim, a dated configuration, an owner, a measure with units, a plausible failure path, an affected stakeholder who may disagree, and an explicit authorization or learning decision. Perform a red-team review: remove one critical source or change one actor's incentive and update the decision.

## Mastery evidence from the original course

**Weekly assessed deliverable:**

**Information and Interoperability Baseline v0.5**: decision-information matrix, information architecture, exchange contracts, semantic mappings, authority/provenance rules, degraded modes, risks, and verification/monitoring plan.

Submit native/textual source, controlled input data, generated views, a change log, and a one-page self-review. Screenshots alone are not acceptable evidence.

**Critical failure modes to avoid:**

* Treating schema validation as full interoperability.
* No units, time basis, identity, provenance, or confidence for mission-critical information.
* Collapsing conflicting sources without preserving disagreement.
* Assuming data may be shared because it technically exists.

**Revision and mastery requirement:**

Demonstrate end-to-end information sufficiency for three mission decisions and close every critical semantic, timing, provenance, security, or policy gap.
