EN.645.753 · Week 06 — Enterprise architecture and capability management

Phase 5 companion · Enterprise Systems Engineering
Central idea: Capabilities and architecture views
Original guide: EN.645.753, Week 6

Learning outcome: Be able to explain the week's mechanism, apply it to a defensible case, identify the boundary of a method and present evidence for the associated engineering decision. This lesson is original companion instruction; source-guide notes are explicitly labeled below.

Explain the concept

Enterprise architecture should support decisions through multiple linked views: outcomes, capabilities, business activities, actors, information, systems, standards, resources and transitions. Capabilities represent what an enterprise can reliably do, not its current department names. Architectures should expose options and dependencies instead of becoming static inventories.

For each capability define a level of performance, scenario, dependency, owning or participating actors, observable evidence, and improvement pathway. Maintain traceability from investment to expected outcome and the assumptions connecting them.

A boundary worth remembering: Replacing an application can change the technology view without improving enterprise capability.

Work through an independent case

A coalition needs capability to coordinate accessible evacuation, not merely a new dispatch platform. The capability requires trained dispatchers, authority to prioritize vehicles, correct curb data, agreements with carriers and tests under emergency scenarios. One platform may help, but does not deliver the capability alone.

How to generalize the reasoning

  1. Purpose: Write the actual mission, enterprise or people-centered outcome. Avoid replacing an outcome with a proposed tool.
  2. Actors and dependencies: Identify who owns the relevant systems, information, resources, decisions and consequences. Separate authority from influence.
  3. Mechanism: Name the claimed relationship: interface, dependency, feedback, incentive, perception, constraint or testable causal mechanism.
  4. Evidence: Define an observable measure, data provenance, time window, alternative explanation and relevant configuration.
  5. Action: Compare at least two interventions and state the preconditions, uncertainty, legitimate decision owner, stop rule and learning plan.

Instructor notes from the guide

  • Capabilities describe the ability to achieve an effect under stated conditions; they are not projects, organizations, or systems.
  • Architecture viewpoints should be selected from stakeholder concerns and decision questions.
  • Correspondences and queries expose inconsistency across value, capability, process, organization, information, and resource views.
  • Capability gaps can arise from coordination, policy, workforce, information, or incentives—not only missing technology.
  • Target capability trajectories need timing, dependencies, maturity, evidence, and retirement of obsolete capability.

The guide's worked illustration

The enterprise initially labels 'shared dispatch platform' as a capability gap. Architecture analysis shows the real gaps are cross-provider eligibility recognition, emergency-priority governance, trusted availability semantics, and trained coordination roles. A platform may enable these capabilities, but purchasing software alone would not close them.

Deliberate practice: build and critique an artifact

The original guide recommends the following weekly exercises:

  1. List the top five enterprise decisions and their stakeholders/concerns.
  2. Define the minimum set of viewpoints and model kinds needed to answer them.
  3. Build a capability map with outcomes, conditions, owners, enabling systems/processes/skills, measures, and dependencies.
  4. Run queries for orphan capabilities, duplicated investments, single-owner dependencies, and missing organizational capability.
  5. Develop current, planned, and target capability trajectories.

Pause after each step and compare the result with the stated decision purpose. Record discrepancies rather than forcing the artifact to match the expected story.

Companion extension: Produce a one-page claim–evidence–authority ledger for the worked case. Include one supported claim, one disputed or untested claim, a dated configuration, an owner, a measure with units, a plausible failure path, an affected stakeholder who may disagree, and an explicit authorization or learning decision. Perform a red-team review: remove one critical source or change one actor's incentive and update the decision.

Mastery evidence from the original course

Weekly assessed deliverable:

Enterprise Architecture and Capability Baseline

Required contents:

  • Stakeholder/concern/viewpoint matrix.
  • Integrated architecture views with source-controlled relationships.
  • Capability dictionary and dependency network.
  • Gap, overlap, fragility, ownership, maturity, and evidence analysis.
  • Current-to-target capability roadmap hypotheses.

Include a one-page review note stating the decision supported, evidence used, assumptions, limitations, unresolved conflicts, and changes made to prior baselines.

Critical failure modes to avoid:

  • Treats systems or projects as capabilities without stating the effect and conditions.
  • Creates architecture views with no stakeholder concern or decision use.
  • Declares a capability gap without checking process, governance, workforce, information, and incentive causes.

Any critical failure requires revision regardless of the numerical score.

Revision and mastery requirement:

Revise until every target capability traces to value, owners, enablers, measures, dependencies, and evidence and each architecture view has a declared decision use.

Minimum weekly score: 80%, with no critical failure and at least Proficient performance on the first two rubric criteria.
