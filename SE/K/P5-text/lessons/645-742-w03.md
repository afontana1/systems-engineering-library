# EN.645.742 · Week 03 — Type A — technical complexity and hard systems thinking

**Phase 5 companion · Management of Complex Systems**

**Original guide:** [EN.645.742, Week 3](../../../C/P5/EN645742.md)

## Explain the concept

Type A technical complexity is relatively amenable to quantitative definition, decomposition, modeling and optimization when goals, decision rights and measures are sufficiently agreed. Use requirements, architectures, trade studies and verification judiciously.

The method's boundary of validity must be explicit. A route algorithm can minimize travel time given assumptions, but it cannot resolve who deserves priority or whether a policy is politically legitimate. When objectives are contested, use a complementary participatory approach rather than extending the optimization model beyond its remit.

**A boundary worth remembering:** A mathematically optimal solution can be institutionally infeasible or ethically objectionable.

## Work through an independent case

Given agreed vehicle capacity and schedules, solve a small transfer-allocation problem and test it with changed demand. Then compare what the model cannot answer: whether displaced riders accept the chosen priority rule.

### How to generalize the reasoning

1. **Purpose:** Write the actual mission, enterprise or people-centered outcome. Avoid replacing an outcome with a proposed tool.
2. **Actors and dependencies:** Identify who owns the relevant systems, information, resources, decisions and consequences. Separate authority from influence.
3. **Mechanism:** Name the claimed relationship: interface, dependency, feedback, incentive, perception, constraint or testable causal mechanism.
4. **Evidence:** Define an observable measure, data provenance, time window, alternative explanation and relevant configuration.
5. **Action:** Compare at least two interventions and state the preconditions, uncertainty, legitimate decision owner, stop rule and learning plan.

### Instructor notes from the guide

* Type A methods are strongest when goals, boundaries, variables, constraints, and decision authority are sufficiently stable.
* A quantitative optimum is conditional on model structure, data, weights, constraints, and omitted effects.
* Technical decomposition can hide feedback and burden transfer across boundaries.
* Verification shows the method was implemented as intended; validation asks whether it is adequate for the decision.
* Hard evidence remains essential even in pluralist or coercive contexts, but it does not resolve legitimacy or value conflict.
* Use Type A to answer bounded questions, then expose its assumptions to other perspectives.

### The guide's worked illustration

Optimize emergency vehicle staging across six hubs under a fixed heat-emergency scenario. The model improves expected response time, but assumes staffing availability, trusted routing data, and agreement that average response time dominates equity and worker fatigue. The solution is useful as one evidence stream, not the regional answer.

Reconstruct the example in a diagram, table, memo, or model. Then state where the example is deliberately simplified and why the same conclusion might not transfer to the regional case.

**Active reading task:** Change one major assumption in the original example. Identify exactly which claim, conclusion or decision becomes unsupported and what evidence could restore it.

## Deliberate practice: build and critique an artifact

The original guide recommends the following weekly exercises:

1. Select one bounded technical decision with an identified authority.
2. Define objectives, constraints, alternatives, measures, data, uncertainty, and excluded effects.
3. Build or reuse a simple optimization, trade-study, or requirements-allocation model.
4. Verify calculations and test sensitivity to weights, demand, and resource assumptions.
5. Write an applicability critique and list what must be examined by later methods.

Pause after each step. Compare the result with the decision purpose and record disagreement, ambiguity, and negative evidence instead of forcing convergence.

**Companion extension:** Produce a one-page *claim–evidence–authority ledger* for the worked case. Include one supported claim, one disputed or untested claim, a dated configuration, an owner, a measure with units, a plausible failure path, an affected stakeholder who may disagree, and an explicit authorization or learning decision. Perform a red-team review: remove one critical source or change one actor's incentive and update the decision.

## Mastery evidence from the original course

**Weekly assessed deliverable:**

**Type A Technical Analysis and Critique**

Required contents:

* bounded problem and decision authority
* objectives, constraints, alternatives, and measures
* reproducible model or analysis
* verification, validation, and sensitivity evidence
* conditional recommendation
* assumption and exclusion register
* critique of whole-problem applicability and handoff to other methods

Include a one-page review note stating the decision supported, approach used, assumptions, boundary judgments, evidence, affected groups, limitations, and changes made to prior baselines.

**Critical failure modes to avoid:**

* Presents the technical optimum as the socially preferred regional outcome.
* Hides objective weights, exclusions, or authority assumptions.
* Uses model precision to imply certainty not supported by data or structure.

Any critical failure requires revision regardless of the numerical score.

**Revision and mastery requirement:**

Approve Type A work only as a bounded evidence product with explicit use conditions—not as the overarching intervention strategy.

Minimum weekly score: 80%, with no critical failure and at least Proficient performance on the first two rubric criteria.
