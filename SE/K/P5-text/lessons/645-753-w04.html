EN.645.753 · Week 04 — Enterprise processes, value streams, information, and work

Phase 5 companion · Enterprise Systems Engineering
Central idea: Value streams and information work
Original guide: EN.645.753, Week 4

Learning outcome: Be able to explain the week's mechanism, apply it to a defensible case, identify the boundary of a method and present evidence for the associated engineering decision. This lesson is original companion instruction; source-guide notes are explicitly labeled below.

Explain the concept

Enterprise work crosses functional boundaries, and the waiting time at handoffs can dominate execution time. A value-stream map connects customer or mission need to funding, authorization, service delivery, feedback and adjustment. Information flows—who knows what, when, and with what authority—must be mapped alongside physical work.

Classify activities as value enabling, required control, avoidable rework and delay. Do not eliminate an independent safety review merely because it adds elapsed time; redesign the evidence flow so the control remains effective while unnecessary waiting decreases.

A boundary worth remembering: Local efficiency improvements may increase end-to-end delay when they add upstream work to a constrained queue.

Work through an independent case

An accessible-service request touches intake, eligibility, dispatch and carrier assignment. Each team says its own processing takes only a day, while total lead time is 23 days. Instrument end-to-end timestamps and returns for correction before assuming a software automation will solve the problem.

How to generalize the reasoning

  1. Purpose: Write the actual mission, enterprise or people-centered outcome. Avoid replacing an outcome with a proposed tool.
  2. Actors and dependencies: Identify who owns the relevant systems, information, resources, decisions and consequences. Separate authority from influence.
  3. Mechanism: Name the claimed relationship: interface, dependency, feedback, incentive, perception, constraint or testable causal mechanism.
  4. Evidence: Define an observable measure, data provenance, time window, alternative explanation and relevant configuration.
  5. Action: Compare at least two interventions and state the preconditions, uncertainty, legitimate decision owner, stop rule and learning plan.

Instructor notes from the guide

  • A process model should represent decisions and state changes, not merely a chronological list of meetings.
  • Pools and message flows help expose independent organizations; lanes alone can imply authority that does not exist.
  • Value-stream measures must distinguish processing time, waiting, rework, transfer, queue, abandonment, and external burden.
  • Shadow processes often reveal unmet needs or infeasible formal controls rather than simple noncompliance.
  • Model validation requires comparison with observed traces, stakeholder walkthroughs, and exception cases.

The guide's worked illustration

The formal incident-to-mobility-support process shows five steps and a 20-minute target. Event records reveal 14 variants, repeated eligibility checks, missing hospital-discharge timestamps, manual phone calls, and an average 74-minute wait. The process model adds message events, data-quality gates, escalation, fallback transport, and funding authorization, revealing that the largest delay is an unowned inter-organizational decision rather than vehicle availability.

Reproduce the example in a small table, model, or notebook before applying the method to the running case. Record where the example is intentionally simplified.

Learning outcomes from the original guide

  1. Define process purpose, boundary, triggers, states, outcomes, actors, information, resources, and controls.
  2. Create BPMN-compatible current-state models for normal and exception/degraded flows.
  3. Develop value-stream and information-flow views with wait, rework, queue, handoff, and burden data.
  4. Identify variants, shadow processes, policy workarounds, and unowned transitions.
  5. Validate the process model against synthetic event data and actor evidence.

Deliberate practice: build and critique an artifact

The original guide recommends the following weekly exercises:

  1. Select three value streams: routine accessible trip, major-event surge, and emergency transfer.
  2. Define trigger, end state, customer/value recipient, owners, and evidence for each.
  3. Model normal and exception paths using collaboration semantics.
  4. Join event records to process steps and calculate processing, waiting, rework, and abandonment.
  5. Conduct a walkthrough from the perspective of operator, rider, hospital, funder, and regulator.

Pause after each step and compare the result with the stated decision purpose. Record discrepancies rather than forcing the artifact to match the expected story.

Companion extension: Produce a one-page claim–evidence–authority ledger for the worked case. Include one supported claim, one disputed or untested claim, a dated configuration, an owner, a measure with units, a plausible failure path, an affected stakeholder who may disagree, and an explicit authorization or learning decision. Perform a red-team review: remove one critical source or change one actor's incentive and update the decision.

Mastery evidence from the original course

Weekly assessed deliverable:

Current-State Process and Value-Stream Baseline

Required contents:

  • Three cross-organizational process models with normal and exception paths.
  • Value-stream table with processing, waiting, rework, queue, abandonment, cost, and burden.
  • Information/data lineage and decision-rights overlays.
  • Variant/conformance assessment with evidence limitations.
  • Prioritized process hypotheses for Week 5 dynamics analysis.

Include a one-page review note stating the decision supported, evidence used, assumptions, limitations, unresolved conflicts, and changes made to prior baselines.

Critical failure modes to avoid:

  • Uses a single swimlane hierarchy that implies nonexistent command authority.
  • Models only the nominal process for an emergency or accessibility claim.
  • Concludes that a bottleneck is causal without evidence or alternative explanations.

Any critical failure requires revision regardless of the numerical score.

Revision and mastery requirement:

Revise until each value stream has validated ownership, information, exception, and measurement semantics and the main delay/rework claims can be reproduced from source data.

Minimum weekly score: 80%, with no critical failure and at least Proficient performance on the first two rubric criteria.
