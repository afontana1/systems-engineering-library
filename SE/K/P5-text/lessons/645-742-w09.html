EN.645.742 · Week 09 — Critical systems theory, critical systems thinking, and methodological critique

Phase 5 companion · Management of Complex Systems
Central idea: Critical methodological critique
Original guide: EN.645.742, Week 9

Learning outcome: Be able to explain the week's mechanism, apply it to a defensible case, identify the boundary of a method and present evidence for the associated engineering decision. This lesson is original companion instruction; source-guide notes are explicitly labeled below.

Explain the concept

Critical systems thinking asks whether a chosen method's assumptions fit the situation and whose interests they serve. Hard methods may conceal values behind weights; soft methods may produce consensus without implementation authority; critical methods may reveal injustice but still need feasible actions.

Write a method-selection argument: problem dimensions, assumptions, expected insight, blind spots, participation constraints and how results will be challenged. Combine methods when doing so yields complementary evidence, not simply more diagrams.

A boundary worth remembering: Mixing methods without an epistemic rationale can multiply contradictions rather than insight.

Work through an independent case

Use queuing analysis to quantify service delay, soft systems mapping to understand competing purposes, and boundary critique to expose who bears missed-service costs. Explain which conclusion each method can legitimately support and what disagreement remains.

How to generalize the reasoning

  1. Purpose: Write the actual mission, enterprise or people-centered outcome. Avoid replacing an outcome with a proposed tool.
  2. Actors and dependencies: Identify who owns the relevant systems, information, resources, decisions and consequences. Separate authority from influence.
  3. Mechanism: Name the claimed relationship: interface, dependency, feedback, incentive, perception, constraint or testable causal mechanism.
  4. Evidence: Define an observable measure, data provenance, time window, alternative explanation and relevant configuration.
  5. Action: Compare at least two interventions and state the preconditions, uncertainty, legitimate decision owner, stop rule and learning plan.

Instructor notes from the guide

  • Every methodology foregrounds some features and backgrounds others.
  • Critical awareness means knowing assumptions, strengths, limits, consequences, and conditions of use.
  • Pluralism seeks complementary insight but does not erase contradictions among methods.
  • Improvement is provisional, contested, and ethically bounded; it should not be reduced to efficiency.
  • Emancipatory concern asks whether arrangements constrain people’s ability to pursue legitimate interests.
  • Reflective practice includes critique of the practitioner, sponsor, intervention process, and claimed evidence.

The guide's worked illustration

A technical optimization, system-dynamics model, SSM inquiry, and CSH critique produce different recommendations. Instead of choosing the most persuasive slide, the practitioner identifies which question each method answers, conflicts in assumptions, and a sequence in which boundary critique reshapes participation, SSM reframes purposes, system dynamics tests long-run mechanisms, and optimization supports a bounded resource decision.

Reconstruct the example in a diagram, table, memo, or model. Then state where the example is deliberately simplified and why the same conclusion might not transfer to the regional case.

Active reading task: Change one major assumption in the original example. Identify exactly which claim, conclusion or decision becomes unsupported and what evidence could restore it.

Deliberate practice: build and critique an artifact

The original guide recommends the following weekly exercises:

  1. Complete the six-approach matrix: complexity dimension, assumptions, questions, outputs, strengths, limitations, ethics, and handoffs.
  2. Map philosophical conflicts and decide which can be sequenced, triangulated, or must remain unresolved.
  3. Define five methodological-pluralism principles for the case.
  4. Revisit “improvement” through technical, organizational, user, and affected-party perspectives.
  5. Conduct a self-red-team on preferred methods and professional identity.

Pause after each step. Compare the result with the decision purpose and record disagreement, ambiguity, and negative evidence instead of forcing convergence.

Companion extension: Produce a one-page claim–evidence–authority ledger for the worked case. Include one supported claim, one disputed or untested claim, a dated configuration, an owner, a measure with units, a plausible failure path, an affected stakeholder who may disagree, and an explicit authorization or learning decision. Perform a red-team review: remove one critical source or change one actor's incentive and update the decision.

Mastery evidence from the original course

Weekly assessed deliverable:

Critical Systems Thinking and Six-Approach Critique

Required contents:

  • six-approach capability/limitation matrix
  • philosophical and methodological assumption map
  • method conflict and complementarity analysis
  • provisional definition of improvement
  • pluralism, power, ethics, and evidence principles
  • practitioner self-critique
  • implications for the intervention strategy

Include a one-page review note stating the decision supported, approach used, assumptions, boundary judgments, evidence, affected groups, limitations, and changes made to prior baselines.

Critical failure modes to avoid:

  • Equates critical thinking with personal skepticism or disagreement.
  • Creates an unstructured toolbox without method logic or conflict analysis.
  • Defines improvement solely as efficiency, performance, or sponsor preference.

Any critical failure requires revision regardless of the numerical score.

Revision and mastery requirement:

Approve the critique only if it can explain why each method is or is not used and can preserve unresolved conflicts honestly.

Minimum weekly score: 80%, with no critical failure and at least Proficient performance on the first two rubric criteria.
