Diagnosing complexity without calling every problem chaotic

Graduate companion concept essay · EN.645.742 · Phase 5

All case data are invented teaching examples; models and calculations are not approved operational or safety evidence.

Complexity has several dimensions: technical interconnection, process variation, dynamic feedback, organizational coordination, plural interpretations and power conflict. A complex problem situation can mix all of them. Categorizing its components does not eliminate interactions; the point is to choose methods with appropriate assumptions and limits.

Complicated versus complex

Repairing a known mechanical assembly may be complicated but tractable through decomposition, specifications and expert analysis. A regional transport redesign changes rider habits, employer scheduling, operator incentives and public trust while being implemented; its problem boundaries and behaviors may evolve. Some parts still benefit from hard optimization. What fails is claiming the optimization settles the whole situation.

Nonlinearity is not chaos

A queue can have a sharp increase in waiting as utilization approaches capacity. For the ideal M/M/1 queue, W=1/(μ−λ) if arrival rate λ is below service rate μ. With capacity 10/hour and demand 9/hour, average time is 1 hour; at demand 9.8/hour, it is 5 hours. This sharp response is nonlinear but does not establish deterministic chaos. The M/M/1 assumptions may not hold for real service.

Diagnostic protocol

Collect evidence for: changing actor strategies, disputed values, feedback, delays, power imbalance, nonlinear thresholds, known versus unknown mechanisms, and observational limits. State which elements are readily modeled and which demand participatory or critical inquiry. Keep a boundary critique alongside every technical model.

Practice. Classify a route optimization problem, a cross-agency funding dispute and the interaction between public trust and ridership. Assign one method and one important blind spot to each.
