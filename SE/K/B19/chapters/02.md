## The problem you are learning to solve

Imagine the regional mobility council meets after an emergency drill. Agencies individually meet many of their own targets, yet some residents still cannot reach essential care. A sponsor wants a decisive engineering answer and a single performance number. You are responsible for making the inquiry sufficiently precise to support action without pretending away divided responsibility, incomplete evidence or affected people.

This week's question is **actors, authority, objectives, legitimacy, and governance**. The key conceptual move is *authority, legitimacy and incentives are distinct*. The decision is not merely whether an artifact can be drawn: it is whether the claims made by that artifact survive a realistic change in authority, timing, evidence or public consequences. We therefore work from a real decision question toward an explicit representation, then test where the representation breaks.

## The central concept, built carefully

A data-sharing council can authorize a protocol, but may not legitimately speak for riders denied accessible transport. Map formal power, contractual rights, informal influence, and those subject to the consequences.

Construct a five-role decision matrix including who can authorize, fund, implement, veto and appeal each proposed change. Compare the delay of centralized approval with the risk of a fragmented rule.

The engineering reasoning should move in a disciplined order. First name the observed or desired outcome, the boundary, the actors who have the right to act, and the time horizon. Next identify the mechanism hypothesized to link an intervention to that outcome. Then choose a representation—network, causal loop, interface contract, process model, probability table, capability map or deliberative inquiry—whose assumptions match the phenomenon. Finally specify evidence able to disconfirm the model. That last step matters because a persuasive representation can still be false, incomplete, or legitimate for only a narrow class of decisions.

### Foundational distinctions from the course guide

* Authority is multidimensional and may be split among funding, policy, operations, data, safety, labor, procurement, and public accountability.
* Influence is not authority; a central network position does not grant the right to direct another organization.
* Governance defines how decisions are proposed, evaluated, authorized, contested, monitored, and revised.
* Legitimacy and consent affect adoption, data access, workforce behavior, and the durability of enterprise change.
* Objective conflict should be modeled, not smoothed into a fictitious shared objective.

### Follow the supplied worked case

A regional steering board can approve a shared data standard but cannot compel a private vendor to expose historical data or require a transit authority to reassign union-covered dispatch work. A feasible governance design combines procurement clauses, a data trust, operating agreements, labor consultation, public reporting, and an escalation path rather than asserting coalition command authority.

### Enterprise governance distributes authority and legitimacy

An enterprise is more than the sum of its projects. It consists of actors with different rights, constraints, incentives, and understandings of value. A technical architecture can establish which interfaces must exist, but it cannot by itself confer authority on an agency to share data or redirect resources. Governance provides the decision structure through which common capabilities are negotiated and conflicts addressed.

Effective governance must distinguish accountability for outcomes from control over individual resources. An agency may be judged on regional service performance while lacking authority over neighboring agencies' schedules. The gap can create chronic coordination failure unless agreements, incentives, and escalation mechanisms bridge it. Legitimacy also matters: a decision can be legally permitted and still provoke resistance if affected groups had no meaningful voice. Enterprise engineering must address both technical capability and the institutions through which capability becomes action.

## Graduate depth: authority, legitimacy, and enterprise decision architecture

### Authority is distributed across formal and informal systems

An enterprise is a social and technical system in which many parties can affect outcomes without possessing a single chain of command. Formal decision rights specify who may authorize a budget, release, or process change; informal influence determines whose information is trusted, which issues are escalated, and which actions actually occur. A governance chart often captures the first and misses the second. Enterprise engineering must distinguish *accountability for an outcome*, *authority to change an asset*, *control of evidence*, and *legitimacy in the eyes of affected stakeholders*.

A decision architecture maps the sequence from observation to analysis, authorization, execution, and evaluation. At each transition, ask what information is available, who owns the decision, who bears the consequences, and what happens when parties disagree. Centralizing every decision can reduce local inconsistency but introduce long queues and weak contextual knowledge. Delegating everything can improve responsiveness while creating incompatible standards and unmanaged shared risks. The relevant trade is between coordination cost and the cost of independent errors under the actual structure of dependencies.

### Principal-agent problems and measurement incentives

When a sponsor delegates work, the implementer may know more about local feasibility and effort than the sponsor. Metrics meant to align interests can then distort behavior. If units are rewarded solely for their own throughput, they may reject difficult cases, move unfinished work across boundaries, or defer maintenance. The enterprise's true mission outcome worsens while departmental dashboards improve. This is not necessarily unethical conduct by individuals; it can be a rational response to incentives embedded in the governing system.

Agency issues become especially important when benefits and burdens fall on different groups. An organization can reduce its budget by transferring work to another department, suppliers, users, or future maintainers. A proper enterprise decision accounts for cost and outcome across the declared boundary and exposes externalities outside it. A narrow financial return calculation can be internally accurate yet strategically misleading if it ignores those displaced burdens.

### Legitimacy, conflict, and decision rights

Legitimate governance does not require unanimous agreement, but it must make affected voices and due-process constraints visible. Technical experts may judge an architecture feasible while operators judge it unacceptable; senior executives may authorize funding but lack the right to redefine regulated public-service obligations. These are different kinds of authority. A governance design should state consultation, veto, appeal, escalation, conflict-of-interest, and change-control arrangements rather than bury disagreements in an averaged stakeholder score.

Organizational networks matter when the problem crosses boundaries. An interdependency matrix can show which units need coordinated decisions; an evidence-provenance graph can show who supplies important facts. Neither determines legitimacy. Formal network centrality is a descriptive property, not automatic justification for control. A highly connected organization may be a critical coordination hub or a bottleneck that requires redundancy. Changes in authority should be evaluated for both efficiency and resilience.

### Northstar enterprise example

Suppose Northstar's transit operator is measured on cost per passenger, municipal agencies on road safety, and an accessibility partner on service to remote users. Closing a costly rural route may improve the transit operator's score while increasing missed appointments, emergency referrals, and burden on other agencies. An enterprise-level review makes these transferred effects visible and separates mandatory access commitments from compensable preferences.

A useful governance redesign might assign a cross-agency council authority over the shared service-level policy while leaving local scheduling authority with operators. It would specify evidence rules, dispute resolution, periodic outcome review, and transparent allocation of shared costs. Its success should be judged by legitimate mission outcomes and reduced coordination failures, not by the number of governance meetings eliminated or committees created. Enterprises are governed through structures that shape behavior; their engineering is therefore inseparable from incentives, knowledge, and consent.

### Where this method stops working

Broad stakeholder attendance is not proof of representation or informed consent; affected people need a usable contest mechanism.

A responsible engineer should separate *model correctness* (the math or logic follows from its premises), *model credibility* (the premises are supported for this use), and *decision legitimacy* (the process respects authority, rights and affected parties). Passing the first test does not guarantee the other two. In particular, neither a hypothetical exercise nor an attractive decision matrix certifies an operational regional service.

## Reading the broader system

The three Phase 5 books offer complementary lenses. The systems-of-systems lens asks which independently managed constituents must cooperate and what technical evidence can travel between them. The enterprise lens asks how resources, incentives, governance and institutional history shape that cooperation. The critical-complexity lens asks whose interpretation dominates, which boundaries exclude harms and when no single technical objective deserves priority. Use all three when a proposed solution changes both technology and public obligations. Their explanations can disagree without one being automatically mistaken: they may be answering different questions.

## Self-check: work before revealing the lab response

1. **Define the model.** What is the unit of analysis and why does it match this week's decision? Name a plausible alternative boundary.
2. **Trace the mechanism.** Which chain of events, institutions or variables connects intervention to outcome? Name one missing link.
3. **Check the evidence.** Which premise is most fragile? What independent observation would falsify it?
4. **Change the case.** Suppose an agency can technically comply with a policy yet shift waiting time onto disabled riders. Revise governance and complaint escalation. Which claims and actors must be reconsidered?
5. **Defend a bounded decision.** What can be responsibly recommended today, to whom, with what review trigger and non-claims?

### Explanations and answer criteria

A proficient answer identifies a bounded outcome, a model whose assumptions are explicit, a provenance chain, a credible alternative explanation, and a named legitimate decision maker. For Question 4, a single undifferentiated average or a redraw of the same diagram is insufficient: explain which causal or normative premise changed and how that affects the recommendation. For Question 5, separate evidence-backed statements from conditional hypotheses and unresolved stakeholder disputes. If your answer cannot state a stop rule or the consequence of error, revise it before proceeding.
