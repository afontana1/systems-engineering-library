## The problem you are learning to solve

Imagine the regional mobility council meets after an emergency drill. Agencies individually meet many of their own targets, yet some residents still cannot reach essential care. A sponsor wants a decisive engineering answer and a single performance number. You are responsible for making the inquiry sufficiently precise to support action without pretending away divided responsibility, incomplete evidence or affected people.

This week's question is **transition architecture, experiments, adoption, evaluation, and sustainment**. The key conceptual move is *transformation is an experiment with adoption mechanisms*. The decision is not merely whether an artifact can be drawn: it is whether the claims made by that artifact survive a realistic change in authority, timing, evidence or public consequences. We therefore work from a real decision question toward an explicit representation, then test where the representation breaks.

## The central concept, built carefully

Treat a pilot as a theory of change: intervention, targeted process, expected intermediate effect, outcome, comparison group, leading indicators and harms to monitor. Plan how people will learn and whether the change can be undone.

An illustrative before/after average falls from 35 to 28 minutes, but seasonal demand falls too. Introduce a comparison corridor before claiming a causal effect.

The engineering reasoning should move in a disciplined order. First name the observed or desired outcome, the boundary, the actors who have the right to act, and the time horizon. Next identify the mechanism hypothesized to link an intervention to that outcome. Then choose a representation—network, causal loop, interface contract, process model, probability table, capability map or deliberative inquiry—whose assumptions match the phenomenon. Finally specify evidence able to disconfirm the model. That last step matters because a persuasive representation can still be false, incomplete, or legitimate for only a narrow class of decisions.

### Foundational distinctions from the course guide

* Transition architectures are real operating states with their own risks, controls, service levels, and authority—not arrows between current and target diagrams.
* Pilots should answer decision questions; activity completion is not evidence of value.
* Adoption depends on workflow fit, incentives, competence, trust, workload, leadership, and local adaptation.
* Benefit realization often occurs after delivery and therefore needs owners, measures, baselines, and continuation decisions.
* Sustainment includes governance, funding, workforce, data quality, model maintenance, audits, and retirement of obsolete processes or systems.

### Transformation evidence requires causal and institutional interpretation

An enterprise transformation can appear successful because performance improves after its launch, but improvement after an intervention is not necessarily improvement caused by it. Demand, funding, staffing, regulation, and learning may have changed simultaneously. Comparative evaluation can reduce ambiguity, but methods such as difference-in-differences require assumptions about comparable trends and absence of important spillovers.

The institutional consequences also matter. An aggregate regional measure may improve while one community experiences longer delays or reduced choice. The decision maker should examine distributional results, adoption burdens, and unintended incentives. A credible transformation review distinguishes technical implementation, observed outcome, causal interpretation, and ethical or governance judgment. These are different questions requiring different evidence. Sustainment then asks whether the structures that produced improvement will persist after special project support disappears.

## Deeper theory: enterprise transformation is a migration of interacting capabilities

A target operating model describes how an enterprise is intended to work, but the enterprise cannot usually be switched from current to target state in one step. It must continue serving stakeholders while processes, software, suppliers, authority structures, and workforce practices change. Transformation is therefore a **migration problem with transient risks**. A target architecture that is attractive in its final state may be impossible or dangerous to reach through the proposed sequence.

### Transition states are real architectures

Suppose a regional mobility coalition plans to unify its dispatch interface. During migration, some agencies may operate the new interface and others an older one. The temporary mixed configuration has its own failure modes: inconsistent identifiers, duplicated bookings, translation errors, and ambiguous authority during incidents. Those are not implementation details that can be ignored until the final architecture is complete. Each transition state is an operational architecture requiring bounded interfaces and assurance.

A transition architecture specifies which old and new capabilities coexist, where translation occurs, who can initiate actions, and how rollback would work. A “big bang” change concentrates dependency and failure risk at one moment. A phased rollout distributes risk but lengthens the period in which mixed configurations must be supported. Neither is universally superior; the choice depends on compatibility, recovery cost, data migration, and the consequences of interruption.

### Adoption is part of system performance

Technology alone does not produce capability. Staff must understand their responsibilities, trust the new information, and be able to carry out changed workflows. A system that offers a technically excellent dashboard may worsen decisions if operators cannot tell which agency owns the displayed status or if they continue relying on an unofficial spreadsheet. Enterprise transformation thus involves habits, incentives, training, professional identity, and informal coordination alongside formal processes.

A useful conceptual model separates technical availability, operational adoption, and realized mission value. Deploying a tool may make it technically available to all agencies, yet only a subset use it correctly. Even high adoption may fail to deliver intended outcomes if the decision process remains bottlenecked by approval authority. Tracking software-login percentages alone can therefore overstate transformation success.

### Learning without producing an ungoverned experiment

A pilot can test a proposed operating model before expansion, but the pilot environment may be privileged. Staff may receive extra technical support; managers may waive routine approvals; partner agencies may cooperate unusually closely. Those conditions can create a false impression of scalability. Evidence must distinguish what worked because of the intervention from what worked because of exceptional pilot support.

A sound implementation theory identifies which mechanisms must persist at scale—such as shared data meanings, distributed decision rights, and rapid resolution of interface exceptions. It also identifies constraints that may emerge only when many agencies participate, such as support queues, supplier capacity, and conflicting service priorities. Evaluation should examine leading operational mechanisms and lagging outcomes, rather than declare success after the first positive dashboard report.

### Backward compatibility is a social as well as technical property

A protocol can be wire-compatible while the enterprise becomes operationally incompatible. Suppose a legacy agency is permitted to send a “request canceled” event, but the new network interprets that event as an immediate release of reserved capacity. If the legacy agency previously used it to mean “pending manual review,” a technical translation changes business obligations. A responsible migration maintains a semantic contract and documents any change of authority or process meaning.

Transformation should also include an exit and recovery strategy. If a pilot exposes unacceptable safety or equity effects, what can be reversed, and what has already become irreversible? Data conversion, vendor contracts, workforce attrition, and decommissioning decisions may make rollback costly. Contingency options should therefore be considered before commitments eliminate them.

Enterprise engineering succeeds when the **path** to the target creates manageable risk and real learning, not merely when the final architecture diagram appears coherent. A credible transformation argument describes the transition states, their temporary controls, the adoption mechanism, what evidence would trigger revision, and how mission performance will be protected throughout change.

### Where this method stops working

A statistically significant metric shift may still be irrelevant if a high-risk group deteriorates or the process cannot be sustained.

A responsible engineer should separate *model correctness* (the math or logic follows from its premises), *model credibility* (the premises are supported for this use), and *decision legitimacy* (the process respects authority, rights and affected parties). Passing the first test does not guarantee the other two. In particular, neither a hypothetical exercise nor an attractive decision matrix certifies an operational regional service.

## Reading the broader system

The three Phase 5 books offer complementary lenses. The systems-of-systems lens asks which independently managed constituents must cooperate and what technical evidence can travel between them. The enterprise lens asks how resources, incentives, governance and institutional history shape that cooperation. The critical-complexity lens asks whose interpretation dominates, which boundaries exclude harms and when no single technical objective deserves priority. Use all three when a proposed solution changes both technology and public obligations. Their explanations can disagree without one being automatically mistaken: they may be answering different questions.

## Self-check: work before revealing the lab response

1. **Define the model.** What is the unit of analysis and why does it match this week's decision? Name a plausible alternative boundary.
2. **Trace the mechanism.** Which chain of events, institutions or variables connects intervention to outcome? Name one missing link.
3. **Check the evidence.** Which premise is most fragile? What independent observation would falsify it?
4. **Change the case.** Suppose a pilot succeeds for volunteers only. Explain selection bias and design a more credible expansion test. Which claims and actors must be reconsidered?
5. **Defend a bounded decision.** What can be responsibly recommended today, to whom, with what review trigger and non-claims?

### Explanations and answer criteria

A proficient answer identifies a bounded outcome, a model whose assumptions are explicit, a provenance chain, a credible alternative explanation, and a named legitimate decision maker. For Question 4, a single undifferentiated average or a redraw of the same diagram is insufficient: explain which causal or normative premise changed and how that affects the recommendation. For Question 5, separate evidence-backed statements from conditional hypotheses and unresolved stakeholder disputes. If your answer cannot state a stop rule or the consequence of error, revise it before proceeding.

**Specific red-team test:** A statistically significant metric shift may still be irrelevant if a high-risk group deteriorates or the process cannot be sustained.
