## The problem you are learning to solve

Imagine the regional mobility council meets after an emergency drill. Agencies individually meet many of their own targets, yet some residents still cannot reach essential care. A sponsor wants a decisive engineering answer and a single performance number. You are responsible for making the inquiry sufficiently precise to support action without pretending away divided responsibility, incomplete evidence or affected people.

This week's question is **target operating models, governance, and transformation alternatives**. The key conceptual move is *a target operating model is a bundle of choices*. The decision is not merely whether an artifact can be drawn: it is whether the claims made by that artifact survive a realistic change in authority, timing, evidence or public consequences. We therefore work from a real decision question toward an explicit representation, then test where the representation breaks.

## The central concept, built carefully

Compare centralized allocation, federated coordination and negotiated hybrid governance. Evaluate who owns a case, who resolves conflicts, which platform is authoritative and how exceptions reach a real decision maker.

Use a decision-rights matrix for emergency overrides and trace the exception from trigger through approval, execution, audit and appeal.

The engineering reasoning should move in a disciplined order. First name the observed or desired outcome, the boundary, the actors who have the right to act, and the time horizon. Next identify the mechanism hypothesized to link an intervention to that outcome. Then choose a representation—network, causal loop, interface contract, process model, probability table, capability map or deliberative inquiry—whose assumptions match the phenomenon. Finally specify evidence able to disconfirm the model. That last step matters because a persuasive representation can still be false, incomplete, or legitimate for only a narrow class of decisions.

### Foundational distinctions from the course guide

* A target operating model describes how value is created and governed, not only a target IT architecture.
* Alternatives should vary governance and operating logic: centralized services, federated standards, shared utility, contractual network, mutual-aid compact, or governance-first paths.
* Architecture correspondences expose contradictions such as decentralized accountability with centralized data control.
* Transformation feasibility includes political, legal, workforce, procurement, data, supplier, and change-capacity constraints.
* A low-automation or governance-first alternative is often necessary to reveal whether technology is actually the limiting factor.

## The mechanism beneath the method

A target operating model specifies the future interaction of authority, process, information, incentives and capabilities.

### Distinguishing related ideas

An organization chart alone cannot explain whether cross-functional decisions will be made quickly and legitimately.

### Interpretation and boundary conditions

Transformation failure may arise because the proposed governance contradicts the performance measures or resource allocation rules.

### Follow the supplied worked case

Alternative A creates a centralized regional mobility authority; Alternative B keeps independent operators but creates shared standards, a data trust, mutual-aid agreements, and pooled emergency funding; Alternative C uses a commercial platform and performance contracts. Authority analysis makes A legally infeasible in the horizon; C creates lock-in and weak public accountability. B provides less central control but better feasibility, resilience, and legitimacy when paired with staged technical services.

## Graduate depth: target operating models allocate decisions and incentives

### Organization design is part of system architecture

A target operating model describes how an enterprise will make decisions, provide services, coordinate resources, monitor performance, and adapt. It is not merely an organization chart. Two enterprises with identical technical equipment can perform differently because authority, incentives, information delays, and escalation paths differ. A governance change can alter system dynamics as strongly as an equipment change. The operating model should allocate decision rights at the level where relevant information is available while preserving accountability for cross-boundary consequences.

Consider the tension between local optimization and enterprise outcomes. An agency rewarded for minimizing its own dispatch cost may reject cross-border requests that are cheap for the region as a whole. If the regional mission values completed accessible journeys, the metric and decision authority must span the handoff. Otherwise a rational local actor can contribute to an irrational enterprise result. The principal-agent problem is not solved by writing a new policy alone; incentives, data, monitoring, and the credibility of enforcement matter.

### Decision latency and information architecture

Suppose a service incident takes 12 hours to detect, 20 to assemble evidence, 30 to obtain cross-agency approval, and 10 to implement a change. The 72-hour response is not mostly an implementation problem. A target operating model could authorize predefined responses locally while escalating only changes that cross safety, legal, or funding thresholds. This requires a documented delegation rule, access to current evidence, auditability, and an escalation route for unanticipated conditions. Decentralization without shared semantics can produce conflicting decisions; centralization without timely local information can create dangerous delay.

In feedback terms, an enterprise responds to observed performance after information and decision delays. If leadership changes policy aggressively based on stale indicators, the organization may overshoot, reverse, and oscillate between competing initiatives. A stable operating model defines measurement cadence and review thresholds consistent with the physical and organizational response time. Do not punish teams for a lagging outcome before their intervention could plausibly influence it.

### Comparing alternative governance architectures

One design centralizes service standards and data definitions while leaving day-to-day resource dispatch local. Another centralizes both operations and investment decisions. A third uses a federated board with negotiated service obligations. Compare them using the same mission scenarios and governance failure cases: conflicting priorities, missing data, local outage, urgent safety exception, and funding dispute. Metrics should include mission outcomes, equity of service, time to authorized action, auditability, adaptability, and administration cost.

A weighted score may help organize preferences, but legal duties and safety obligations cannot automatically be traded against lower administrative effort. Model where obligations are hard constraints, where legitimate stakeholders disagree, and where political or contractual authority limits the feasible set. An enterprise model that presumes unanimous cooperation is not a realistic design of governance.

### Transformation as a controlled hypothesis

Northstar may pilot shared dispatch governance in two districts while maintaining a protected fallback. The pilot should measure end-to-end service, not just board meeting frequency, and should record impacts on frontline work. If the measured benefits depend on unusually cooperative leaders or temporary project staff, scaling may fail. Define who owns residual risks after the pilot and how the operating model can be revised when evidence challenges its premises.

This is the connection between enterprise architecture and institutional design: a blueprint becomes a working enterprise only when people possess the authority, information, incentive, and capacity to perform the specified behavior. The target operating model should therefore be evaluated as a dynamic sociotechnical system rather than accepted as a static administrative diagram.

### Where this method stops working

Organizational redesign without funding, incentives and changed routines produces diagrams rather than changed behavior.

A responsible engineer should separate *model correctness* (the math or logic follows from its premises), *model credibility* (the premises are supported for this use), and *decision legitimacy* (the process respects authority, rights and affected parties). Passing the first test does not guarantee the other two. In particular, neither a hypothetical exercise nor an attractive decision matrix certifies an operational regional service.

## Reading the broader system

The three Phase 5 books offer complementary lenses. The systems-of-systems lens asks which independently managed constituents must cooperate and what technical evidence can travel between them. The enterprise lens asks how resources, incentives, governance and institutional history shape that cooperation. The critical-complexity lens asks whose interpretation dominates, which boundaries exclude harms and when no single technical objective deserves priority. Use all three when a proposed solution changes both technology and public obligations. Their explanations can disagree without one being automatically mistaken: they may be answering different questions.

## Self-check: work before revealing the lab response

1. **Define the model.** What is the unit of analysis and why does it match this week's decision? Name a plausible alternative boundary.
2. **Trace the mechanism.** Which chain of events, institutions or variables connects intervention to outcome? Name one missing link.
3. **Check the evidence.** Which premise is most fragile? What independent observation would falsify it?
4. **Change the case.** Suppose a hybrid model improves dispatch speed but leaves incident ownership ambiguous. Repair the operating contract. Which claims and actors must be reconsidered?
5. **Defend a bounded decision.** What can be responsibly recommended today, to whom, with what review trigger and non-claims?

### Explanations and answer criteria

A proficient answer identifies a bounded outcome, a model whose assumptions are explicit, a provenance chain, a credible alternative explanation, and a named legitimate decision maker. For Question 4, a single undifferentiated average or a redraw of the same diagram is insufficient: explain which causal or normative premise changed and how that affects the recommendation. For Question 5, separate evidence-backed statements from conditional hypotheses and unresolved stakeholder disputes. If your answer cannot state a stop rule or the consequence of error, revise it before proceeding.
