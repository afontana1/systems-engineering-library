## The problem you are learning to solve

Imagine the regional mobility council meets after an emergency drill. Agencies individually meet many of their own targets, yet some residents still cannot reach essential care. A sponsor wants a decisive engineering answer and a single performance number. You are responsible for making the inquiry sufficiently precise to support action without pretending away divided responsibility, incomplete evidence or affected people.

This week's question is **value, outcomes, measures, incentives, and burden transfer**. The key conceptual move is *outcome hierarchies must detect displacement*. The decision is not merely whether an artifact can be drawn: it is whether the claims made by that artifact survive a realistic change in authority, timing, evidence or public consequences. We therefore work from a real decision question toward an explicit representation, then test where the representation breaks.

## The central concept, built carefully

Start with equitable emergency access rather than vehicles dispatched. A metric such as average response time can improve if hard-to-reach passengers are dropped from the denominator. Connect activities, intermediate outcomes, mission benefits and distributional effects.

For neighborhoods A and B, compute completion 90/100 and 30/60: aggregate 120/160=75%, but subgroup rates are 90% and 50%. Report both.

The engineering reasoning should move in a disciplined order. First name the observed or desired outcome, the boundary, the actors who have the right to act, and the time horizon. Next identify the mechanism hypothesized to link an intervention to that outcome. Then choose a representation—network, causal loop, interface contract, process model, probability table, capability map or deliberative inquiry—whose assumptions match the phenomenon. Finally specify evidence able to disconfirm the model. That last step matters because a persuasive representation can still be false, incomplete, or legitimate for only a narrow class of decisions.

### Foundational distinctions from the course guide

* Enterprise value is plural and distributed. A regional average can improve while accessibility, neighborhood, workforce, or emergency outcomes worsen.
* Measures require operational definitions, provenance, context, uncertainty, owners, thresholds, and prescribed responses.
* Incentives can make an apparently irrational enterprise outcome locally rational for every actor.
* Leading indicators support intervention; lagging outcomes show whether value was actually realized.
* Measure portfolios need anti-gaming and burden-transfer checks.

Enterprise measures shape incentives and therefore change the behavior they observe.

## Deeper theory: enterprise value is distributed across actors and time

### Measures sit inside causal and institutional systems

An enterprise objective such as reliable emergency access is not a single process output. It is a joint outcome produced by agencies with different mandates, budgets, accountability rules, and affected populations. A value hierarchy distinguishes fundamental objectives—what stakeholders ultimately care about—from means objectives that contribute to them. Faster dispatch is a means, while timely access to needed care is closer to a fundamental outcome. Measuring the means alone risks declaring success when downstream capacity or geographic inequity prevents real benefit.

Value also depends on whose perspective is counted and over what horizon. A cost-saving route consolidation may improve one agency's budget while increasing patient travel burden, unpaid caregiver time, or clinic overload. An enterprise value model should state which consequences are included, how distribution matters, and whether certain harms are noncompensatory. Stakeholder preferences may be legitimately different; the purpose is not to hide disagreements inside a synthetic average. Governance must explain how conflicting interests inform a publicly accountable decision.

### Goodhart effects emerge when actors respond to the indicator

A performance indicator used only for diagnosis can become distorted when tied to sanctions or rewards. If an agency is measured on calls closed within ten minutes, it may reclassify complex calls, transfer them to another unit, or close them prematurely. The metric improves while the underlying outcome deteriorates. This is a feedback mechanism: policy changes incentives, incentives change behavior and recording practices, and the observed data cease to represent the original construct. Metric gaming is therefore not just individual misconduct; it can be a predictable structural response to an incomplete control system.

Use a measurement architecture that separates service volume, quality, outcomes, and burden transfer. Track distributions and exception cases alongside central tendencies. An average response time can improve while remote residents experience longer waits; a fall in reported incidents may mean reduced reporting access. Triangulate administrative data with operational observation and affected-population feedback. Every important measure needs a definition, owner, data lineage, known exclusions, and a reasoned link to the intended outcome.

### Burden transfer is a cross-boundary conservation problem

Improvements often move work rather than eliminate it. Automating a municipal dispatch step may reduce operator labor but increase callers' documentation requirements or shift triage to hospitals. If total effort is defined across the enterprise boundary, one organization's reduction can be another actor's increase. The analogy to conservation is useful but incomplete: human burden is not a single conserved physical quantity, and changes can create or destroy real work through learning, automation, or avoidance. The practical task is to trace consequences across institutional boundaries before claiming net efficiency.

Causal maps should include queue displacement, delayed costs, and capacity feedback. Suppose a policy reduces transport dispatch time but funnels more patients to one constrained facility. The receiving queue may grow nonlinearly as utilization approaches capacity. A local performance win can then harm the end-to-end mission. Test the value claim under realistic demand coupling and assess both immediate and deferred effects, such as clinician fatigue or diminished public trust.

### Incentive alignment is not the same as central control

A regional enterprise rarely has unlimited authority over its constituents. It must use negotiated service agreements, transparent outcome definitions, shared evidence, and escalation mechanisms. Joint performance metrics can motivate coordination, but they can also obscure accountability if every participant can blame the collective. Assign responsibility for measurable contributions while evaluating the aggregate mission outcome. Define what decisions each actor can make, what information must be shared, and how conflicts are resolved. Enterprise value is credible when the system of objectives, incentives, boundaries, and evidence makes the real distribution of benefits and costs visible.

### Where this method stops working

Optimizing a proxy can induce strategic behavior; incentives tied only to throughput often exclude slower cases.

A responsible engineer should separate *model correctness* (the math or logic follows from its premises), *model credibility* (the premises are supported for this use), and *decision legitimacy* (the process respects authority, rights and affected parties). Passing the first test does not guarantee the other two. In particular, neither a hypothetical exercise nor an attractive decision matrix certifies an operational regional service.

## Reading the broader system

The three Phase 5 books offer complementary lenses. The systems-of-systems lens asks which independently managed constituents must cooperate and what technical evidence can travel between them. The enterprise lens asks how resources, incentives, governance and institutional history shape that cooperation. The critical-complexity lens asks whose interpretation dominates, which boundaries exclude harms and when no single technical objective deserves priority. Use all three when a proposed solution changes both technology and public obligations. Their explanations can disagree without one being automatically mistaken: they may be answering different questions.

## Step 03 — NS-ENT19.1 enterprise transformation laboratory
### Value systems require disaggregation, not a single performance number

Define enterprise value \(V(x)\) as a stakeholder-negotiated *comparison*, not a universal scalar. A defensible measurement system separates beneficiary outcomes, process leading indicators, resources, distributional harms and legitimacy. The [value dictionary](../case/value-measure-dictionary.csv) specifies numerators, denominators, observation windows, owners, disaggregation and limitations. For a four-hour emergency screen, the accessible fulfillment fraction is \(F_A=A/62\) and standard fulfillment is \(F_S=S/186\). These are invented **trip-slot** capacities, not observed completed journeys, and they cannot measure dignity, delay, consent or physical safety.

A simple fairness diagnostic is \(\Delta=F_S-F_A\). Under the synthetic nominal **status quo**, \(F_A=55/62=0.8871\) while \(F_S=178/186=0.9570\), so \(\Delta\approx0.0699\), or almost seven percentage points. A weighted overall rate \((55+178)/(62+186)=233/248\approx93.95\%\) conceals that the accessible group alone misses a 90% threshold. A majority-weighted aggregate can improve even while a smaller group falls behind: this is a textbook counterexample to optimizing average customer throughput.

### Value modeling and burden transfer

A proposed multi-criteria model can be written

\[
U(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_i u_i(x),\qquad w_i\ge0,\quad\sum_iw_i=1,
\]

but additivity assumes preference independence, agreed scaling, and legitimate weights. Such assumptions are **not** supplied by the course data. State explicitly which objectives are noncompensatory gates, such as lawful use, accessibility, safety and minimum workforce protections. Economic benefits must not compensate for an illegal data transfer. If weights are hypothetical, publish sensitivity and avoid presenting \(U\) as an adopted community preference. An observed increase in adoption or average trip slots cannot substitute for a distributional impact assessment.

In the capacity-first option, nominal accessible supply reaches \(55+12=67\), capped at demand 62; standard supply reaches \(178+3+13=194\), capped at 186. Thus both display 100% in the *screen*, yet neither number includes dwell time, journey completion, experienced harms or uncertainty. In workforce stress, the same option reaches 60/62=96.77% accessible and at least 186/186 standard. The [scenario results](../case/scenario-screen.csv) preserve the denominators so a reviewer can audit those claims.

### Exercise and worked answer

**Exercise.** A policy delivers 60 of 62 accessible slots and 170 of 186 standard slots. Compute each fulfillment fraction and whether both meet the 90% screen. **Solution.** \(60/62=96.77\%\) and \(170/186=91.40\%\). Both clear the numerical threshold, but this says nothing about whether any resident completed an end-to-end safe trip. Identify the affected communities and independently review complaint or exclusion evidence before claiming equitable benefit.

**Review task:** specify one countermeasure per claimed benefit and one burden whose owner is outside the coalition office. Use the measure dictionary, not an untraceable dashboard average.
