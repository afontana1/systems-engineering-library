Chapter 2 — Evidence-based stakeholder needs

Book 6: System Conceptual Design · Week 2 — Stakeholder evidence and current-state analysis

The engineering problem

At this point the Northstar team is still free to select among competing mobility-service concepts. A proposed autonomous shuttle is one hypothesis, not the definition of the problem. The governing question this week is evidence-based stakeholder needs. Decisions in this course are cumulative: an assumption made now will reappear as an allocation, a test condition, or a readiness limitation later. Always preserve the exact scenario, configuration and affected stakeholder when making a claim.

One of the most useful distinctions in systems engineering is the difference between a claim, a model, and evidence. A claim says what must or does hold. A model explains or predicts behavior under named conditions. Evidence is a measured or inspected outcome from a known configuration. Moving among these three without recording assumptions produces false confidence even when all calculations are correct.

Build the intuition

A stakeholder statement contains observation, meaning, desired outcome, and proposed solution; these are different data types. Convert verbatim interviews into claims with conditions, frequency, source, and uncertainty before assigning priorities. One dramatic interview must not silently become a fleet-wide probability estimate.

There are two levels of reasoning. At the local level, ask whether the calculation, drawing, allocation, or test is internally correct. At the mission level, ask whether that local statement actually changes the stakeholder's decision. The distinction prevents a common mistake: optimizing a subsystem characteristic while harming the system-level outcome. In this case, an apparently efficient dispatch plan can be unacceptable if it systematically excludes accessible requests during event surges.

A second discipline is to make the operating envelope visible. State the load, timing, participants, environmental assumptions, failure modes and decision authority. If the result depends on a parameter you have not measured, classify it as conditional. An uncertainty is not automatically a defect, but unexplained certainty is one.

A robust need model distinguishes the actor, the situation, the undesired outcome, and the value of changing it. Different actors can value the same outcome differently. Dispatchers may prioritize recovering service after disruptions, passengers may prioritize predictable arrival, and maintainers may prioritize safe access to components. These are legitimate perspectives even when they conflict. The designer's obligation is to expose tradeoffs and decision authority rather than to average away disagreements before alternatives have been generated.

Stakeholder statements mix observations, preferences, constraints, predictions, and proposed solutions. “We need a larger vehicle” may actually mean that peak-hour passengers are being left behind; it could also reflect anxiety about reliability or a contractual commitment to existing equipment. Conceptual design begins by asking what observable condition makes the statement important, not by accepting the proposed implementation as a requirement. Interviews and workshops reveal plausible hypotheses, but they do not establish their frequency or consequence without further inquiry.

Stakeholder needs are evidence about value, not a feature list

Deeper theory: stakeholder evidence and the risk of solving the wrong problem

The usual picture of conceptual design begins with stakeholders telling engineers what they need. Reality is less orderly. Stakeholders observe different parts of the service, remember exceptional experiences, have different incentives, and may already have a favored solution. A current-state analysis therefore needs to distinguish observation, interpretation, preference, and proposed intervention. These are different kinds of knowledge and should not be promoted into requirements with equal confidence.

Consider two statements: “I waited 25 minutes last Tuesday” and “We need autonomous shuttles.” The first is a report about an event that can potentially be corroborated with records; the second is a proposed architecture that might or might not address a broader mobility problem. A third statement—“late-night riders feel unsafe”—is an experience with both a measurable context and important subjective content. The engineering task is not to discard subjective experience but to understand the mechanism by which the service creates or relieves harm. That mechanism might involve lighting, information reliability, boarding access, traffic interactions or the availability of human assistance.

Evidence must be sampled from the actual operational population

A satisfaction survey may systematically omit riders who stopped using the service because it failed them. A study of completed trips excludes denied requests. A stakeholder workshop may overrepresent institutional managers and underrepresent contract staff or passengers with disabilities. These are forms of selection bias: the process used to observe the system changes the population represented by the resulting data. More survey responses from the same biased frame will not fix the problem.

The conceptual model should therefore define a target population and an observation process. If the mission is dependable access to evening services, the target includes people who attempted but could not obtain rides. Useful evidence may combine service logs, rejected requests, interviews, field observation, and operational records; each source has different biases and limitations. Triangulation is valuable because independent forms of observation can reveal a discrepancy that one data source hides.

Current state is a causal system, not a list of complaints

Suppose riders report long waits. A naive explanation is insufficient fleet size. Another is inefficient dispatch. A third is bursty demand after large events. A fourth is a policy that reserves vehicles for one route while another route accumulates requests. Each hypothesis suggests a different intervention. The team needs an operational model of arrival demand, available service capacity, routing, boarding times, charging, and exception handling before interpreting the symptom.

A simple queue illustration shows the stakes. Under an idealized M/M/1 model with arrival rate \(\lambda\) and service rate \(\mu\), the expected time in the system is \(W=1/(\mu-\lambda)\) when \(\lambda<\mu\). At \(\lambda=8\) and \(\mu=10\) per hour, \(W=0.5\) hour. This result does not imply that a real shuttle system is a single exponential server. It illustrates how apparently modest utilization can create substantial delays under variability. If demand is batched, travel times are spatial, or service is scheduled, a different model is needed. The lesson is not the formula itself but the causal question: which mechanism is producing the observed wait?

A need is not a design constraint unless the boundary requires it

Stakeholder needs are often phrased using technologies that were available at the time of the interview. “Build a smartphone app” may express a desire for easily discoverable service information. Treating the app as an immutable requirement rules out alternatives such as accessible kiosks, displays and assisted booking. Concept-neutral formulation separates the desired operational outcome from candidate means of achieving it. This preserves the space of feasible concepts long enough for genuine comparison.

However, neutrality cannot be taken to extremes. Some context conditions are genuinely fixed: safety regulations, physical interfaces to an existing fleet, accessibility obligations or a contractual requirement to interoperate with a particular emergency service. These are constraints on the concept space rather than preferences. Confusing constraints with aspirations leads to impossible trade studies in which legal or safety obligations are allowed to be compensated by a lower cost score.

Credibility comes from maintaining alternative explanations

Good conceptual design keeps competing hypotheses alive until relevant evidence can distinguish them. If long waits are caused primarily by inaccessible booking, adding vehicles may improve a metric for existing users while doing little for excluded users. If waits reflect synchronized peaks, a targeted schedule change may outperform a costly fleet expansion. A current-state analysis should therefore make explicit the proposed causal pathway from intervention to outcome and record which other pathways could explain the same observations.

The result is not a definitive “problem statement” carved in stone. It is a transparent account of stakeholder values, operational mechanisms, current evidence and unresolved uncertainty. That account prevents the project from mistaking a technically elegant solution for a solution to the mission.

Worked engineering case

One wheelchair user reports a pickup 25 minutes late during a football game. Log event-day conditions and distinguish a single 25-minute lateness observation from the unknown distribution of wait times.

Reason through the case in this order:

  1. Frame the decision. What is the mission claim being evaluated? State who accepts or rejects the conclusion.
  2. List supplied information. Label each number or statement as requirement, observation, assumption, or estimate; name the units.
  3. Check structure and arithmetic. Show the formula, interfaces, event chain, or causal link; verify that numerator, denominator, configuration and time window agree.
  4. Challenge the favorable interpretation. Identify a plausible failure or operating condition that could reverse it. If a case includes a conditional probability or a percentile, do not promote it into a universal guarantee.
  5. Select the next evidence. Write a measurement or review criterion that could change your decision, rather than an activity with no pass/fail interpretation.

These steps do not automatically prove the design. They prevent a correct local calculation from being offered as a conclusion about unmeasured system behavior. When assumptions are uncertain, calculate sensitivity or state the required experiment instead of inventing precision.

Competing explanations and failure modes

A popular preference may conflict with a nonnegotiable accessibility or safety outcome. This is why reviewers must distinguish unknown from failed, and not applicable from not tested. A robust decision record preserves an alternative hypothesis, the evidence needed to discriminate it, and the owner of that next investigation. Ask what would happen if the controlling requirement changed, a data feed became stale, or an operational context moved outside the model boundary.

How this connects to the next part of the lifecycle

In the concept stage, design freedom is preserved and choices are screened against needs and feasibility. In design and integration, provisional choices become owned interfaces and controlled budgets. In test and evaluation, those statements become hypotheses tested against configurations and populations. Keeping this chain visible matters more than using the same document format across teams.

Your prior connection is Reopen the problem and audit the incumbent baseline; the next connection is Desired end state, ConOps, scenarios, and mission calculator v0.1. Treat this chapter as a bridge between them, not a standalone checklist. In the final review, someone must be able to reconstruct how a stakeholder need became a claim, why an option was chosen, what was actually implemented and which evidence supports operation.

Try the reasoning yourself

Problem A — Construct the decision. Create an evidence ledger for six stakeholder claims; identify which are observations, inferences and unmet needs.

Problem B — Adversarial review. Which stakeholders can be harmed without being frequent system users?

Problem C — Evidence and limitation. Propose one test or analysis with an acceptance criterion, a configuration identifier, a limit on generalization and a named decision owner. Explain what you would do if the result were borderline.

<details><summary>Worked answer guidance (look only after trying)</summary>

For A, produce the requested artifact with stable identifiers, explicit scenario assumptions and at least one exceptional condition. The answer is acceptable only if someone else could reconstruct the logic; plausible words alone are insufficient.

For B, evaluate the question against the actual mission, not just the subsystem metric. An answer must name the stakeholder affected and a plausible mechanism by which the current interpretation could fail.

For C, use a controlled artifact and a predeclared decision rule. A measured result can support only the specified configuration, environment and sampling conditions. Where that evidence does not yet exist, mark the claim open and assign a closure action rather than marking it pass.
