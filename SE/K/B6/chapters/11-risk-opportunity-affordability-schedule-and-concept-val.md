## The engineering problem

At this point the Northstar team is still free to select among competing mobility-service concepts. A proposed autonomous shuttle is one hypothesis, not the definition of the problem. The governing question this week is **conditional concept validation**. Decisions in this course are cumulative: an assumption made now will reappear as an allocation, a test condition, or a readiness limitation later. Always preserve the exact scenario, configuration and affected stakeholder when making a claim.

One of the most useful distinctions in systems engineering is the difference between a *claim*, a *model*, and *evidence*. A claim says what must or does hold. A model explains or predicts behavior under named conditions. Evidence is a measured or inspected outcome from a known configuration. Moving among these three without recording assumptions produces false confidence even when all calculations are correct.

## Build the intuition

A selected concept carries a validity envelope, unresolved risks and gates for transferring it to detailed design. Risk and opportunity are scenario-specific and require owners, triggers, actions, and closure criteria. Conditional approval is a controlled decision, not an excuse to treat unknowns as passed tests.

There are two levels of reasoning. At the local level, ask whether the calculation, drawing, allocation, or test is internally correct. At the mission level, ask whether that local statement actually changes the stakeholder's decision. The distinction prevents a common mistake: optimizing a subsystem characteristic while harming the system-level outcome. In this case, an apparently efficient dispatch plan can be unacceptable if it systematically excludes accessible requests during event surges.

A second discipline is to make the operating envelope visible. State the load, timing, participants, environmental assumptions, failure modes and decision authority. If the result depends on a parameter you have not measured, classify it as *conditional*. An uncertainty is not automatically a defect, but unexplained certainty is one.

### Validation is a test of problem framing as well as performance

A conceptual baseline may satisfy all the requirements generated by its own design team and still miss the stakeholders' real purpose. Concept validation asks whether the proposed system would actually improve outcomes in plausible operational contexts. A scheduling model might demonstrate shorter average waits while making service unreliable for riders who need assistance or travel outside the modeled peak period. The problem is not necessarily a mathematical error; it may be a decision boundary that excluded relevant people or scenarios.

Because an early concept is not a deployed system, validation relies on multiple kinds of evidence: stakeholder walkthroughs, simulations, prototypes, operational analogues, and expert challenge. Each carries different uncertainty. A mock-up can reveal interface confusion but cannot establish fleet-level reliability. A simulation can compare concepts under hypothetical demand but cannot establish that its demand distribution is real. The appropriate conclusion is therefore conditional: which aspects of intended use are supported, which remain assumptions, and which future decisions depend on resolving them.

## Worked engineering case

Bounded-zone automation may be acceptable as a future increment only if a demonstration retires perception and remote-assist risks before procurement or deployment gates.

**Reason through the case in this order:**

1. **Frame the decision.** What is the mission claim being evaluated? State who accepts or rejects the conclusion.
2. **List supplied information.** Label each number or statement as requirement, observation, assumption, or estimate; name the units.
3. **Check structure and arithmetic.** Show the formula, interfaces, event chain, or causal link; verify that numerator, denominator, configuration and time window agree.
4. **Challenge the favorable interpretation.** Identify a plausible failure or operating condition that could reverse it. If a case includes a conditional probability or a percentile, do not promote it into a universal guarantee.
5. **Select the next evidence.** Write a measurement or review criterion that could change your decision, rather than an activity with no pass/fail interpretation.

These steps do not automatically prove the design. They prevent a correct local calculation from being offered as a conclusion about unmeasured system behavior. When assumptions are uncertain, calculate sensitivity or state the required experiment instead of inventing precision.

## Graduate synthesis: validating a concept before the design is fixed

### Concept validation asks whether the right problem is being solved

At conceptual design, evidence is incomplete by definition: detailed hardware, software, operating procedures, and trained crews may not exist. The central error is to confuse a plausible prediction with demonstrated mission value. Validation of the *concept* asks whether the proposed service addresses stakeholder needs in its intended context; verification of the model asks whether its calculations and implementations conform to specification. A model can be verified and still give irrelevant answers because its boundary excludes accessibility, night operations, or maintenance logistics.

A disciplined concept claim has the form: under operating context `O`, concept `C` is expected to deliver outcome `Y` with uncertainty `U`, provided assumptions `A` hold. For Northstar, a shuttle concept might claim fewer than five minutes median passenger wait during class changes. A median alone hides the upper tail, where missed connections matter. A serious case includes a 90th- or 95th-percentile waiting-time target, demand surges, wheelchair boarding time, vehicle-outage conditions, and who would experience disproportionate delay. These are not extra numerical flourishes; they determine whether the promise fits the actual service problem.

### Construct an evidence ladder

Early concept evidence can be organized by distance from reality: analytical bounds, simulation studies, component demonstrations, representative integrated prototypes, and operational trials. Higher fidelity is not automatically stronger evidence if the test conditions fail to represent intended use. A beautifully detailed model calibrated from one mild-weather afternoon may be less decision-relevant than a simple queueing analysis stress-tested against peak load and failure scenarios. Evaluate evidence on **relevance, independence, repeatability, and challengeability** as well as fidelity.

Suppose two concept options offer the same mean wait, but one has a 95th-percentile wait of 12 minutes and the other 7. A board concerned about missed classes should not choose on the mean. Yet the tail estimates depend on demand sampling and how long simulations ran. Replications must sample uncertainty in demand, service time, and fleet availability rather than merely repeat the same deterministic trajectory. If the ranking changes when maintenance downtime doubles, condition the recommendation on maintenance evidence and retain both options until that uncertainty is resolved.

### Value of information guides the next experiment

Concept engineering should spend limited study resources on assumptions capable of changing the decision. The expected value of perfect information is `EVPI = E[max_a V(a,θ)] − max_a E[V(a,θ)]`, where `a` denotes an option and `θ` an uncertain state. This is a theoretical upper bound on what any information-gathering activity could be worth in the same value units. A real prototype or field observation offers only imperfect information and incurs its own cost and schedule exposure.

A concept with the highest nominal score may be too uncertain to commit to; another can be retained as a hedge while a short field study tests a critical assumption. Translate uncertainty into an *explicit decision trigger*: if measured peak boarding time exceeds a specified threshold, revise fleet size or concept choice. Also specify who accepts residual safety and equity risks. Conditional validation is not indecision; it is a rational allocation of irreversible commitment and further learning.

## Competing explanations and failure modes

A risk register without an exposure owner or retirement event is a list, not control. This is why reviewers must distinguish *unknown* from *failed*, and *not applicable* from *not tested*. A robust decision record preserves an alternative hypothesis, the evidence needed to discriminate it, and the owner of that next investigation. Ask what would happen if the controlling requirement changed, a data feed became stale, or an operational context moved outside the model boundary.

## How this connects to the next part of the lifecycle

In the **concept** stage, design freedom is preserved and choices are screened against needs and feasibility. In **design and integration**, provisional choices become owned interfaces and controlled budgets. In **test and evaluation**, those statements become hypotheses tested against configurations and populations. Keeping this chain visible matters more than using the same document format across teams.

## Step 03 integrated reference: NS-CD6.1 — concept validation and the value of an experiment

A Concept Validation Review should ask whether a proposed concept is **worth carrying into detailed design under declared conditions**, not whether the team produced attractive figures. The synthetic NS-CD6.1 model screens B and C as feasible and ranks C higher under the declared weights. That supports **selecting C for a controlled experiment**, but its degraded timing fails the reference and its accessibility and pickup-wait field evidence remains unexecuted.

### Make information worth its cost

Suppose a separate *illustrative loss analysis* represents two uncertain future demand states: ordinary (probability 0.7) and severe stress (0.3). Let downstream loss, in thousands of dollars, be:

| Concept | Ordinary loss | Severe-stress loss |
|---|---:|---:|
| B | 135 | 160 |
| C | 100 | 190 |

Expected loss is \(E[L_B]=0.7(135)+0.3(160)=142.5\) thousand dollars and \(E[L_C]=0.7(100)+0.3(190)=127\) thousand dollars, favoring C. With **perfect foreknowledge** of state, choose C in ordinary conditions and B under severe stress, giving \(0.7(100)+0.3(160)=118\) thousand dollars. The expected value of perfect information is therefore \(EVPI=127-118=9\) thousand dollars. A $12,000 data campaign cannot be justified on **this loss model solely** by perfect information, because even ideal information is worth only $9,000. A $5,000 test might be worthwhile, but its expected *sample* information value still depends on test accuracy and consequences; it cannot simply be set equal to EVPI.

This loss table is a separate illustrative risk example, not a derivation from the weighted value scores or an empirical forecast. If the probabilities or losses are contestable, vary them and disclose the assumptions before suggesting an actual experiment budget.

### Build a condition-based review argument

For C, report: 52.8 nominal effective trips/h versus a 46.2 screen; a model p90 wait of 9.5 minutes versus a 12-minute target; 98% *assumed* accessible coverage versus 95%; capital and annual cost within nominal caps; nominal command timing of 420 ms; **degraded command timing of 560 ms**; and missing pilot and fallback observations. The reviewer should require specific evidence to close CD6-R02, R06, R07 and stale CD6-R08 before any field-use authorization.
