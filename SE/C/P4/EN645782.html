EN.645.782 — Foundations of Digital and Mission Engineering

Credits: 3
Prerequisite: EN.645.662 Introduction to Systems Engineering
Recommended self-study preparation: completion of EN.645.631 Introduction to MBSE, EN.645.757 Foundations of Modeling and Simulation, EN.645.632 Applied Analytics for MBSE, and EN.645.780 Agile Systems Engineering

1. Course purpose

This course prepares a systems engineer to participate meaningfully in a digital-engineering and mission-engineering program without mistaking either discipline for tool acquisition, diagram production, or data accumulation. The learner will connect lifecycle decisions to authoritative data and models; define intended uses for models and simulations; apply MBSE method, language, and tool concepts; construct queryable system and mission representations; design a secure collaborative digital-engineering ecosystem; define credible digital-thread and digital-twin capabilities; and use mission engineering to evaluate end-to-end outcomes across systems, organizations, environments, and operational processes.

The course retains the U.S. Department of Defense acquisition, policy, and mission-engineering context of the source course while using an unclassified public-safety mobility case that can be studied without controlled information. Defense-specific terms are learned and applied, but the engineering practices are framed so they can transfer to aerospace, transportation, healthcare, energy, infrastructure, and other mission-oriented enterprises.

The final product is an integrated Digital Mission Engineering Strategy and Evidence Package that shows how models, data, simulations, architecture, digital threads, digital twins, and mission analysis support specific decisions. The package must demonstrate authority, provenance, traceability, credibility, security, configuration, and limitations—not merely attractive views.

4. Prerequisites and readiness diagnostic

Before Week 1, complete a 75–90 minute diagnostic. The learner should be able to:

  1. Describe the major technical and technical-management processes across a system lifecycle.
  2. Trace one stakeholder need through requirements, architecture, analysis, verification evidence, operational observation, and decision.
  3. Distinguish a model, simulation, dataset, view, report, and authoritative record.
  4. Explain intended use, verification, validation, uncertainty, and configuration status for one prior analytic model.
  5. Execute or inspect a query that finds orphan requirements, missing allocations, or incomplete verification traces.
  6. Use version control and reproduce one prior model-generated or analytic result.
  7. Explain the difference among a system, a system of systems, an enterprise, a capability, and a mission.
  8. Identify information that should not be freely shared because of security, privacy, proprietary, export-control, or data-rights constraints.

Diagnostic standard: at least 80%, with no failure on traceability, model credibility, configuration, or information-protection reasoning. Complete targeted remediation before continuing.

6. Essential questions

  • Which decision, user, lifecycle stage, and consequence justify each digital artifact?
  • What makes a model or dataset authoritative for one use but not another?
  • How do method, language, tool, metamodel, data, and governance interact?
  • Which digital continuity must be preserved, and which transformations or manual handoffs are acceptable?
  • How is a digital thread tested rather than merely drawn?
  • What distinguishes a digital twin from a simulation, dashboard, digital model, or digital shadow?
  • What synchronization, fidelity, uncertainty, and validation are required for a twin’s stated use?
  • How do mission questions change the model boundary and the systems that must be represented?
  • How can mission architectures remain reusable, transparent, and comparable across organizations?
  • Where do security, proprietary data, access, classification, and data rights constrain collaboration?
  • What evidence shows that digital engineering improved a decision rather than only changing the tools used?

7. Running case and controlled evidence

Northstar Resilient Mobility Mission

Northstar Mobility Systems has been asked to support a regional public-safety mission: maintain safe movement of students, staff, emergency personnel, and critical supplies during severe weather, grid disruption, communications degradation, and transportation-capacity loss.

The mission includes:

  • autonomous and manually operated campus shuttles;
  • municipal transit and emergency vehicles;
  • dispatch, routing, accessibility, and rider-information services;
  • charging infrastructure and the regional electric grid;
  • campus emergency management, local government, hospitals, law enforcement, and mutual-aid partners;
  • weather, traffic, cyber, infrastructure, and demand data;
  • policies, staffing, training, contracts, communications, and operational procedures.

The learner serves as Digital and Mission Engineering Lead. Required case evidence includes prior Phase 1–3 models and analyses, the EN.645.780 transformation operating model, synthetic operational and engineering data, stakeholder and organization records, controlled disruptions, and digital-environment constraints.

The mission is intentionally broader than the Northstar product. A correct mission analysis may recommend changes to technology, organizations, policies, information exchanges, staffing, training, logistics, or procedures rather than a new vehicle feature.

Controlled scenario changes

  • Week 2: the sponsor changes the primary decision and imposes a data-rights constraint.
  • Week 6: a model query reveals inconsistent requirement, interface, and test identifiers.
  • Week 8: a supplier interface update breaks an automated trace and generated verification report.
  • Week 9: field telemetry shows bias and intermittent synchronization loss.
  • Week 11: a communications degradation invalidates the nominal mission approach.
  • Week 12: the review board requests a live change to one mission assumption and asks for impact evidence.

The learner may substitute another unclassified mission only when equivalent system, SoS, organizational, data, and operational evidence exists.

SysML policy

SysML v2 received final OMG adoption in July 2025 and is the preferred standards-learning path. A SysML v1.7 path remains acceptable because many deployed tools and prior course artifacts use v1.x. The learner must identify the chosen version, semantics, interchange limits, and any translation or compatibility assumptions. (12–14)

Information-protection policy

Use only public, fictional, or properly sanitized information. Every repository must define access roles, data classifications or sensitivity labels, proprietary/data-rights restrictions, export limitations where applicable, and release rules. “Authoritative” never means “universally accessible.”

14. Critical mastery criteria

The course cannot be passed through point accumulation while any of these remain:

  • digital engineering is defined as buying tools, converting documents to PDFs, or placing files in one repository;
  • an authoritative source is declared without owner, scope, status, pedigree, access, and acceptable-use rules;
  • a digital thread is only a diagram and cannot answer cross-domain queries or detect a controlled break;
  • a digital twin lacks a stated physical/operational referent, synchronization mechanism, intended use, validation, or uncertainty bounds;
  • model and simulation results are used outside their validated context without disclosure;
  • security, privacy, proprietary information, intellectual property, data rights, or access constraints are ignored;
  • mission success is inferred from one system’s performance without end-to-end mission analysis;
  • the mission architecture omits organizations, information exchanges, environment, assumptions, or non-materiel contributors;
  • critical identifiers, baselines, or interfaces are inconsistent and unresolved;
  • review findings are hidden or closed without evidence.

Completion standard: at least 80% overall; at least 70% in each major category; Proficient or better on every critical capstone dimension; all critical criteria satisfied; successful oral defense and live change.

Week 1 — Lifecycle, acquisition, and digital/mission framing

Competency alignment

C1 (D), C4 (A), C10 (D), C11 (D), C12 (A)

Professional context and essential question

A digital-engineering initiative fails early when lifecycle decisions, mission questions, and accountable users are not defined. The essential question is: Which decisions require connected digital evidence, and what consequence follows if that evidence is late, inconsistent, inaccessible, or wrong?

Weekly learning outcomes

  • Reconstruct the principal technical and technical-management activities across the lifecycle and relate them to acquisition decisions.
  • Distinguish system, system of systems, enterprise, capability, mission, mission thread, and operational scenario.
  • Identify decision authorities, evidence users, timing, consequence, and information constraints.
  • Frame the Northstar mission problem without assuming the product is the only intervention.
  • Create a receiving baseline that distinguishes inherited evidence, assumptions, gaps, and unsupported claims.

Prerequisite retrieval and readiness check

  • Recreate a need-to-decision evidence chain from one Phase 2 or Phase 3 artifact.
  • Name four technical-management controls that remain necessary in a digital environment.
  • Distinguish a lifecycle model from an acquisition pathway and from a technical process.
  • Identify one prior model that was authoritative for analysis but not for configuration or operations.

A learner unable to complete at least three of the four items should revisit the cited prior-course artifacts before beginning independent synthesis.

Required study

  • JHU Fall 2026 syllabus: Description, Course Topics, Goals, and CLOs. Identify the source course’s required distinctions and demonstrations. (2)
  • DoD Systems Engineering Guidebook: Introduction and lifecycle/technical-process sections. Record which decisions need technical evidence. (3)
  • Engineering of Defense Systems Guidebook: overview and digital-engineering references. Identify connections among engineering, technical reviews, and acquisition. (4)
  • DoDI 5000.97: Purpose, applicability, and definitions. Mark what is policy, what is implementation choice, and what is outside the case. (5)

For every source, record one claim, one implication for Northstar, one limitation, and one unresolved question.

Instructor-style lesson notes

  • Digital engineering is an integrated way of performing engineering; it does not replace systems engineering or acquisition accountability.
  • Lifecycle stages create different evidence users and authority. A concept model, released design, test record, and operational state estimate can all describe the same system while having different authority.
  • Mission engineering begins with mission outcomes and end-to-end dependencies. It may change products, organizations, processes, doctrine, training, logistics, policy, or information exchanges.
  • Decision framing should state decision owner, question, timing, alternatives, evidence, consequence, and acceptable uncertainty.
  • The receiving review prevents inherited models and dashboards from becoming trusted by default.

Worked example

The sponsor asks Northstar to “build a digital twin for emergency mobility.” The example reframes this into four decisions: whether to pre-position vehicles, whether to curtail charging, how to route accessibility-dependent riders, and whether to request mutual aid. Each decision has different update rates, evidence, consequence, and uncertainty. The example shows that one monolithic twin is neither necessary nor automatically credible.

Reproduce the example using controlled course data, change one assumption, and explain how the decision or evidence changes.

Guided practice

  • Classify 20 case artifacts by lifecycle stage, owner, decision use, configuration status, and sensitivity.
  • Create a decision-to-evidence map for the four emergency-mobility decisions.
  • Draw separate product, enterprise, SoS, and mission boundaries and identify what crosses each.
  • Write a mission-neutral receiving problem statement and challenge it for hidden solution bias.

Checkpoint: compare the result with the reference rationale, record discrepancies, correct the source artifact, and rerun affected queries or analyses.

Independent exercises

  • Foundation: Build a 30-term glossary with source-grounded definitions and examples.
  • Application: Create a lifecycle decision register with at least 15 decisions, owners, evidence, timing, consequence, and uncertainty tolerance.
  • Analysis: Audit inherited Phase 1–3 artifacts and identify at least 12 authority, traceability, credibility, or configuration gaps.
  • Synthesis: Write a 1,200-word receiving memo that defines the mission, digital-engineering problem, evidence priorities, and initial review gates.
  • Stretch: Compare how the evidence strategy changes under major-capability, software, and urgent-capability acquisition patterns.

Weekly deliverable

Digital and Mission Engineering Receiving Package containing the lifecycle map, decision register, boundary views, evidence inventory, authority/gap assessment, mission-neutral problem statement, and receiving memo. Audience: sponsor, chief engineer, mission owner, and data/model governance lead. Length: 10–14 pages plus controlled tables and source files.

Submit native or textual source, data and transformations, generated views, query/analysis source, a change log, and a one-page self-review. Screenshots alone are not acceptable evidence.

Knowledge check and answer guidance

  1. Why can two artifacts both be accurate but authoritative for different uses?
    Answer guidance: Authority is scoped by decision, lifecycle state, configuration, owner, pedigree, and acceptable use.
  2. What distinguishes a mission from a system?
    Answer guidance: A mission is an intended outcome achieved by interacting systems, people, organizations, processes, and environment; a system is one engineered contributor.
  3. What is the purpose of a receiving review?
    Answer guidance: To establish what is inherited, controlled, credible, authoritative, missing, or disputed before use.
  4. Why does digital engineering not remove technical management?
    Answer guidance: Connected models and data increase the need for planning, authority, configuration, risk, decisions, access, and review.
  5. What makes a decision-to-evidence map useful?
    Answer guidance: It exposes whether the right evidence exists, is timely, trusted, accessible, and connected to the accountable decision.
