EN.645.742 — Management of Complex Systems

Credits: 3

1. Course purpose

This course develops the judgment needed to manage complex problem situations in which technical systems, organizations, people, institutions, incentives, histories, and environments interact across multiple scales. It is not a course in making a difficult project merely more orderly. It addresses situations where boundaries are contested, purposes conflict, behavior is nonlinear or emergent, future states are only partly predictable, authority is distributed, knowledge is incomplete, and interventions can change the very system being studied.

The learner will combine systems theory, systems thinking, systems engineering, management, complexity science, and critical systems thinking. The emphasis is on selecting, combining, critiquing, and adapting systems approaches rather than treating any method as universally valid. The culminating capability is to formulate and defend a responsible multimethodological intervention for a consequential complex problem while explicitly bounding authority, evidence, uncertainty, ethics, and unintended consequences.

2. Source scope and self-study adaptation

The current JHU syllabus organizes the course around:

  • introduction to management of complex systems;
  • systems theory and systems thinking;
  • complexity, chaos, and complex systems;
  • examples of complex-system problems;
  • Type A systems approaches for technical complexity;
  • Type B systems approaches for process complexity;
  • Type C systems approaches for structural complexity;
  • Type D systems approaches for organizational complexity;
  • Type E systems approaches for people complexity;
  • Type F systems approaches for coercive complexity;
  • critical systems theory and critical systems thinking;
  • critical systems practice; and
  • the future of complex-systems management. (1, 2)

The required source text is Michael C. Jackson's Critical Systems Thinking and the Management of Complexity. The source course is reading intensive and assesses discussion, module assignments, a complex-systems literature review, and a final project. This self-study version preserves that emphasis, but adapts it as follows:

  • weekly discussion becomes a dialectical critique journal or optional peer seminar;
  • module homework becomes method-application and critique assignments;
  • the literature review becomes a structured 5–7 page review due at the end of Week 6;
  • the final paper becomes a controlled intervention portfolio plus a 12–15 page synthesis paper and oral defense;
  • every selected approach must be accompanied by an applicability claim, limitation analysis, and evidence plan;
  • methods are used to support learning and responsible action, not to claim prediction or control that the situation does not permit.

The source syllabus states that generative AI may not produce course assignments. For this self-study adaptation, use AI only as a disclosed reviewer, critic, or formatting assistant. Preserve an AI-use log, verify every claim and citation, and ensure that the analysis, judgments, diagrams, models, and writing remain your own.

3. Relationship to adjacent courses

Inputs from earlier phases

The learner should bring:

  • controlled requirements, architectures, risk, test, and lifecycle evidence from Phases 0–2;
  • system dynamics, uncertainty, decision analysis, modeling, simulation, and credibility skills from Phase 3;
  • agile learning, digital-thread, mission-engineering, and process-improvement skills from Phase 4;
  • the Phase 5 SoS architecture and enterprise-transformation baselines from EN.645.771 and EN.645.753.

Distinct contribution of this course

EN.645.771 asked how to engineer capability across independently managed constituent systems. EN.645.753 asked how to align enterprise value, governance, investment, processes, and transformation. EN.645.742 asks a different question:

How should responsible leaders understand and intervene when the problem situation is multidimensional, adaptive, politically contested, path-dependent, only partly observable, and not amenable to a single methodology?

The course may challenge or reverse conclusions from earlier courses. That is expected. The aim is not to preserve the previous answer; it is to expose where prior framing, authority, evidence, or methods were too narrow.

Self-study prerequisite: completion of Phases 0–4 and EN.645.771 and EN.645.753, or equivalent evidence of competence.

  2. construct and critique a causal-loop diagram and a behavior-over-time graph;
  3. explain uncertainty, sensitivity, risk propagation, resilience, and decision reversal;
  4. identify actor authority, incentives, conflicts, exclusions, and evidence ownership;
  5. compare a hard systems method, system dynamics, a soft/problem-structuring method, and a critical/boundary method;
  6. trace an intervention from purpose and stakeholder values through evidence, monitoring, and adaptation;
  7. distinguish model validation from social legitimacy and ethical acceptability;
  8. write a properly cited critical review that separates source claims, interpretation, and judgment.

5. Course learning outcomes

By the end of the course, the learner will be able to:

  1. explain core systems concepts and complexity mechanisms and apply them to real problem situations;
  2. distinguish complication, dynamic complexity, organizational complexity, social pluralism, coercion, and complex adaptation;
  3. formulate a complex problem situation without prematurely fixing boundaries, purposes, stakeholders, or solutions;
  4. compare and critique Type A–F systems approaches in terms of assumptions, strengths, blind spots, ethics, and use conditions;
  5. apply selected hard, process, structural, organizational, participative, and critical methods to a shared case;
  6. identify feedback, delay, nonlinearity, path dependence, emergence, adaptation, scale mismatch, and observability limits;
  7. analyze power, legitimacy, boundary judgments, affected-but-unrepresented groups, and coercive conditions;
  8. construct a justified multimethodological intervention strategy rather than selecting methods by familiarity or fashion;
  9. design safe-to-learn interventions with monitoring, guardrails, stop conditions, and adaptation or reversal criteria;
  10. assess dynamic risk, resilience, cascading effects, and unintended consequences under irreducible uncertainty;
  11. evaluate an intervention using technical, organizational, human, ethical, and systemic evidence;
  12. produce and defend graduate-level scholarly work that accurately states sources, assumptions, uncertainty, authority, and limitations.

Primary program competencies: C1, C4, C8, C9, C10, C11, and C12, with advanced integration of earlier competencies.

6. Essential questions

  • What makes a problem situation complex rather than merely difficult or complicated?
  • Which assumptions about purpose, knowledge, control, prediction, and rationality are embedded in a method?
  • When does a technically rigorous method become socially or ethically inadequate?
  • How can different systems approaches be combined without producing an incoherent collection of tools?
  • Who defines improvement, who controls resources, who supplies expertise, who bears harm, and who is excluded?
  • What can be learned or influenced, and what remains irreducibly uncertain?
  • How should leaders act when observation and intervention alter the system?
  • What evidence is sufficient to continue, adapt, stop, or reverse an intervention?

7. Running case and controlled evidence

Regional Mobility and Emergency Access Complex Problem Situation

Continue the Phase 5 regional ecosystem, but introduce a compound, contested situation:

  • a prolonged heat emergency and rolling electrical outages;
  • intermittent cellular and cloud-service failures;
  • a cyber incident affecting traveler information and payment systems;
  • labor shortages and work-rule disputes;
  • conflicting municipal, hospital, campus, and private-vendor priorities;
  • unequal access for disabled, low-income, rural, and non-English-speaking populations;
  • online misinformation and declining public trust;
  • delayed state and federal funding;
  • legacy fleets, fragmented data rights, and incompatible modernization schedules;
  • pressure for a rapid autonomous-mobility expansion despite incomplete safety and equity evidence.

No actor has complete information or authority. Actors learn and change behavior. Measures can be gamed. Local optimization can worsen regional outcomes. The learner must not assume that the problem is a single objective, that stakeholder consensus exists, or that the system can be centrally designed.

8. Tool and integrity policy

The course is method-centered, not tool-centered. Suitable tools include:

  • Markdown, a citation manager, and Git for scholarship and configuration;
  • diagrams.net, Kumu, or Graphviz for influence, network, and boundary views;
  • Vensim PLE or an equivalent tool for bounded system-dynamics experiments;
  • Python/Jupyter with pandas, NetworkX, and SALib for reproducible analysis;
  • qualitative coding in a spreadsheet or open-source QDA tool;
  • decision logs, assumption registers, and structured review checklists.

Tool output is not evidence by itself. Preserve source data, transformations, model logic, version information, test results, and interpretation. Do not use an opaque complexity score, AI-generated stakeholder synthesis, or simulation result as a substitute for judgment.

13. Major assignment specifications

A. Six-approach application and critique portfolio

For Types A–F, produce one bounded application and one critique. For each, state:

  • the problem dimension addressed;
  • philosophical and methodological assumptions;
  • required conditions and data;
  • method steps and products;
  • useful insight produced;
  • blind spots and likely failure modes;
  • affected parties and ethical concerns;
  • how the method complements or conflicts with other approaches.

B. Complex-systems literature review

Write a 5–7 page scholarly review on one approved theme, such as emergence and management, adaptive governance, dynamic risk, critical systems thinking, resilience, coercive complexity, multimethodology, complex-program failure, or responsible intervention. Use at least eight credible sources, including peer-reviewed scholarship and at least two sources that disagree. The paper must synthesize themes, expose assumptions, compare evidence, identify limitations, and state implications for the running case.

C. Multimethodological intervention strategy

Use Critical Systems Practice to produce an EPIC-aligned strategy. Select and sequence methods based on the problem situation—not convenience. Explain what each method contributes, how outputs pass between methods, where paradigms or assumptions conflict, how affected parties participate, and how the strategy will adapt when new information emerges.

D. Final complex-systems management project

Produce a 12–15 page synthesis paper and controlled evidence package addressing a consequential issue in the regional case. Include framing, theory, Type A–F critique, multimethodology, intervention design, dynamic risk, resilience, ethics, monitoring, evidence, limitations, and reflection. The final recommendation must describe both what to do and what not to claim.

15. Critical criteria and mastery gates

A learner cannot pass with any unresolved item below:

  • defines complexity only by size, number of parts, or technical difficulty;
  • claims prediction, optimization, consensus, or control beyond the evidence and authority available;
  • applies a method without stating its assumptions, use conditions, and limitations;
  • excludes materially affected groups or treats coercion and unequal power as ordinary stakeholder disagreement;
  • makes causal claims from a diagram, anecdote, or unvalidated model without appropriate evidence;
  • proposes intervention without monitoring, guardrails, adaptation, and stop/reversal criteria;
  • combines methods incoherently or hides philosophical conflicts behind a list of tools;
  • fabricates citations, data, stakeholder views, or results;
  • cannot explain how the final recommendation changed through critique and learning.

Course completion requires at least 80% overall, at least Proficient performance in complexity framing, methodological understanding, pluralism/power, and intervention/learning, successful completion of the literature review, and a defensible final oral examination.

During the final review, one condition will change—for example:

  • a major private mobility platform withdraws data access;
  • a new accessibility ruling changes service obligations;
  • a labor action removes 20% of available operators;
  • a severe heat event coincides with a hospital surge and charging outage;
  • misinformation causes travelers to avoid designated emergency hubs;
  • a cyber compromise invalidates trusted identity or payment services;
  • a low-income municipality rejects the preferred funding arrangement.

The learner must identify affected boundaries, assumptions, methods, actors, risks, models, interventions, evidence, and decisions; revise the recommendation; and state whether the intervention should continue, adapt, pause, or stop.

17. Oral defense prompts

  1. Why is this a complex problem situation rather than a difficult engineering problem?
  2. Which boundaries are most consequential, and who has standing to challenge them?
  3. What did the Type A method reveal, and what would it have hidden if used alone?
  4. Where did process, structural, organizational, people, and coercive complexity require different approaches?
  5. Which method assumptions conflict, and how did you manage those conflicts?
  6. What evidence supports your causal claims, and what remains a hypothesis?
  7. Who benefits, who bears cost or risk, and who lacks effective voice?
  8. What uncertainty is reducible, and what must be managed as irreducible?
  9. Why is the proposed intervention safe enough to try?
  10. Which signals would cause adaptation, pause, reversal, or escalation?
  11. How could actors strategically respond and change the intervention's effects?
  12. What did Critical Systems Practice add beyond ordinary method selection?
  13. Which prior Phase 5 recommendation did you revise or reject, and why?
  14. What is the strongest critique of your final recommendation?
  15. What should the decision authority explicitly avoid claiming?

Week 1 — Complex problem situations, systems theory, and receiving review

Book companion · Week 1 : Read Book 20, Chapter 01 → Practice the hypothetical lab. Keep the original assigned readings, assessed deliverable and grading rubric. Earlier short primer.

Competency alignment

C1 systems thinking; C9 decision reasoning; C11 complexity; C12 professional judgment.

Professional context and essential question

Leaders often label a program “complex” when it is merely large. The first responsibility is to diagnose what kind of complexity is present and to avoid converting a contested problem situation into a falsely precise problem statement.

Essential question: What evidence shows that the regional situation is complex, and which early framing choices could create blind spots?

Weekly learning outcomes

By the end of the week, you should be able to:

  • distinguish simple, complicated, dynamically complex, organizationally complex, and complex-adaptive conditions
  • define a problem situation without presuming a single purpose, owner, boundary, or solution
  • identify open-system relationships, hierarchy, emergence, feedback, and multiple perspectives
  • construct initial behavior-over-time graphs and a complexity-characteristics profile
  • state provisional boundaries, exclusions, uncertainties, and non-claims

Prerequisite retrieval and readiness check

Before beginning the new material:

  • Retrieve the Phase 5 SoS and enterprise boundaries and list where they disagree.
  • Draw one feedback loop from EN.645.753 from memory and explain its evidence.
  • Identify one stakeholder affected by the regional ecosystem but not represented in formal governance.
  • Write a 150-word distinction between “complicated” and “complex.”

Readiness threshold: 80%. Correct misunderstandings before continuing; do not carry an unexamined boundary, power, causal, or method assumption into the week's baseline.

Required study

  • JHU syllabus and course description (1, 2)
    Purpose: Anchor the course in its actual emphasis on systems theory, nonlinear behavior, irreducible uncertainty, adaptive strategy, and scholarly critique.
    Guiding questions: Which source-course expectations require a different mindset from traditional project control?
  • Jackson, introductory and systems-thinking sections (3)
    Purpose: Understand systems traditions, problem situations, and the need to match approaches to complexity dimensions.
    Guiding questions: What assumptions about purpose, knowledge, and control are made by conventional management?
  • SEBoK Complexity and Systems Thinking (5, 7)
    Purpose: Establish a systems-engineering vocabulary for emergence, relationships, and prediction limits.
    Guiding questions: Which characteristics are observable in the running case, and which are only hypotheses?

Record concise notes answering every guiding question. For the required book, read the named section rather than relying on a summary. Identify at least one claim you accept, one you question, and one implication for the running case.

Instructor-style lesson notes

  • Complexity is not a compliment, excuse, or score; it is a claim about relationships, behavior, knowledge, and intervention.
  • A system boundary is a judgment made for a purpose, not a discovered natural line.
  • Open systems exchange information, energy, resources, people, legitimacy, and risk with an environment.
  • Emergence describes whole-system properties arising through interaction; it does not mean “mysterious.”
  • Problem formulation is already an intervention because it privileges some actors, outcomes, and evidence.
  • The receiving review should preserve earlier artifacts but suspend commitment to their preferred solutions.

Worked example

A regional authority frames the issue as “optimize autonomous shuttle deployment.” Reframing it as “maintain equitable emergency access during compound disruption” changes the relevant actors, boundaries, options, measures, and authority. The original optimization problem remains useful only as a bounded subproblem.

Reconstruct the example in a diagram, table, memo, or model. Then state where the example is deliberately simplified and why the same conclusion might not transfer to the regional case.

Guided practice

  1. Inventory prior Phase 5 claims and classify each as fact, assumption, interpretation, value judgment, or recommendation.
  2. Create six behavior-over-time graphs: access reliability, emergency travel time, workforce load, public trust, service inequity, and recovery time.
  3. Build a complexity profile covering diversity, connectivity, feedback, delay, adaptation, scale, authority, uncertainty, and conflict.
  4. Draft three alternative system boundaries and compare what each includes and excludes.
  5. Conduct a receiving review and record findings that must be resolved before method selection.

Pause after each step. Compare the result with the decision purpose and record disagreement, ambiguity, and negative evidence instead of forcing convergence.

Independent exercises

  • Foundation: classify 12 sample situations as simple, complicated, dynamically complex, pluralist, or coercive and justify each classification.
  • Application: write the regional problem-situation charter and non-claims.
  • Analysis: show how two plausible boundaries produce different measures and intervention options.
  • Synthesis: create a one-page complexity framing for an executive who wants a single answer.
  • Stretch: obtain a peer or external reviewer’s framing and document irreducible disagreements.

Dialectical critique journal / optional seminar

Write a 300–500 word response that:

  1. reconstructs one assigned author's central claim fairly;
  2. identifies its assumptions and strongest evidence;
  3. develops a substantive critique or counterexample;
  4. applies the claim to the regional case; and
  5. states what evidence or argument would change your current judgment.

With a cohort, exchange critiques with at least two peers and respond to the strongest challenge. Studying solo, write a steelman and a red-team response separated by 24 hours.

Weekly deliverable

Complexity Framing and Receiving Package

Required contents:

  • problem-situation charter and decision purpose
  • three candidate boundaries and boundary rationale
  • behavior-over-time graphs with data/provenance notes
  • complexity-characteristics profile
  • stakeholder and affected-party preliminary registry
  • assumption, uncertainty, exclusion, and non-claim log
  • receiving-review findings and dispositions

Include a one-page review note stating the decision supported, approach used, assumptions, boundary judgments, evidence, affected groups, limitations, and changes made to prior baselines.

Analytic rubric

Criterion Weight Proficient standard
Complexity distinction 20% Uses mechanisms rather than size or difficulty
Boundary/purpose 20% Alternatives and exclusions are explicit
Dynamic evidence 20% Reference modes are grounded and uncertainty-aware
Perspective/affected parties 20% Formal and informal actors are visible

Critical failures

  • Treats the regional issue as a predefined autonomous-shuttle optimization problem.
  • Presents a single boundary as objectively correct.
  • Uses “emergent” or “irreducible” without evidence or operational meaning.

Any critical failure requires revision regardless of the numerical score.

Knowledge check and answer guidance

  1. Why is a boundary a judgment? — Because relevance depends on purpose, values, authority, evidence, and who is affected.
  2. What distinguishes complication from complexity? — Complication can often be decomposed and predicted; complexity includes interaction, adaptation, feedback, uncertainty, or contested meaning that defeats straightforward decomposition.
  3. Why use behavior-over-time graphs? — They make the dynamic problem explicit before choosing causes or solutions.
  4. What is a non-claim? — A deliberate statement of what the analysis does not establish, predict, authorize, or optimize.
  5. Why reopen earlier Phase 5 conclusions? — Earlier methods and boundaries may have hidden adaptation, conflict, coercion, or uncertainty.

Create five additional retrieval questions and answer them without notes 48–72 hours later.

Revision and mastery gate

Pass the receiving review only when the situation is framed as revisable, multiple boundaries are preserved, and no preferred solution has been smuggled into the problem definition.

Minimum weekly score: 80%, with no critical failure and at least Proficient performance on the first two rubric criteria.

Week 2 — Complexity science, chaos, adaptation, scale, and irreducible uncertainty

Book companion · Week 2 : Read Book 20, Chapter 02 → Practice the hypothetical lab. Keep the original assigned readings, assessed deliverable and grading rubric. Earlier short primer.

Competency alignment

C1 systems thinking; C8 uncertainty; C11 complex adaptive systems; C12 evidence judgment.

Professional context and essential question

Managers need enough complexity science to recognize nonlinear dynamics, adaptation, network effects, and scale-dependent behavior—but not so much borrowed terminology that metaphor replaces analysis.

Essential question: Which complexity mechanisms plausibly generate the observed behavior, and which future conditions cannot be reduced to ordinary parameter uncertainty?

Weekly learning outcomes

By the end of the week, you should be able to:

  • explain sensitivity to initial conditions, attractors, thresholds, phase transitions, networks, self-organization, adaptation, and path dependence
  • distinguish deterministic chaos, stochastic variation, deep uncertainty, ambiguity, and ignorance
  • analyze how patterns and interventions differ across temporal, spatial, organizational, and social scales
  • construct testable dynamic hypotheses without claiming universal complexity laws
  • develop an uncertainty and observability map linked to decisions

Prerequisite retrieval and readiness check

Before beginning the new material:

  • Reproduce the Week 1 reference modes without notes.
  • Identify one reinforcing and one balancing loop from prior work.
  • Explain aleatory, epistemic, and deep uncertainty in one sentence each.
  • List three ways an intervention could change actor behavior.

Readiness threshold: 80%. Correct misunderstandings before continuing; do not carry an unexamined boundary, power, causal, or method assumption into the week's baseline.

Required study

  • Jackson complexity, chaos, and complex-systems sections (3)
    Purpose: Connect complexity science to management without confusing metaphor with method.
    Guiding questions: Which concepts have actionable implications and which require caution?
  • Santa Fe Institute introductory complexity material (12)
    Purpose: Review adaptation, emergence, networks, nonlinear dynamics, and multiscale reasoning.

Week 11 — Critical Systems Practice: Intervene; dynamic risk, resilience, and safe-to-learn action

Book companion · Week 11 : Read Book 20, Chapter 11 → Practice the hypothetical lab. Keep the original assigned readings, assessed deliverable and grading rubric. Earlier short primer.

Competency alignment

C6 V&V; C8 risk/uncertainty; C10 implementation; C11 resilience; C12 responsible action.

Professional context and essential question

Complex interventions change relationships, incentives, expectations, and information. The objective is not to “implement the solution” but to act responsibly, observe, learn, and adapt while protecting people and essential capability.

Essential question: What intervention can be attempted safely enough to generate decision-relevant learning under dynamic risk and irreducible uncertainty?

Weekly learning outcomes

By the end of the week, you should be able to:

  • translate the intervention strategy into bounded, testable action
  • construct dynamic risk and cascading-effect models
  • design resilience through prevention, absorption, adaptation, recovery, and learning
  • define hypotheses, measures, leading indicators, guardrails, stop conditions, and reversibility
  • plan flexible intervention and evidence collection without freezing the method strategy

Prerequisite retrieval and readiness check

Before beginning the new material:

  • Identify the highest-risk irreversible action in the strategy.
  • List three possible strategic actor responses.
  • Retrieve the Week 2 uncertainty map and Week 8 safeguards.
  • State the minimum evidence needed to continue after a pilot.

Readiness threshold: 80%. Correct misunderstandings before continuing; do not carry an unexamined boundary, power, causal, or method assumption into the week's baseline.

Required study

  • Jackson CSP Intervene material in the practitioner guide (4, 15)
    Purpose: Understand flexible action and return to earlier EPIC stages when learning requires it.
    Guiding questions: What does disciplined flexibility look like?
  • ISO 31000 and DoD RIO guide (21, 22)
    Purpose: Integrate continuous risk, issue, and opportunity management.
    Guiding questions: How do risk ownership, indicators, and review change in a dynamic system?
  • NIST cyber and community resilience resources (23, 24)
    Purpose: Design resilience across disruption, degradation, recovery, and adaptation.
    Guiding questions: Which essential functions and vulnerable populations require protected outcomes?

Record concise notes answering every guiding question. For the required book, read the named section rather than relying on a summary. Identify at least one claim you accept, one you question, and one implication for the running case.

Instructor-style lesson notes

  • Safe-to-learn does not mean low stakes; it means bounded exposure, protected essentials, monitoring, and the ability to stop or adapt.
  • Dynamic risks change as actors learn, dependencies shift, and interventions redistribute load.
  • Resilience includes anticipation, absorption, adaptation, recovery, and institutional learning.
  • Leading indicators should be tied to mechanisms, not selected because they are easy to count.
  • Guardrails protect critical outcomes and vulnerable groups; stop conditions convert ethics into operational decisions.
  • Flexible intervention requires configuration control so changes and outcomes remain interpretable.

Worked example

Pilot a protected emergency-access protocol in two districts rather than region-wide. The pilot includes minimum accessibility service, workforce load limits, offline dispatch fallback, public communication, independent monitoring, and stop criteria for inequitable denial rates, safety incidents, or unmanageable staff fatigue.

Reconstruct the example in a diagram, table, memo, or model. Then state where the example is deliberately simplified and why the same conclusion might not transfer to the regional case.

Guided practice

  1. Select one bounded intervention and state hypotheses about mechanisms and actor responses.
  2. Map cascading risk, common-cause conditions, burden transfer, and vulnerable populations.
  3. Define resilience functions and minimum acceptable service.
  4. Specify leading indicators, outcome measures, guardrails, stop/reversal criteria, and review cadence.
  5. Run tabletop stress tests for at least four compound disruptions and revise the pilot.

Pause after each step. Compare the result with the decision purpose and record disagreement, ambiguity, and negative evidence instead of forcing convergence.

Independent exercises

  • Foundation: distinguish mitigation, contingency, resilience, robustness, adaptability, and recovery.
  • Application: build the regional safe-to-learn pilot package.
  • Analysis: identify how the intervention could create new incentives, gaming, or inequity.
  • Synthesis: recommend continue/adapt/pause/stop criteria before the pilot begins.
  • Stretch: implement a simple stress-test or Monte Carlo analysis without assigning false precision to deep uncertainty.

Dialectical critique journal / optional seminar

Write a 300–500 word response that:

  1. reconstructs one assigned author's central claim fairly;
  2. identifies its assumptions and strongest evidence;
  3. develops a substantive critique or counterexample;
  4. applies the claim to the regional case; and
  5. states what evidence or argument would change your current judgment.

With a cohort, exchange critiques with at least two peers and respond to the strongest challenge. Studying solo, write a steelman and a red-team response separated by 24 hours.

Intervention, Dynamic Risk, and Resilience Package

Required contents:

  • intervention hypotheses and causal mechanisms
  • scope, authority, participants, and protected essential outcomes
  • dynamic risk and cascading-effect map
  • resilience and degraded-operation strategy
  • measures, leading indicators, guardrails, and stop conditions
  • stress tests and revised pilot design
  • learning, governance, configuration, and communication plan

Include a one-page review note stating the decision supported, approach used, assumptions, boundary judgments, evidence, affected groups, limitations, and changes made to prior baselines.

Analytic rubric

Criterion Weight Proficient standard

Resilience 20% Essential functions and recovery are credible
Guardrails/ethics 20% Vulnerable groups and stop conditions are protected

Critical failures

  • Proposes a large irreversible rollout as the first learning step.
  • Uses generic risk scores without propagation, indicators, or ownership.
  • Defines success while omitting safety, accessibility, workforce, or equity guardrails.

Any critical failure requires revision regardless of the numerical score.

Knowledge check and answer guidance

  1. What makes an intervention safe-to-learn? — Bounded exposure, protected essentials, monitoring, guardrails, and credible stop or adaptation options.
  2. How is dynamic risk different? — Risk changes through feedback, actor adaptation, dependencies, and the intervention itself.
  3. What is a guardrail? — A protected condition that constrains action even if primary performance improves.
  4. Why define stop criteria in advance? — It reduces motivated reasoning and makes ethical limits operational.
  5. What is disciplined flexibility? — Adapting methods and action through explicit evidence, authority, configuration, and review—not improvising without traceability.

Week 12 — Critical Systems Practice: Check; final defense and the way ahead

Book companion · Week 12 : Read Book 20, Chapter 12 → Practice the hypothetical lab. Keep the original assigned readings, assessed deliverable and grading rubric. Earlier short primer.

Competency alignment

All course competencies; emphasis on C11 integration and C12 reflective professional practice.

Professional context and essential question

Check evaluates both the intervention and the systems approaches used. The final product must show what improved, for whom, at what cost, under what uncertainty, and what the practitioner learned about methods, power, evidence, and responsibility.

Essential question: What can responsibly be concluded, what should change next, and what does the intervention reveal about managing complex systems?

Weekly learning outcomes

By the end of the week, you should be able to:

  • evaluate intervention outcomes, mechanisms, participation, harms, and learning
  • assess the usefulness and limitations of the selected methods and multimethodology
  • reproduce the evidence chain and disposition review findings
  • respond to a live system change by revisiting boundaries, methods, risks, and decisions
  • defend a bounded recommendation and professional retrospective

Prerequisite retrieval and readiness check

Before beginning the new material:

  • Reproduce the final decision-critical outputs from a clean repository checkout.
  • List the five weakest claims in the draft final paper.
  • Identify one group that could reasonably reject the definition of improvement.
  • State what evidence would overturn the recommendation.

Readiness threshold: 80%. Correct misunderstandings before continuing; do not carry an unexamined boundary, power, causal, or method assumption into the week's baseline.

Required study

  • Jackson CSP Check paper and practitioner guide (20, 4)
    Purpose: Evaluate progress, improvement, method performance, reflection, and next action.
    Guiding questions: How should the practitioner judge both outcomes and the intervention process?
  • JHU source outcomes and assessment expectations (1)
    Purpose: Confirm that the release demonstrates explanation, comparison, critique, formulation, and scholarly analysis.
    Guiding questions: Which learning outcome remains least supported?
  • INCOSE complex-systems resources (8, 9)
    Purpose: Connect the final reflection to ongoing systems-engineering practice.
    Guiding questions: What heuristics remain useful and what cautions should accompany them?

Record concise notes answering every guiding question. For the required book, read the named section rather than relying on a summary. Identify at least one claim you accept, one you question, and one implication for the running case.

Instructor-style lesson notes

  • Check asks whether improvement occurred, how it was judged, and who might disagree.
  • Evaluation should examine mechanisms and unintended consequences, not only target metrics.
  • Method evaluation asks which approaches helped, misled, conflicted, or required adaptation.
  • A live change tests whether the repository connects boundaries, assumptions, models, actors, interventions, and decisions.
  • A mature conclusion narrows unsupported claims rather than defending the original plan.
  • The way ahead is an adaptive learning agenda, not a declaration that complexity has been solved.

Worked example

During final review, a private platform withdraws data access. The technical model loses demand visibility, the process pilot loses automated triage, the VSM intelligence function degrades, public trust risks increase, and participation concerns change. The learner narrows the pilot, activates offline processes, revises evidence thresholds, and pauses one automated feature.

Reconstruct the example in a diagram, table, memo, or model. Then state where the example is deliberately simplified and why the same conclusion might not transfer to the regional case.

Guided practice

  1. Run a clean reproduction and complete a source/citation audit.
  2. Evaluate outcomes, mechanisms, distributional effects, participation, and unintended consequences.
  3. Complete a method-performance retrospective for Types A–F and EPIC.
  4. Execute a randomly selected live change and update connected artifacts and the decision.
  5. Conduct the final oral defense and write the next-learning agenda.

Pause after each step. Compare the result with the decision purpose and record disagreement, ambiguity, and negative evidence instead of forcing convergence.

Independent exercises

  • Foundation: complete the final traceability, citation, and configuration audit.
  • Application: assemble the final complex-systems management release.
  • Analysis: narrow or retract the five weakest claims.
  • Synthesis: execute the live change and defend continue/adapt/pause/stop.
  • Stretch: invite reviewers representing technical, community, labor, and critical perspectives and disposition their findings.

  2. identifies its assumptions and strongest evidence;
  3. develops a substantive critique or counterexample;
  4. applies the claim to the regional case; and
  5. states what evidence or argument would change your current judgment.

Knowledge check and answer guidance

  3. What should a live change update? — Affected boundaries, actors, assumptions, methods, models, risks, safeguards, evidence, and decisions.
  4. Why narrow a claim? — Credibility improves when conclusions match evidence and authority.
  5. What is the “way ahead”? — A governed agenda for continued observation, adaptation, participation, and responsible intervention.
