EN.645.753 — Enterprise Systems Engineering

Credits: 3
Prerequisite: EN.645.769 System Test & Evaluation or advisor/instructor approval
Recommended self-study preparation: EN.645.771 System of Systems Engineering, EN.645.781 Systems Thinking and Systems Dynamics, EN.645.784 Decision Science & Analytics, EN.645.782 Foundations of Digital and Mission Engineering, and EN.645.783 Systems Engineering Process Improvement

1. Course purpose

This course prepares a systems engineer to analyze, architect, improve, and transform a large sociotechnical enterprise: a purposeful network of organizations, people, processes, information, technology, funding, policy, infrastructure, and external relationships that collectively produces outcomes no single organization controls.

The enterprise is treated as the engineered system—not merely the setting in which product systems are acquired. The learner must reason across conflicting objectives, distributed authority, incentives, budgets, portfolios, organizational capabilities, value streams, information flows, technology and standards, workforce, public legitimacy, and environmental change. Traditional product-level requirements are replaced by negotiated outcomes, measures, capability gaps, investment choices, governance mechanisms, and transformation hypotheses. Architecture becomes a decision-support description of how strategy, value, capabilities, organizations, processes, information, systems, resources, and policies fit together. Improvement must remain credible even when participating organizations retain autonomy and can resist, reinterpret, or abandon the proposed change.

The final product is a controlled Regional Mobility and Emergency Access Enterprise Transformation Release containing the enterprise charter and boundary, actor and authority model, value/outcome hierarchy, current-state enterprise architecture and process model, capability and portfolio analysis, enterprise dynamics model, external-scenario analysis, target operating-model alternatives, investment and incentive design, technology/standards/workforce roadmaps, transformation experiments, governance and measurement plan, implementation roadmap, live-change response, and oral defense.

2. Source scope and self-study adaptation

The Summer 2026 JHU syllabus describes Enterprise Systems Engineering as a multidisciplinary application of systems engineering and systems thinking to broad sociotechnical enterprises as complex adaptive systems. It emphasizes multiple independent organizations with their own objectives, resources, and authority structures; the absence of top-level cross-cutting authority; conflicting objectives; and creation of a process model showing key enterprise activities, interactions, and external factors. That model is then used to understand enterprise composition and dynamics and to evaluate issues or actions as the basis for engineering trades and recommendations. (1, 2)

The abridged public syllabus does not publish a detailed weekly sequence. This self-study adaptation therefore preserves the stated course outcomes and expands them using current public enterprise-systems-engineering guidance. It adds:

  • explicit enterprise-boundary, value, stakeholder, authority, incentive, and legitimacy analysis;
  • current-state and target enterprise architecture governed by stakeholder concerns and decision questions;
  • capability-based planning across operational, system, and organizational capability;
  • process, value-stream, information-flow, and enterprise-dynamics modeling;
  • portfolio, investment, affordability, standards, technology, workforce, supplier, and data considerations;
  • enterprise evaluation and assessment using balanced measures, uncertainty, and breakpoints rather than isolated project metrics;
  • external scenarios, resilience, opportunity, and strategic-risk analysis;
  • transformation alternatives, target operating models, transition architectures, experiments, adoption, and sustainment;
  • formal review gates, required revision, a live enterprise-change challenge, and oral defense.

The course does not reproduce private JHU lectures, unpublished assignments, or copyrighted textbook chapters. Public standards pages, public SEBoK and government resources, learner-authorized texts, and original exercises are used instead.

4. Prerequisites and readiness diagnostic

Before Week 1, complete a 90-minute diagnostic. The learner should be able to:

  1. Distinguish system, SoS, enterprise, enterprise system, ecosystem, organization, portfolio, program, and complex adaptive system.
  2. Trace a regional outcome through mission threads, constituent contributions, enterprise processes, governance, investment, and evidence.
  3. Construct a stakeholder/authority map showing decision rights, resources, incentives, and veto points.
  4. Interpret a process map, value stream, capability map, causal-loop diagram, portfolio chart, and architecture viewpoint.
  5. Explain why efficiency, effectiveness, value, equity, resilience, and legitimacy can conflict.
  6. Perform a bounded multi-criteria or portfolio comparison with uncertainty and sensitivity.
  7. Reproduce one prior model or notebook from a clean checkout and identify its intended use and limitations.
  8. State how a proposed change could shift cost, risk, workload, or harm to another organization or population.

Diagnostic standard: at least 80%, with no failure on enterprise boundary, authority, value/outcome reasoning, process/dynamics interpretation, or evidence limits. Complete targeted remediation before establishing the enterprise baseline.

5. Course learning outcomes

By the end of the course, the learner will be able to:

  1. Define and justify enterprise purpose, boundary, environment, actors, ownership, authority, resources, exclusions, and evolution conditions. (C1, C10, C11, C12)
  2. Elicit and reconcile enterprise outcomes, stakeholder values, objective conflicts, constraints, legitimacy concerns, and measures. (C2, C9, C10, C11)
  3. Model and assess governance, decision rights, incentives, funding, policy, accountability, and cooperation without assuming a single controlling authority. (C10, C11, C12)
  4. Develop current-state enterprise architecture and process/value-stream models connecting strategy, capabilities, organizations, processes, information, systems, resources, and external factors. (C3, C4, C11)
  5. Analyze enterprise behavior over time using process, network, feedback, delay, workload, and scenario evidence. (C7, C8, C11, C12)
  6. Conduct capability-gap, portfolio, investment, affordability, standards, technology, workforce, supplier, and data analyses. (C3, C8, C9, C10, C11)
  7. Design and compare target operating models and enterprise-architecture alternatives with explicit value, feasibility, uncertainty, and stakeholder effects. (C3, C9, C10, C11)
  8. Develop an enterprise evaluation and assessment system that detects progress, side effects, breakpoints, risk transfer, and opportunity. (C8, C9, C10, C11)
  9. Plan governed transformation experiments, transition architectures, adoption, communications, learning, configuration, and sustainment. (C4, C6, C10, C11)
  10. Recommend and defend an implementable enterprise transformation without overstating control, consensus, prediction, or evidence. (C9, C10, C11, C12)

6. Essential questions

  • What makes the enterprise the appropriate unit of analysis?
  • Whose value is being created, protected, reduced, or transferred?
  • Which actors can authorize, fund, implement, block, reinterpret, or abandon change?
  • Which outcomes require enterprise coordination and which should remain locally optimized?
  • How do incentives, policies, processes, information, technology, workforce, and budgets interact?
  • Which architecture views answer real enterprise decisions rather than produce decorative documentation?
  • Where do delays, feedback, workload, adaptation, and local optimization create enterprise behavior?
  • Which portfolio and investment choices improve capability under real constraints?
  • What external changes could invalidate the current strategy or target operating model?
  • Which transformation steps should be standardized, tailored, piloted, monitored, or reversed?

7. Running case and controlled evidence

Regional Mobility and Emergency Access Enterprise

The learner serves as enterprise systems engineer for the fictional regional coalition introduced in EN.645.771. The technical SoS already has an architecture and evolution recommendation, but the coalition cannot implement it without resolving enterprise-level questions involving value, governance, funding, workforce, procurement, regulation, data, public trust, accessibility, labor, suppliers, and organizational incentives.

The enterprise includes:

  • municipal and regional transit authorities;
  • campuses, hospitals, EMS, paratransit, and emergency-management organizations;
  • traffic, public works, utilities, telecommunications, mapping, payment, and identity providers;
  • technology vendors, contractors, unions, insurers, regulators, elected bodies, community organizations, and advocacy groups;
  • state and federal funding, safety, privacy, accessibility, cybersecurity, and emergency partners.

Controlled evidence includes:

  • organization charters, budgets, service obligations, procurement constraints, and decision rights;
  • synthetic demand, workforce, cost, service-quality, complaint, incident, accessibility, and process-event data;
  • investment proposals and existing portfolio commitments;
  • conflicting actor objectives and negotiation positions;
  • current-state process maps with delays, rework, queues, data gaps, and handoff failures;
  • technology, standards, supplier, workforce, and facility constraints;
  • external scenarios involving funding loss, regulatory change, severe weather, vendor consolidation, workforce shortages, and public opposition.

The case is fictional. No personal, medical, employment, law-enforcement, proprietary, or security-sensitive data are used.

  • process mining and discrete-event simulation tools;

13. Major assignment specifications

A. Current-state enterprise architecture and process model

Produce a queryable or source-controlled architecture connecting enterprise outcomes, actors, authorities, capabilities, organizations, processes, information, systems, resources, policies, funding, measures, and external factors. Include at least three end-to-end value streams and two cross-organizational exception or degraded paths.

B. Enterprise dynamics and evaluation package

Develop a bounded executable model explaining one important behavior-over-time problem such as access inequity, coordination delay, maintenance backlog, workforce overload, investment crowd-out, data-sharing erosion, or emergency-response degradation. Include model purpose, structure, equations or process logic, units, verification, behavior tests, sensitivity, uncertainty, limitations, and decision use.

C. Capability, portfolio, and investment decision

Compare existing and proposed capabilities, programs, systems, services, process changes, standards, technology, workforce, and data investments under resource constraints. Include affordability, dependencies, opportunity cost, uncertainty, stakeholder effects, and at least one low-investment or governance-first alternative.

D. Enterprise transformation release

Define target operating-model alternatives, preferred transition architecture, governance, incentives, investment sequence, roadmaps, pilots, measures, adoption, change control, risk/opportunity, monitoring, and conditions for pausing, adapting, or reversing the transformation.

15. Critical criteria and mastery gates

A learner cannot pass with any of the following unresolved:

  • an enterprise boundary that excludes an actor, policy, funding source, workforce group, or external dependency essential to the claimed outcome;
  • a value or performance recommendation that hides shifted cost, workload, risk, inequity, or loss of legitimacy;
  • governance or implementation that assumes authority, funding, data rights, consent, or cooperation not actually available;
  • a process, architecture, dynamics, portfolio, or scenario result that cannot be reproduced or whose intended use and uncertainty are unstated;
  • a target operating model with no transition architecture, adoption mechanism, measurement plan, owner, or reversal condition;
  • fabricated, proprietary, personal, security-sensitive, or otherwise unauthorized evidence.

Completion standard: at least 80% overall, at least 70% in every major category, Proficient or better on all critical capstone dimensions, clean reproduction, successful live change, and successful oral defense.

The learner must identify affected values, actors, authorities, processes, capabilities, investments, models, measures, risks, and transition steps; revise the recommendation; and state which evidence must be regenerated.

  2. Which stakeholder bears the largest hidden burden in the current state?

  4. Which performance measure is most likely to be gamed, and how will you detect it?
  5. Which feedback loop most strongly resists the preferred transformation?
  6. Which capability gap is real, and which is an artifact of poor coordination or incentives?
  7. What existing investment should be stopped or reduced, and what is the opportunity cost?
  8. Which target operating-model assumption is least credible?
  9. How does the recommendation change under the most disruptive external scenario?
  10. What evidence would cause the coalition to pause or reverse the transformation?
  11. Which conflict cannot be engineered away and therefore requires an explicit governance choice?

Week 1 — Enterprise definition, boundary, purpose, and receiving review

Professional context and essential question

Enterprise work fails early when the analyst inherits an organizational chart or technology portfolio and mistakes it for the enterprise. The boundary must follow the outcome, value system, actors, resources, authority, and environmental interactions relevant to the decision.

Essential question: What must be inside, outside, or explicitly interfaced for the enterprise claim and transformation purpose to be credible?

Weekly learning outcomes

  1. Distinguish enterprise, enterprise system, SoS, ecosystem, organization, portfolio, program, and complex adaptive system using observable evidence.
  2. Define the regional enterprise purpose, outcomes, time horizon, decision scope, boundary, exclusions, and environment.
  3. Identify initial actors, resources, systems, services, policies, funding, and external dependencies.
  4. Create a receiving-review record linking EN.645.771 evidence to enterprise-level questions and gaps.
  5. State intended uses and prohibited uses for the enterprise models to be developed.

Instructor-style lesson notes

  • An enterprise boundary is decision-relative. It should be broad enough to include mechanisms that materially shape the outcome and narrow enough to support analysis.
  • The enterprise is not synonymous with the coalition office. It includes independent organizations and resources whose interactions create the result.
  • Purpose, outcomes, value recipients, authority, time horizon, and transformation decision should be declared before architecture work begins.
  • Exclusions require interface and assumption treatment; they do not disappear because they are outside the model.
  • Receiving reviews distinguish inherited evidence from new enterprise assumptions and identify where SoS evidence is insufficient.

Worked example

The inherited SoS roadmap assumes region-wide sharing of accessible-vehicle availability. The enterprise receiving review reveals that three providers are funded under different statutes, one vendor contract prohibits secondary data use, and a union agreement limits dispatch-role changes. The technical interface is feasible, but the enterprise boundary must include funding authorities, procurement, labor, privacy, and service-accountability actors.

Reproduce the example in a small table, model, or notebook before applying the method to the running case. Record where the example is intentionally simplified.

Guided practice

  1. Write a one-sentence enterprise purpose and a separate one-sentence transformation decision.
  2. Create a context table with actors/resources inside, outside, and at the boundary.
  3. For each excluded actor, record the dependency, assumption, owner, and evidence need.
  4. Classify inherited artifacts as accepted, conditionally accepted, rejected, or not applicable.
  5. Run a boundary challenge: remove one actor or resource and test whether the claimed outcome can still be explained.

Pause after each step and compare the result with the stated decision purpose. Record discrepancies rather than forcing the artifact to match the expected story.

Independent exercises

  • Foundation: Classify 15 case entities as organization, enterprise resource, enterprise system, SoS constituent, portfolio item, or external factor.
  • Application: Produce the enterprise charter and boundary/context view.
  • Analysis: Compare two plausible boundaries and show how each changes measures, authority, data, and recommendations.
  • Synthesis: Define the model intended uses, users, decisions, time horizons, and prohibited claims.
  • Stretch: Build a machine-readable actor/resource registry and query all dependencies that cross the declared boundary.

Knowledge check and answer guidance

Week 2 — Actors, authority, objectives, legitimacy, and governance

Book companion · Week 2 : Read Book 19, Chapter 02 → Practice the hypothetical lab. Keep the original assigned readings, assessed deliverable and grading rubric. Earlier short primer.

Competency alignment

Program competencies: C2, C10, C11, C12
Weekly role: Foundation and integration
Nominal effort: 10–12 hours

Professional context and essential question

Enterprise transformation is constrained less by organizational boxes than by decision rights, resources, incentives, accountability, legitimacy, and the ability of actors to refuse or reshape a change. A technically coherent proposal can be infeasible because no available governance mechanism can authorize or sustain it.

Essential question: Who can decide, fund, implement, block, appeal, or legitimize each enterprise change?

Weekly learning outcomes

  1. Map actors, objectives, resources, authority, accountability, dependencies, and veto or appeal rights.
  2. Distinguish formal authority, contractual authority, operational influence, expertise, funding power, and social legitimacy.
  3. Identify objective conflicts, cooperation dependencies, principal-agent problems, and accountability gaps.
  4. Design alternative governance mechanisms without inventing top-level control.
  5. Conduct the Enterprise Framing and Governance Review.

Prerequisite retrieval and readiness check

  • Reconstruct the prior week's main evidence chain from memory.
  • Identify one inherited assumption that this week's work could invalidate.
  • Complete a short terminology or interpretation check before opening the case files.
  • State what decision the week's deliverable is intended to support.

Readiness threshold: 80%. Correct misunderstandings before continuing; do not carry an unexamined authority, value, or evidence assumption into the baseline.

Required study

  • SEBoK Enterprise Systems Engineering Key Concepts (5)
    Purpose: Focus on enterprise governance, distributed objectives, interactions, and adaptation.
    Guiding questions: Which actor behaviors are rational locally but harmful enterprise-wide?
  • SEBoK Related Business Activities (9)
    Purpose: Review portfolio, resource allocation, budgeting, governance, and cross-enterprise relationships.
    Guiding questions: Where do business decisions constrain technical options? Which relationships rely on contracts versus cooperation?
  • INCOSE ESE Working Group scope (10)
    Purpose: Review current emphasis on capability planning, acquisition, mission architecture, and digital transformation.
    Guiding questions: Which enterprise decisions require model-based support? Which governance questions are not technical architecture questions?

Record concise notes answering every guiding question and identify at least one disagreement, limitation, or applicability concern for the regional case.

Instructor-style lesson notes

  • Authority is multidimensional and may be split among funding, policy, operations, data, safety, labor, procurement, and public accountability.
  • Influence is not authority; a central network position does not grant the right to direct another organization.
  • Governance defines how decisions are proposed, evaluated, authorized, contested, monitored, and revised.
  • Legitimacy and consent affect adoption, data access, workforce behavior, and the durability of enterprise change.
  • Objective conflict should be modeled, not smoothed into a fictitious shared objective.

Worked example

A regional steering board can approve a shared data standard but cannot compel a private vendor to expose historical data or require a transit authority to reassign union-covered dispatch work. A feasible governance design combines procurement clauses, a data trust, operating agreements, labor consultation, public reporting, and an escalation path rather than asserting coalition command authority.

Professional context and essential question

The final review evaluates whether the enterprise recommendation remains coherent when challenged across value, authority, process, capability, investment, dynamics, scenarios, adoption, and evidence. The objective is not to claim control over the enterprise; it is to show a disciplined, legitimate, and adaptive transformation strategy.

Essential question: Can the enterprise transformation recommendation survive clean reproduction, adversarial review, and a material live change without hiding uncertainty or authority limits?

Weekly learning outcomes

  1. Integrate all course artifacts into one controlled enterprise transformation release.
  2. Reproduce key architecture queries, process measures, model results, portfolio decisions, and scenario conclusions from source.
  3. Respond to a live change by updating affected value, authority, process, capability, investment, model, measure, and roadmap elements.
  4. Present an executive and technical recommendation with unresolved conflicts and residual uncertainty.
  5. Defend the work orally and prepare the EN.645.742 complexity handoff.

Instructor-style lesson notes

  • A final release is a controlled evidence chain, not a folder of disconnected weekly submissions.
  • Executive communication should state the decision, value, authority, cost, timing, risks, conflicts, and evidence without overstating certainty.

  • Live-change analysis tests whether architecture and evidence are connected enough to support real revision.
  • The complexity handoff should identify adaptive and emergent mechanisms that enterprise planning could not resolve.

Worked example

During the review, a major transit authority withdraws from pooled funding while remaining willing to share limited data. The learner must revise portfolio affordability, governance, capability ownership, value distribution, emergency mutual aid, measures, scenarios, transition experiments, and roadmap timing. A defensible response may preserve the strategy through a smaller coalition or may reverse the preferred target model.

Reproduce the example in a small table, model, or notebook before applying the method to the running case. Record where the example is intentionally simplified.
